Introduction

The Long Way Around

There is a gap between the businesses in business books and the businesses actual people run. This book lives in that gap.

The first business book I ever bought, I bought to impress a man named Henk.

Henk was my first mentor, though neither of us would have used that word. He was a retired machine-parts wholesaler who drank his coffee black, smelled faintly of cigars even though I never once saw him smoke, and had the unsettling habit of being right about things he had no business being right about. I was nineteen and building websites for local shops. He was the uncle of a client, and he had taken an interest in me the way some retired men take an interest in a stray cat: gruffly, and against his own stated principles.

The book was a famous founder memoir. You have probably read it, or at least nodded at it in an airport. I brought it to Henk's kitchen table because I wanted him to see that I was serious, that I was studying, that I was the kind of nineteen-year-old who reads.

Henk picked it up, read the back cover, flipped to a page in the middle, read for maybe ninety seconds, and put it down.

"This man remembers wrong," he said.

I asked what he meant. Was the guy lying?

"No, no." Henk waved his hand like he was clearing smoke. "Worse. He is telling the truth about the decisions and lying about the weather. Every choice in this book, he makes it in a clean room, with all the information laid out on the table. I promise you he made half of them at two in the morning because a customer was screaming and the bank was closed." He tapped the cover. "Read it if you want. But when you have your own company, do not compare your Tuesday to his book. His Tuesday looked like your Tuesday. He just doesn't remember."

I did read the book. It was good. And Henk was right anyway, which took me about fifteen years and three companies to fully appreciate.

This book you are holding is my attempt to write the one Henk would not have put down: a field guide to the actual phases of building a business, written by someone who has done it badly, then done it less badly, and taken notes both times.

Hindsight Is a Liar with Good Manners

Here is the problem with most business books, and I say this as someone who owns shelves of them and loves many: they are written backwards.

The author starts at the ending. The company sold, or went public, or became a household name. Then they walk back through the years and arrange the events into a line that points at the ending. The pivot becomes "we saw the market shifting." The desperate hire becomes "we knew we needed world-class talent." The product that only exists because a big customer threatened to leave becomes "customer obsession."

None of this is lying, exactly. It is what memory does. Memory is a helpful colleague who tidies your desk while you are at lunch and throws away everything that looks like a mistake. By the time you sit down to write the book, the mess is gone. What remains is a story where each chapter causes the next one, where the founder's beliefs were vindicated, where the strategy was a strategy and not a series of dodges.

And then a real person, in a real business, reads that book on a bad night. Their biggest customer just left. Their cofounder is acting strange. The tax bill arrived and it is not the number they wrote in the spreadsheet. And the book says: we always believed in our vision, and we executed relentlessly.

The reader concludes, reasonably, that something is wrong with them. Everyone else apparently builds companies the way the books describe: forward, on purpose, in order. Only they are doing it like this, sideways, in the rain, making it up.

I want to say this as plainly as I can, because it might be the most useful sentence in this book: everyone is doing it sideways, in the rain, making it up. The books are cleaned-up transcripts. The businesses were live radio.

There is a second, sneakier form of the lie, which is the lie of sequence. Business books love numbered steps. Step one, find your why. Step two, validate your market. Step three, build your MVP. Steps imply that the world holds still while you complete them, and that step four politely waits for step three to finish. In my experience, step nine shows up during step two, uninvited, holding an invoice.

So this book has phases instead of steps, and the difference matters enough that it gets its own section.

Why Phases, Not Steps

A step is something you complete. A phase is something you enter.

When you build a business, you never finish deciding who the company is. You never finish figuring out the money. You never finish hiring, or selling, or leading. What happens instead is that different questions take the foreground at different times. In the beginning, the identity question is loud and the scaling question is a distant rumor. Five years in, it may be the reverse. But the identity question never actually goes away. It just gets quieter, waits for a merger or a scandal or a growth spurt, and then walks back into the room like it never left.

That is what a phase is: a period when one set of questions is loudest. The sixteen chapters of this book are sixteen sets of questions, arranged in the order they usually get loud for the first time. Identity before mission. Mission before vision. The founding team before the hiring machine. The money chapter early, because the money questions arrive early and never leave.

But, and I need you to hear this part, the order is a courtesy, not a law. Businesses do not respect it. You will be neck-deep in the sales phase while your identity quietly rots. You will hit the scaling questions before you have honestly answered the mission ones, and the scaling will go badly in ways that trace directly back to the skipped chapter. The phases loop, overlap, and interrupt each other.

Nobody builds a business in order. You build it in the order the fires start, and then you tell people it was a plan.

Let me make that concrete. At the routing company, we wrote our values in year one, on a Friday afternoon, mostly to fill a slide. Then we spent year four in a mess that looked like a sales problem: deals stalling, discounts creeping, two good account managers gone in a single quarter. We tried sales fixes. New pipeline stages, a new commission plan, a workshop with a consultant who said "buyer persona" forty times before lunch. Nothing moved. The actual problem lived three chapters earlier: we had never honestly decided what kind of company we were, so every salesperson improvised a different answer, and customers could smell the improvisation. The fire was in the sales phase. The fuel was in the identity phase. That pattern, fire in one chapter and fuel in another, repeats so often in this book that you could call it the plot.

So why arrange the book as a sequence at all? Because a map drawn in the order of a typical trip is still useful even when your trip is not typical. When your business is on fire, it helps enormously to know which kind of fire it is, and that other people have had this exact fire, and that there is a chapter about it. The sequence gives you a spine. Life will provide the interruptions.

One more thing about phases. Because they are entered rather than completed, you can be in several at once, and you usually are. A founder at year three is typically living in four or five chapters of this book simultaneously. That is the job, not a sign of chaos. The trick is knowing which one deserves your best hours this month, and the map at the end of this introduction is meant to help you make that call with your eyes open.

Who I Am, and Why You Should Stay Skeptical

You should know whose voice this is, and you should know it early, so you can calibrate your skepticism.

My name is Wouter Simons. I started my first business at nineteen, building websites for local shops: a butcher, two hair salons, a shop that sold garden furniture and, inexplicably, aquariums. I charged too little, delivered too slowly, and learned more about business from the butcher's complaints than from anything I studied. He paid me 350 euros and made me earn every cent twice.

At twenty-two, my friend Sam and I started a recipe app. We were going to change how people cook. We had a logo before we had a user, a growth model before we had a recipe, and eighteen months later we had neither users nor money nor, for a few tense weeks, a friendship. The app died. The friendship recovered. You will hear more about the recipe app in this book than about anything that went well, because failure keeps better records.

Then came the company I spent most of my adult life on: a logistics software business I will call the routing company. We helped delivery fleets plan their routes. It was unglamorous, difficult, and wonderful. I grew it slowly, hired people better than me, broke most of the things described in the later chapters of this book, repaired some of them, and eventually sold it. The selling has its own chapter, because nobody warned me about the part that comes after the wire transfer, and I intend to warn you.

These days I run a company called Lionsville. That is the first and last time it will be named in this book, and I will not tell you what it does, because the moment I do, this becomes a book about my company instead of a book about yours.

Now, the skepticism. I am one founder, from one small corner of Europe, who built one kind of company well and two kinds badly. Where my experience runs thin, I have leaned on people who filled the gaps: friends who built agencies and bakeries and venture-backed software companies, who let me interrogate them over long dinners and longer walks. But every author of a book like this is generalizing from a sample size you could count on your hands. When something in these pages contradicts what you are seeing with your own eyes in your own business, trust your eyes. The book is a map. You are standing on the terrain.

Who This Book Is For

This book is for people building something with other people, where the something has to pay for itself.

That is a deliberately wide door. Through it fit the two engineers in a rented office, the woman turning her consulting sideline into a firm, the brother-in-law with one bakery and an itch, the founder at forty employees wondering why everything feels harder than it did at twelve. It fits people who want to raise venture capital and people who would rather eat glass. It fits first-timers and, I hope, second-timers, who will read some chapters nodding and some chapters wincing.

It is not really for people seeking a side hustle they can automate from a beach, and it is not for executives at large companies looking for innovation frameworks, although both are welcome to eavesdrop. The assumptions throughout are small-to-medium, founder-led, and paid for by customers rather than by slideware. If your company has a legal department, some chapters will feel quaint. If your company is you and a laptop and a nervous feeling, you are exactly who I pictured.

A word about the range of businesses inside. The stories here come from software companies, a design agency, a chain of bakeries, a machine-parts wholesaler, and a few one-off characters you will meet along the way. That mix is deliberate. The startup world has convinced itself that its problems are special, and the bakery world has convinced itself that books like this are not about bakeries. Both are wrong. A cofounder dispute works the same whether the asset is code or ovens. Cash runs out at the same speed in both, which is to say: faster than the spreadsheet promised. I have tried to write every chapter so that the software founder and the ninth-bakery owner can both see themselves in it, because from where I sit, they are in the same business. The business of keeping a promise to customers without running out of money or friends.

You do not need an MBA to read it. I do not have one. I looked into it once, decided the tuition would fund about fourteen months of the routing company's server bills, and chose the servers. I have tried to write so that the only prerequisite is that you care whether the thing survives.

How to Read This Book

There are two correct ways to read this book, and I designed it for both.

The first way is front to back. The chapters are arranged in the order the phases usually get loud, so reading in sequence gives you the whole arc: from the identity questions nobody thinks they need to answer, through money and people and market, into the machinery of a growing company, and out the far end to selling it and, possibly, starting again. If you have not started yet, or you have just started, read it this way. Consider it a long walk with someone who has been up the road and is willing to describe the potholes with unusual honesty.

The second way is to raid it. If your business exists and something is bleeding, do not read in order. Go directly to the chapter you are bleeding in. Cofounder trouble, chapter five. Cash sweating, chapter six. Nobody buying, chapter nine. Twenty-five employees and a creeping sense that nobody tells you anything anymore, chapter eleven. Each chapter stands on its own legs, restates what it needs from elsewhere, and ends with a summary blunt enough to be useful at midnight. Raiding is what field guides are for.

Whichever way you read, I make one request: do the exercises. Every chapter has one or two blocks marked "Try this," and they are small on purpose, things you can do today with a pen and an honest hour. A business book you only read is entertainment. I have written plenty of entertainment into this one, but the exercises are where it earns its price.

Try this

Before you read another page, take a pen and write down, in one sentence each: the phase your business is loudest in right now, and the phase you have been avoiding. Not the one you are worst at, the one you avoid. Fold the paper, put it inside the back cover, and check it when you finish the book. Mine, for the record, said "sales" and "sales." It was lying about the first one.

Field note

If you are currently in a genuine crisis, the emergency chapters, the ones raided most often in my experience, are six (money), five (founding team), and twelve (yourself). Go. The rest of us will be here when you get back.

A Note About the Stories

This book is full of stories, and you deserve to know what kind of true they are.

They are true the way bar stories are true. Every story in this book happened, in the sense that its bones are real: real founders, real invoices, real 2 a.m. phone calls. But I have changed names, merged people, moved events between companies and years, and rounded the dialogue to what was meant rather than what was said. Henk is real and also three people. Marta, whom you will meet in the next chapter, ran a design agency the way I describe, except she is also two other people and one of her disasters belonged to someone else entirely. The details are rearranged to protect the guilty, and the guilty, in most of these stories, is me.

I did this for two reasons. The first is decency: my friends told me things over their second drink that they would not want traceable over anyone's first. The second is usefulness. A composite story can be more honest than a factual one, because it keeps the pattern and discards the coincidence. What you need from a story about a cofounder blowup is the shape of the thing, so you recognize it coming down your own hallway.

So: the stories are made up, and they are not. This is the only chapter that will carry this disclaimer. From here on, I will just tell them straight, and you can trust them the way you trust a friend across a small table: not on the details, but on the point.

The Map: A Walking Tour of Sixteen Phases

Here is the whole road at a glance. Sixteen phases, four bends, one loop at the end that a surprising number of us take. We will walk it briefly now, a sentence or two per stop, so you know where everything lives. Think of this as reading the trail markers, not taking the hike.

Part I: Who You Are Before What You Sell

1. Designing an identity. Before the product, before the pitch, there is the question of who this company is: the values you actually hold, as opposed to the ones that look good on a poster. Most founders skip this and pay for it later with interest.

2. Deciding on mission. The job your company does in the world, written sharply enough that you can use it to say no. A good mission is less a banner and more a bouncer.

3. Articulating vision. A postcard from the future: where all this is headed if it works. We will untangle vision from mission from strategy exactly once, in one table, and never make you sit through it again.

4. Building the product or service strategy. What you sell, to whom, and why they care, starting from their problem rather than your cleverness. Includes the painful art of not building things.

Part II: People and Money

5. Getting the founding team together. Cofounders, equity, and the prenup conversation almost nobody has while they still like each other. The recipe app makes its first full appearance here, as a cautionary exhibit.

6. Managing money. Cash versus profit, runway math, and how to read a P&L without crying. If you only ever raid one chapter, most people raid this one.

7. Evolving the business plan. The plan as a living document with assumptions and kill criteria, not a 40-page PDF that dies the day you print it.

Part III: Going to Market

8. Building the brand. Brand as reputation, not logo: what people say about you when you leave the room, and how to influence it without becoming insufferable.

9. Sales approach. Founder-led selling, discovery that actually discovers, and pipelines sized for your business rather than for a software demo. Also: how to lose a deal without losing your dignity.

10. Hiring people. First hires, interviews that predict instead of charm, onboarding, and the hardest paragraph in the book: letting someone go kindly.

Part IV: Building the Machine

11. Building the skeleton and nervous system. Structure and information flow as you grow: roles, meetings, documentation, and metrics that carry signal instead of decoration. For everyone who has ever said "nobody tells me anything anymore."

12. Evolving your own management and leadership skills. The uncomfortable phase where the biggest bottleneck in the company shaves your face every morning. Delegation, feedback, and getting help before you need it.

13. The entrepreneurial operating system. Operating rhythms and frameworks, EOS and its cousins: when they save a company, and when they curdle into theater with better paperwork.

14. Scaling up. When to scale, what breaks first (spoiler: people, process, cash, and you, roughly in that order), and how to grow without sanding off the soul.

Part V: Endings and Beginnings

15. Selling the business. Why founders sell, what valuation feels like from inside, surviving diligence, and the emotional cliff on the far side of the wire transfer. I have been off that cliff. I brought photographs.

16. Doing it again. What transfers to a second company and what dangerously does not, the ego trap of the sequel, and why, despite everything in the preceding fifteen chapters, you will probably do it again anyway.

One Last Thing Before We Start

Years after that kitchen-table conversation, I told Henk I might write a book someday. He was older by then, slower on the stairs, still faintly cigar-scented, still never smoking. He thought about it for a while, the way he thought about everything, as if the question had to clear customs first.

"Fine," he said. "But write the weather."

That is what I have tried to do. The chapters ahead contain frameworks, tables, exercises, and opinions I will defend at any dinner table in Europe. But underneath all of it, the promise of this book is smaller and, I think, worth more: I will tell you what each phase feels like from inside, while it is raining, before anyone knows the ending. What you do out there on the road is yours.

Take the long way. It is the only way there is.
