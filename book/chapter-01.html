Chapter 1

Designing an Identity

Your company already has values. They are whatever you did last Tuesday, when doing the right thing cost you something.

The poster had been hanging in Marta's agency for two years before anyone actually read it.

It hung by the coffee machine, in a tasteful grey frame, and it said, in a tasteful grey font: Passion. Excellence. Innovation. Integrity. Forty designers walked past it every day. Designers, mind you. People who will stop a print run over a comma that sits half a millimetre too low. Nobody looked at the poster, because nobody looks at posters like that. They are the visual equivalent of the safety card in an airplane seat pocket.

Then a junior designer, three days into the job and still in the phase where new people ask dangerous questions, raised her hand at the Friday all-hands. Two client teams had just spent twenty minutes politely fighting about whether to tell a client his beloved new packaging design was failing every test they ran. One team wanted to tell him straight. The other wanted to soften it, stage it, maybe let the test results do the talking in a month or two. The junior pointed at the poster and asked, with the innocence of someone who has not yet learned what not to ask, which of the four words applied here. Integrity said tell him today. Passion, she supposed, said protect the relationship. Which one wins?

Silence. Then laughter, the good kind. Then Marta, who tells this story against herself and insists I keep this part in, said: "Honestly, I don't know. I don't even remember writing those."

She had not written them. That was the thing. After the meeting she asked her office manager where the poster came from, and the office manager checked, and the answer came back that it had been left behind by the previous tenant. An insurance intermediary. The words on the wall of one of the most respected design agencies in the region were the corporate values of an insurance broker who had moved out two years earlier, and in two years, in a building full of professionals trained to notice everything, not one person had noticed. Including the founder.

It gets worse, and this is the part that matters. The same month, Marta lost a pitch she should have won. The work was strong, the chemistry was good, the budget fit. She called the client afterwards, as you should always do with a lost pitch, and asked what tipped it. The client was kind and specific, which is the most useful combination and the rarest. "Your work was the best," he said. "But we could describe the other agency in one sentence, and after three meetings we still couldn't describe you. We knew what you make. We never worked out who you are."

And the same month, a senior designer who had been there since year two handed in her notice. Her resignation letter contained a sentence Marta can still recite: "I no longer know what we are trying to be, other than busy."

Marta called me on a Tuesday evening, which is when the honest calls happen. "Fifteen years," she said. "Forty people. Good clients, good margins, awards on the shelf. And I have just discovered that my company has no personality. It has revenue and no character. I have built a very profitable ghost."

This chapter is about the question underneath that phone call. Not what your company does, not where it is going, not how it looks. Who it is. It is the first chapter of this book on purpose, because it is the phase almost everyone skips, and the interest on the skipped chapter compounds quietly for years before the bill arrives, usually addressed to a different department.

The Question Nobody Asks on Purpose

Nobody starts a company to answer the question "who are we?" You start a company because you saw a gap, or you were fed up with your boss, or a customer offered you money and you said yes before thinking. The doing comes first. The doing should come first. If you spend your first six months on identity workshops instead of customers, you do not have an identity problem, you have a customer problem wearing a philosophy costume.

But here is what I have watched happen, in my own companies and in every founder story I have collected over long dinners: while you are busy doing, the identity forms anyway. Every company has one. It forms the way an accent forms, not because anyone decided, but because a thousand small sounds got repeated. You answered an angry customer at 23:00 or you didn't. You quietly redid the sloppy work or you shipped it. You paid the supplier on time even in the bad month or you sat on the invoice. Each one felt like an isolated decision. Together, they were the company introducing itself.

So the real question of this chapter is not "should your company have an identity?" It already does. The question is whether you designed it or inherited it from your own unexamined habits, the way Marta inherited a poster from an insurance broker.

Let me define the word before we go further, because "identity" gets used to sell a lot of expensive workshops. When I say identity, I mean the character of the company. The values it actually holds, the temperament it shows under pressure, the things it would never do even for money, and the things it always does even when they cost money. Identity is who the company is when nobody senior is in the room and a weird situation walks in the door.

It is worth being precise about what identity is not, because three of its neighbours have their own chapters and I intend to stay out of their gardens.

Identity is not your mission. The mission is the job your company does in the world, and writing one sharp enough to say no with is the whole business of chapter 2. Identity is not your vision, the postcard from the future, which gets chapter 3. And identity is emphatically not your brand, your logo, your name, your website, all the ways the identity gets expressed and perceived, which live in chapter 8. Mixing these up is not a small semantic slip. It is how founders end up hiring a branding agency to fix a character problem, which works about as well as buying a new suit to fix a marriage.

The relationship between them is the oldest metaphor in the book, and I am going to use it anyway because it is old for a reason. Identity is the trunk. Mission, vision and brand are branches. They grow out of the identity, they are fed by it, and when the trunk is hollow, you can tell from the branches long before the tree falls over. Marta's lost pitch looked like a brand problem, a chapter 8 problem. Her senior designer's resignation looked like a retention problem. Both were branches showing symptoms of a trunk that had never been deliberately grown. It fed on whatever was lying around, which turned out to be an insurance broker's poster and Marta's improvised taste.

One more thing about why this is chapter one and not chapter nine. You can survive for a surprisingly long time without a deliberate identity. Years. Marta did fifteen. What you cannot do is scale without one, and I mean scale in the humblest sense: any growth past the point where you personally are in every room. Up to about ten people, the founder is the identity, walking around, correcting course in real time. Past that, the company starts making decisions in rooms you are not in, and it makes them by asking "what do we do here?" If the only honest answer is "whatever the founder would do," you have built a company that runs on guessing. And customers, as Marta's lost pitch showed, can smell guessing through three layers of good design.

Four Words in a Grey Frame

Let us talk about the poster, because your company either has one or will be offered one soon.

Here is how poster values get made, and I describe this with the confidence of a man who has personally committed the crime. The company reaches a size, usually somewhere between fifteen and forty people, where the founder feels a vague drafty sensation, a sense that the place needs "more culture." A day is booked. Sometimes a facilitator, at day rates that would make a plumber blush. Flipcharts appear. Everyone shouts words they associate with the company at its best, and the words go on the flipchart, and by mid-afternoon the flipchart holds forty perfectly nice words, and then the wordsmithing begins. Wordsmithing is the process by which forty specific words become four generic ones. "The way Tom stayed until midnight rebuilding the order that got corrupted" becomes Dedication. "The time we refunded the client because we knew the work wasn't our best even though he was happy" becomes Excellence. All the blood drains out, the corpses are laminated, and the laminate goes up by the coffee machine.

At the routing company we did exactly this, in year one, on a Friday afternoon, mostly to fill a slide for a customer presentation. I mentioned it in the introduction and I will now make the confession complete: our four words were Reliability. Innovation. Teamwork. Customer focus. I did not have to look them up just now. I had to look them up in year three, when a job candidate asked me what they meant in practice, and I heard myself improvising, and the candidate heard it too. He took the other offer.

The problem with poster values is not that they are false. Nobody at Marta's agency was against integrity. The problem is that they are unfalsifiable. They fail four cheap tests, and I want to give you the tests before I give you the table, because the tests are the part you can use tomorrow morning.

The competitor test. Could your nearest competitor hang the exact same words without anyone blinking? Then the words are not an identity, they are a category description. "Fresh ingredients" on a bakery wall says nothing. Every bakery claims fresh. "We bake twice a day and give the evening surplus away at closing, which is why we sometimes run out and will not apologize for it" is an identity. A competitor would have to actually argue with it.

The dissent test. Could a reasonable, competent person disagree with the value? Nobody has ever stood up in a meeting and argued for dishonesty, sloppiness or hating the customer. If no sane person would oppose it, adopting it cost you nothing and tells nobody anything. Real values are contestable. "We show clients unfinished work" is a value precisely because plenty of excellent agencies believe you should never do that.

The invoice test. When did this value last cost you something? Money, a deal, a convenience, a comfortable silence. Values are only visible under load, like bridges. If a stated value has never been load-tested, you do not actually know whether you hold it, any more than you know a rope holds your weight because it looks thick.

The recitation test. Ask three people, separately, what the company's values are. Not the words on the wall, the actual operating rules. If you get three different answers, the wall is decoration. If you get roughly the same answer and it does not match the wall, congratulations, you have real values, they are just not the ones you published. That last case is more common than you would think, and it is good news wearing a disguise.

Here is the whole contrast in one table. I suggest reading the right-hand column twice, because the right-hand column is the rest of this chapter in miniature.

Notice the row about violations, because it is the one founders flinch at. The moment a top performer breaks a stated value and nothing happens, every person in the company silently updates the real rulebook. Not the poster, the real one, the one they keep in their heads. The update reads: that value is decorative, and results are the actual value. You will never see the update happen. You will only see its consequences, eighteen months later, in some room you are not in, and you will wonder where the culture went. It went where you sent it, the day you looked away.

Values Are Found, Not Written

Now the constructive part, and it begins with a demotion. You do not get to invent your values. You are not that free. By the time you care enough about this question to read a chapter on it, your company has a history, and the history has already voted.

The work is archaeology, not authorship. You dig through the decisions the company has actually made, especially the expensive and uncomfortable ones, and you look for the pattern that keeps showing up when nobody was trying to be consistent. The pattern is the value. Your only authorial choices are whether to name it, whether to keep it, and whether to say it out loud.

Let me show you the dig at the routing company, because we did it properly exactly once, in year five, after the poster embarrassment with the job candidate. I sat down with my two most senior people and a bottle of decent wine, and we listed every genuinely hard decision we could remember. Not the strategic ones from board slides. The ones that had a bad taste to them, where doing one right thing meant doing another right thing less. We got to about twenty before the wine ran out, and then we looked for repeats.

Three patterns fell out of that list so clearly it was almost embarrassing, like finding your own face in a police lineup.

First: we told customers bad news early, always, even when there was a decent chance the problem would fix itself and they would never have known. Twice this had cost us real money, once a renewal. We kept doing it anyway, without ever having agreed to. When we asked ourselves why, the answer came from the product itself: our customers ran delivery fleets, and the one thing a fleet planner cannot absorb is a surprise. Somewhere along the way we had absorbed our customers' deepest need into our own reflexes. The value got named that night: no surprises, in either direction. We warned about problems early and we also refused to spring last-minute miracles that operations couldn't absorb.

Second: we had turned down, repeatedly, features and projects that were more exciting than the boring reliability work, even when customers waved money. We had told a huge prospect no about a flashy real-time map because it would have destabilized the planning engine for everyone else. At the time each refusal felt like a one-off judgment call. In a row, they were obviously a conviction: boring is a feature. Our customers did not want to be excited by their routing software. They wanted to forget it existed.

Third, and this one we almost missed because it seemed too small: we answered the phone. An actual human, during business hours, usually within three rings, at a time when our whole industry was migrating to ticket portals. It had never been a policy. It was just that I had answered the phone in year one, and the first employees copied me, and the next ones copied them. It cost us real money in support staffing, and we had defended that cost in three separate budget rounds without ever once articulating why. That is what a real value looks like in the wild: a cost you keep re-approving without needing to discuss it.

Notice what is on that list and what is not. Nothing about passion. Nothing about excellence. Our actual values turned out to be a temperament, mildly obsessive, allergic to drama, more proud of uptime than of cleverness. When we finally wrote them down, a new employee read them and said, "So the company is basically a Dutch dad." I have never received a better compliment, and I have received exactly one review in a trade magazine that used the word "visionary," which my team printed out and hung in the toilet.

So how do you run the dig on your own company? Five trenches, in rough order of usefulness.

Where to dig

One: the hard-decision list. As above. Ten to twenty decisions that hurt, from the whole life of the company. What tipped each one? Ignore what you said at the time. Look at what you did.

Two: praise and punishment. Whom have you celebrated, promoted, quietly given the good projects? Whom have you eased out, and for what? People metabolize this data instantly and permanently. Whatever the celebrated people have in common, that is a value, whether or not it is a value you would admit to at a dinner party.

Three: what you tolerate. The uncomfortable trench. The rude genius you keep because the work is brilliant, the chronic lateness nobody mentions, the client who mistreats your staff and keeps getting his calls returned because of the size of his invoices. Tolerated behaviour is endorsed behaviour, at one remove. Every company's real values include a few it would rather not own, and you cannot fix the inventory until you have counted it honestly.

Four: money under pressure. Where does the money go in a bad quarter, when every euro has three jobs? What survived your last cost cut without discussion? At the routing company, phone support survived every cut. That was the value announcing itself in the only language that cannot lie, which is the accounting.

Five: the lunch curriculum. What do veterans actually warn new people about in the first week? Not the onboarding deck, the quiet advice over lunch. "Never let him find out from someone else." "Don't show her anything half-finished." "Here we always call the customer back the same day, even with nothing to report." The lunch curriculum is the real values document, maintained collectively, updated instantly, never once out of date. If you can get an honest transcript of it, you can skip half this chapter.

Try this

The decision autopsy, one honest hour, one pen. Write down the last five genuinely hard decisions your company made, the ones with a bad taste. For each, one line: what did we actually do, and what did it cost? Ignore what you said at the time and what you wrote in the deck. Now look for the repeat, the thing you keep choosing when choosing hurts. Write the pattern as one plain sentence beginning with "Apparently we believe..." That sentence is a value. You may be pleased to meet it. You may not. Both results are worth the hour, and the second is worth more.

A Real Value Has a Price Tag

Everything in this chapter balances on one idea, so let me set it down carefully: a value is only real if it costs you something, at least sometimes, and preferably at the worst possible moment.

This is not me being romantic about suffering. It is a definition. A value is a rule for choosing between two things you want. If a stated value never collides with anything else you want, it never actually chooses anything, and a rule that never chooses is not a rule, it is a mood. "We value quality" costs nothing until the deadline arrives and the work is not right, and you must pick. The picking is the value. Everything before the picking was marketing, aimed mostly at yourself.

Watch how this plays out across very different businesses, because the mechanism is beautifully consistent even when the stakes smell different.

The baker who says quality is everything proves it at 17:30, when the last batch came out pale and dense and technically sellable. Real value: the batch goes to the chickens and the case sits half empty and tomorrow a customer grumbles. Decoration: the batch goes in the case, because it is one batch, because margins are tight, because nobody will notice. Somebody notices. Somebody always notices, and the somebody who notices most is the baker's own staff, who now know exactly how much everything-is-everything costs before it bends: one batch.

The agency that says honesty proves it on the phone call where the campaign is underperforming and the client has not looked at the numbers yet. The software company that says customer first proves it when fixing the old customers' pain and building the shiny thing for new prospects need the same two engineers in the same quarter. The consultancy that says sustainability proves it when the biggest RFP of the year arrives from a company whose business model the consultancy privately finds indefensible, and the RFP is worth two salaries.

In every case the structure is identical. The value sits there, cost-free and gleaming, for months. Then one afternoon it presents an invoice, and what you pay, or refuse to pay, is the actual identity of your company being written, one line item at a time. Nobody will see most of these moments. Identity is mostly built off-camera.

A value that has never cost you anything is not a value. It is decor, waiting for its first invoice.

Two honest caveats before we move on, because I can hear a reasonable reader objecting.

First: no, you cannot pay every invoice every time. A company that upholds every value at any cost in all conditions is a company run by a saint, briefly, until the money runs out. There will be a Tuesday where the rent collides with the principle and the rent wins. That does not make you a fraud. It makes you a business. The difference between a company with real values and a company with decorative ones is not a perfect payment record. It is that the real-values company notices the miss, names it out loud, and treats it as debt to be repaid rather than as the new normal. The decorative company doesn't even feel the bump.

Second: the invoice test cuts both ways, and this is the freeing part. If you dig through your history and find that a value you love saying has never once cost you anything, you have two honest options. Retire it from the poster, with thanks for its service to the pitch deck. Or start deliberately paying for it, this quarter, with a specific decision, and see whether you can afford the habit. Both are respectable. The only disreputable option is the one everyone defaults to, which is leaving it on the wall, unfunded, where it quietly teaches your whole company that the wall is where words go to mean nothing.

Watch out

Values can be used as weapons, and the weapon is usually pointed down the org chart. When "not a culture fit" becomes the unexamined verdict for anyone who disagrees with the founder, or when a value like "positivity" is invoked to shut down the person naming a real problem, the values have stopped being an identity and started being a uniform. A decent stress test: your values should constrain the most powerful person in the company more often than they constrain the most junior one. If in the last year your values were only ever cited against people below you, and never once stopped you personally from doing something you wanted to do, be suspicious of what you have built. How values do and do not belong in hiring decisions is chapter 10's business, and it will pick this thread up properly.

The Founder Is Not the Company, but Try Telling the Founder

Here is a sentence that took me most of a decade to be able to say without flinching: in the beginning, you are the company's identity, and that is fine, and if it stays that way, it will quietly strangle everything you built.

The early overlap is total and it is supposed to be. A three-person company does not need written values. It has you, in the room, all day. Every decision passes through your hands or within earshot of them. The company's temperament is your temperament with a VAT number. This is efficient. It is also, and founders hate this framing, a form of technical debt. You are borrowing coherence from your own presence, and like all borrowing it is cheap right up until the moment it is not.

The moment arrives on a schedule you can almost set your watch by. Somewhere between ten and twenty people, the company starts having experiences you are not part of. A customer call you never hear about. A quality judgment made at 18:00 when you are at your kid's football match. A new employee learning "how we do things" from someone who has been there eight months and is improvising. The company begins to develop an identity in your absence, assembled from guesses about you. And guesses drift. Not because anyone is careless, but because a photocopy of a photocopy is how identity transmits when it was never written down.

Three specific rots set in when founder identity and company identity are never separated, and I have personally cultivated all three, so I describe them from the inside.

The bus problem, without the bus

Everyone knows the morbid version: what if the founder gets hit by a bus? But the real version is smaller and happens daily. You are not hit by a bus, you are simply in another meeting. The company needs to know what it believes at 14:00 on a Wednesday in a room you are not in. If its only method is "guess what Wouter would do," then your company's decision quality degrades with distance from your desk, like wifi. You can watch this on the faces of your best people: the slight hesitation, the "let me check with him first" that adds two days to everything. That hesitation, multiplied across every decision in a growing company, is one of the most expensive things you will never see on a P&L.

The quirk calcification problem

When the founder is the identity, the company cannot tell your convictions from your habits, so it preserves both with equal reverence. At the routing company, we had no meetings before ten. People joined the company believing this reflected some deep philosophy about maker time and deep work. The truth is that I drove my kids to school. That was it. That was the whole philosophy. But nobody asked, and I never said, and by year six "no meetings before ten" had hardened into folklore, complete with justifications I had never made, and when a new operations lead tried to schedule an 09:00 standup for a team that genuinely wanted one, she was told, gravely, that it was against our culture. My school run had become doctrine. It is funny until you count how many of your company's "values" are exactly this: fossilized founder logistics, defended by people who were never told the difference.

Do the sorting while it is cheap. Take the things everyone believes the company believes, and sort them into three boxes: convictions, the things you would defend with money and discomfort, which belong in the identity. Preferences, the things you like but would trade, which belong in a policy, clearly labelled as changeable. And habits, the things that are just true because they have always been true, which belong wherever the evidence says, once someone finally checks. The sorting takes an afternoon. Not doing it takes years, because unsorted, everything defaults into the conviction box, and then every operational change feels like sacrilege to somebody.

The mirror problem

The third rot is the deepest. When founder and company share one identity, the company cannot grow past the founder's self-image, because any change to the company reads, to the founder, as a criticism of the founder. Watch a founder who thinks of himself as a scrappy underdog try to lead a company that has become the market leader. Watch a founder who identifies as "the creative one" respond when the company needs to become excellent at boring delivery. The company strains toward what it needs to become, and the founder, without ever saying so, hauls it back toward what he needs to keep being. Half the strategy fights inside founder-led companies are not strategy fights at all. They are one person's identity crisis, wearing the company as a costume.

Story

Two weeks after the poster discovery, Marta ran an experiment she now recommends with the fervor of the converted. She gathered her twelve most senior people and asked one question: "Describe this agency to me. One rule: you may not use my name."

Silence. Not thinking silence. The other kind.

Forty seconds in, a designer who had been there nine years said, "We're the agency that does whatever Marta would do." A pause, and then, because the room was already ruined, he finished the thought: "And lately, we guess."

Marta says the word "guess" hit like a diagnosis. All the symptoms suddenly had one cause. The pitch feedback, the client who couldn't say who they were after three meetings: the client had been listening to five people guessing. The senior designer's resignation letter, "I no longer know what we are trying to be": she had lost confidence in her guesses. The endless small escalations to Marta's desk: people upgrading their guesses to certainties the only way available. Marta had assumed her constant involvement was the identity working. It was the identity failing, loudly, in the form of a queue outside her office.

"The worst part," she told me later, "is that I liked the queue. The queue felt like being needed. I had built a company that could not describe itself without me, and some honest part of me had built it that way on purpose."

The founder's circle never fully leaves, and it shouldn't: your convictions are the roots of the tree we drew earlier. The work is the middle picture, the deliberate, slightly melancholy work of letting the company become a character that overlaps with you without being you. Melancholy because it is a small goodbye, and I will not pretend otherwise. It is also the only version with a future, including, if you ever reach chapter 15, a future in which the company can be worth something to anyone who is not you.

Writing It Down Without Embalming It

At some point the archaeology is done, the founder sorting is done, and you have to write the thing down, because an identity that lives only in your head has the bus problem, and an identity that lives only in the lunch curriculum drifts one retelling at a time. This is where most companies snatch defeat from the jaws of victory, because writing values well is a specific small craft, and the defaults of corporate writing are all wrong for it. So, five rules, learned by breaking each one.

Rule one: three to five, never more. Seven values is zero values. Nobody can hold seven rules in their head at the moment of decision, and the moment of decision is the only moment that counts. If you emerged from the archaeology with nine patterns, good, you are a rich civilization, but rank them by which ones you have actually paid the most for, and publish the podium. The rest live on as stories.

Rule two: full sentences, never single nouns. Single abstract nouns are how values die. "Integrity" is a tombstone. The sentence is what forces the meaning to show up. "The client hears bad news from us first, the same day we learn it" cannot be misunderstood, cannot be reinterpreted by a clever person under deadline pressure, and can be checked. Was it the same day? No? Then we broke the value, and we know it, and knowing it is the entire point of writing things down.

Rule three: the trade must be visible. Every real value chooses between two goods, so write the choice into the sentence, or at least keep it standing next to the sentence. "We choose thorough over fast" tells an employee what to do at 17:00 on a deadline day. "Quality" tells them nothing except that the poster committee met. A useful format when drafting, even if you polish it away later: we choose X over Y, even when Y is offered by someone waving money.

Rule four: the opposite must be a strategy. This is the dissent test from earlier, worn as a drafting tool. Before a value goes on the list, ask: could a reasonable, successful company choose the opposite on purpose? "We show clients unfinished work" passes, because plenty of superb agencies would rather be seen dead than show a half-baked concept, and they are not wrong, they are different. "We do great work" fails, because no company on earth strategically pursues mediocre work. If the opposite is not a strategy, the value is not a choice, and if it is not a choice it will never help anyone make one.

Rule five: every value gets a story, permanently attached. Not an explanation, a story. The time it cost us. The deal we walked away from, the batch we threw out, the call we made before we had to. Stories are how humans actually store values, which is why the lunch curriculum outperforms every laminated poster ever printed. When you publish your values, publish them with their scar tissue. A value without a scar is a rumour.

A note on that table: do not copy from it. I mean that literally, and not as false modesty. The sentences in the middle column are right for the companies they came from and would be wrong, even harmful, for a company with a different history. A trauma surgeon should not adopt "we would rather miss a deadline." A budget airline should not promise an idea nobody asked for. The table demonstrates a grammar, not a wardrobe. Your sentences must come out of your own dig, or they are just a newer, wordier poster, and you will have spent all this effort upgrading from an insurance broker's values to mine, which is barely an upgrade at all.

Try this

Take your current values, whatever exists, poster or folklore, and rewrite each one tonight as a trade: "We choose ___ over ___, even when it costs us ___." Maximum five. Then run a one-week tally, and this part is the actual exercise: keep the list where you work, and every time a real decision touches one of the values, mark it. Honoured, a tick. Violated, a cross. Never touched, nothing. After a week, read the marks. Values with ticks and crosses are alive, keep them. Values with nothing at all are either decorative or your week was quiet, so run a second week before you sentence them. Crosses are not shameful, by the way. Crosses mean the value is real enough to have enemies.

How Marta Rebuilt It

Back to Marta, because I left her on the phone calling her company a profitable ghost, and because what she did next is the best worked example I know of everything this chapter has claimed so far.

Her first instinct, she freely admits, was to hire a branding agency. There is a comedy in that, a design agency hiring a design agency, but the deeper mistake would have been the category error we flagged at the start: she was reaching for a chapter 8 solution to a chapter 1 problem. New words, new look, same hollow trunk. To her lasting credit, she caught herself, mostly because of a question her financial controller asked in passing: "Before we pay someone to tell people who we are, do we know?"

So instead of an offsite with a facilitator, she ran the dig. Two Friday afternoons, four weeks apart, the whole company invited, attendance voluntary, wine after. The instruction for the first Friday was borrowed from the archaeology playbook and sharpened: no aspirations allowed. Only history. Tell a story about a decision this agency made that was expensive and that you were proud of, or a decision that was profitable and that made you ashamed. Both kinds went on the wall. Thirty-one stories by the end of the afternoon, some of them awkward, two of them about Marta personally, told while she was in the room, which she says was the price of admission and the proof the exercise was real.

Between the two Fridays, three people clustered the stories, looking for the repeats. The repeats were unmistakable, the way they always are once someone finally looks.

The proud-expensive stories kept featuring unfinished work shown early: half-built concepts put in front of clients at the sketch stage, at the risk of looking unpolished, because course-correcting at the sketch is cheap and course-correcting at the launch is a catastrophe. The agency had been quietly brilliant at this for a decade. Half the client relationships that had lasted longest had a story in their first year about a messy sketch on a table and a client saying, relieved, "oh good, I can still change things."

The ashamed-profitable stories clustered just as hard, and most of them were about free pitching: the industry ritual where agencies do weeks of real creative work, unpaid, to win an account. Every free pitch had felt like a forced move at the time. Stacked on a wall, they read as a pattern of self-disrespect that everyone had privately hated and publicly accepted. Two of the agency's worst client relationships, the ones that ground people down, had both entered through a free pitch, which nobody had noticed until the stories were physically adjacent on a wall.

And a third pattern that nobody expected: an enormous number of the proud stories featured a junior. Juniors given real work early, juniors' odd ideas making it into final campaigns, seniors spending unbilled hours teaching. It had never been a policy. It was just the kind of place Marta had built without noticing, the way you furnish a house one impulse at a time and then one day a guest says "this is so you," and you look around, surprised.

The second Friday, they wrote. Three sentences, argued over word by word, each with a story from the wall stapled to it.

We show work before it is ready.

We do not design for free.

Every project teaches somebody.

Look at those against the five rules. Three, not seven. Full sentences, no abstract nouns. The trade is visible in each: polish sacrificed to honesty, revenue opportunities sacrificed to self-respect, short-term efficiency sacrificed to the pipeline of people. Every one has an opposite that is a legitimate strategy, and Marta can name specific admirable competitors who live by each opposite. And every one came with scar tissue attached, thirty-one stories deep.

Story

The expensive part came three weeks later, which is how you know the values were real: they presented an invoice almost immediately.

A national supermarket chain, the kind of account that reshapes an agency's year, invited Marta's agency to pitch. Free, naturally, against four competitors. The old agency would have said yes before the email finished loading. Now there was a sentence on the wall, three weeks old, with the paint barely dry.

Marta gathered the senior team. Her financial controller, the same one whose question had started all this, went visibly pale doing the arithmetic and, to his eternal honour, said, "It's a terrible year to mean it. But if we don't mean it now, we never meant it." They wrote back declining the free pitch and offering, instead, a paid two-week discovery sprint at a fair rate, deliverables theirs to keep either way. The supermarket said no. The account went to a competitor. The number the agency walked away from had six digits in it, and Marta printed the decline email and pinned it to the story wall, where it remains.

Here is the ending, though. The senior designer, the one whose resignation letter said "I no longer know what we are trying to be, other than busy," was working out her notice period through all of this. The Monday after the supermarket decision went around the studio, she asked for ten minutes, sat down across from Marta, and withdrew her resignation. "You finally answered my letter," she said. And eight months later, a different retailer came to them directly, no pitch, paid discovery from day one. Their brief cited, as the reason for calling, "we heard you're the agency that says no to free work." The identity had walked out the door and started selling. Which is all a brand is, and chapter 8 will have much more to say about how to help that along on purpose.

I want to be careful not to varnish this, because varnish is exactly what the introduction promised to avoid. The transition cost Marta real money for real quarters. Two prospects walked when the no-free-pitching line held. One employee genuinely disliked the new explicitness, called it "corporate," and left, and he was good, and it stung. Deliberate identity is not a trick that makes hard things free. It is a decision about which invoices you are willing to keep paying, made once, in daylight, instead of fifty times in the dark. The money she stopped burning on unpaid pitch weeks covered the walked prospects within the year, but she did not know that in advance, and neither will you.

Drift, and How to Notice It Before the Symptoms Do

An identity, once written, immediately begins to erode. I wish I could offer you a version of this chapter where the work stays done. There isn't one, and the erosion mechanism is worth understanding because it is so gentle that no alarm you own will catch it.

Identity is never lost in a single decision. It goes one reasonable exception at a time. The biggest client asks for something that bends a value, and he is the biggest client, so you bend, just this once, and you are probably right to. The busy season arrives and the phone-in-three-rings rule slips to five, then to a callback, and every step had a good reason. Exceptions are like rounding errors: individually defensible, collectively a different number. The company that ends up with a hollow trunk almost never did anything wrong that you could point to in the moment. It just said "just this once" at a compounding interest rate.

You cannot prevent exceptions, and you should not want to. Rigidity is its own disease. What you can do is keep the books. Three habits, cheap enough that you will actually keep them.

Count the exceptions out loud. When the company bends a value, say so, in public, at the time: "We are making an exception to the three-rings rule for the season, and here is why, and it ends in March." Named exceptions stay exceptions. Unnamed exceptions become precedents, and precedents become the new identity, unratified. The sentence costs you twenty seconds and a small amount of pride, which is the entire maintenance budget of a company identity, and almost nobody pays it.

Run the annual invoice audit. Once a year, take your three to five sentences and ask for each one: when did this last cost us something? Get specific, with dates. A value with no invoice in twelve months has stopped operating, whatever the wall says, and you must either revive it with a deliberate decision or retire it honestly. This audit takes one hour, which is a suspicious bargain for what it does, and it pairs naturally with whatever annual rhythm you build later. Chapter 13 will give the rhythm a proper home.

Listen for the leading indicators. Drift announces itself in language before it shows up in numbers. Veterans saying "we used to be the kind of place that..." Explanations of the company getting longer, because coherent things are quick to describe and incoherent things take paragraphs. New employees asking "but what do we actually believe about X?" and getting different answers on different floors. And the classic: the fire that looks like it belongs to a later chapter. When sales conversations start feeling weirdly effortful, when good people leave citing vagueness, when the brand refresh somehow doesn't take, remember the pattern from the introduction. The fire is over there, but check whether the fuel is here, in the trunk, three chapters back from where the smoke is.

Field note

Strategy should change often, identity rarely. A useful rule of thumb: strategies are revised in years, identities in decades. If you find yourself rewriting your values every year or two, you did not have values, you had strategies in formal wear, and you can relax, rename them, and manage them as strategies, which is cheaper. Genuine identity change does happen, after a near-death experience, a generational handover, a true pivot, but it should feel like an amputation performed on purpose, announced honestly, and mourned a little. If it feels easy, either it wasn't real before or it isn't real now.

What All This Buys You

I have asked you to spend an entire chapter, and several honest hours with a pen, on something that produces no product, signs no customer and shows up on no balance sheet. It is fair to ask what you get. Let me give you the returns as concretely as I can, and then let an old man in a kitchen have the last word, as he tends to.

You get speed. This is the one nobody expects, because identity work smells slow. But watch what happens in a company where the values are real, written and scarred: decisions that used to escalate, stop escalating. The project lead at 18:00 does not need to find you, because "we show work before it is ready" already answers her question. Multiply that by every fork in every road in a growing company and you have bought back something scarce and unphotographable: everyone's best judgment, operating at full speed, in parallel, without you. Delegation without a shared identity is dice-rolling with extra steps. Delegation with one is just called working, and there is a whole chapter, 12, about the founder side of learning to let it happen.

You get coherence customers can feel. Marta's lost pitch was lost to a company that could be described in one sentence. Coherence reads as trustworthiness, because it is: a company that is the same in every room will probably also be the same after the contract is signed. Incoherence reads as risk, even when every individual interaction was pleasant. Customers rarely articulate this. They just pick the other agency and say something vague about fit.

You get cheaper coordination. Every organization pays a tax to keep its people rowing in compatible directions: meetings, approvals, check-ins, escalations. A shared identity is the cheapest coordination technology ever invented. It is a set of decisions made once, in advance, for everyone. The alternative is making the same value judgment over and over, per person, per incident, forever, which is roughly how Marta acquired a queue outside her office and mistook it for love.

You get ballast for the bad day. Every company gets its crisis: the lost anchor client, the public mistake, the year the market turns. In the crisis, everything becomes negotiable at once, and companies without a settled identity negotiate away things they needed, at panic prices. The settled identity is the list of what is not for sale today. It shortens the panic, because the hardest question in a crisis is not "what should we do" but "who are we allowed to be while we do it," and you answered that one back when you could think.

And you get a magnet, working quietly. The right people start finding you, the way the second retailer found Marta, and the wrong people start filtering themselves out, which is worth even more. How to use that magnet properly when you hire is chapter 10's territory, and the words you wrap around it for the outside world belong to chapter 8. The trunk just has to be worth growing branches from.

Story

In year five of the routing company, after the wine-and-archaeology evening, I drove to Henk's house with our three freshly written value sentences in my bag. I was proud of them. I wanted him to be proud of them, which at thirty-one was apparently still the engine I ran on.

He would not take the paper. He physically waved it away, the smoke-clearing gesture, and said, "Don't tell me your values. Tell me your last five decisions that hurt, and I will tell you your values."

So I told him. The delayed release that cost us the deal. The flashy real-time map we refused to build. The support hire we made in a quarter when we could not afford it. The customer we let out of a contract early because the fit had gone bad. The bug we disclosed before the customer would ever have found it.

Henk drank his coffee and was quiet for a while, the way he was quiet, like the question had to clear customs. Then he said: "You keep paying money to make sure nobody is ever surprised. That is the whole company. The rest is fonts."

It was on our paper, near enough, and I said so, with some triumph. Henk shrugged, entirely unimpressed, and delivered the sentence I have carried around ever since and am now handing to you, because it is the shortest correct summary of this chapter that exists: "Good. Then you wrote down the truth. Most men write down the wish."

That is the standard, in the end. Not whether your values are inspiring, and not whether they would look good in a grey frame by the coffee machine. Whether they are the truth. Write down the truth about who your company is, with its scars attached and its invoices on file, and everything that follows in this book has something to grow from. The next branch on the tree is the mission, the job your company does in the world and the noes it lets you say, and that is where we go now.

The Short Version

  • Your company already has an identity. The only question is whether you designed it or inherited it from your own unexamined habits.
  • Identity is the trunk. Mission (ch 2), vision (ch 3) and brand (ch 8) are branches. When a branch looks sick, check the trunk first.
  • If a competitor could hang your values poster without blinking, it is not an identity, it is a category description.
  • A value that has never cost you money, comfort or a customer is decor. The invoice is what makes it a value.
  • Values are found, not written. Dig through your hard decisions, your promotions, your tolerated behaviour and your budget under pressure. The repeats are the values.
  • Write three to five of them as full sentences with the trade-off visible, each with a scar story attached. If the opposite could not be a reasonable strategy, it is not a value.
  • Separate founder conviction from founder habit before the company fossilizes your school run into doctrine.
  • The founder is the parent of the company's identity, not its twin. Build a character that answers questions in rooms you are not in.
  • Identity erodes one reasonable exception at a time. Name exceptions out loud, and audit once a year: when did each value last cost us something?
  • The payoff is speed, coherence, cheap coordination and ballast in a crisis. Written down honestly, identity is the truth. Written down hopefully, it is just a wish in a grey frame.
