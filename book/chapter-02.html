Chapter 2

Deciding on Mission

Your mission is not the sentence on the wall. It is the sentence that decides what your company does on an ordinary Tuesday, and, more importantly, what it refuses to do.

The vinyl letters cost me 340 euros, plus another 60 for the man who applied them to the wall without bubbles.

This was early in the routing company, the year we moved out of the room above the bicycle repair shop and into a real office, the kind with a meeting room and a reception area and a large white wall that seemed to demand something. We were six people and one plastic plant. I had just come back from a weekend workshop about company culture with a notebook full of diagrams and a head full of fonts, and I had decided that what the wall demanded was our mission statement. I wrote it myself, over two evenings, feeling very much like a CEO. It said: Empowering the future of logistics through innovative technology.

Henk visited that Friday. He was in the neighborhood the way retired men are always in the neighborhood, meaning he had driven forty minutes on purpose. I gave him the tour, which took four minutes, and made him a coffee, which he inspected with suspicion because we had bought one of those machines with buttons.

He ended up in front of the wall. He read it the way he read everything, slowly, as if the words had to clear customs first. The office went slightly quiet around him, the way offices do when someone's mentor is visibly thinking.

"Who is this for?" he asked.

"Customers," I said. "And the team. And candidates, when we interview. It sets the tone."

"Hm." He took a sip and grimaced, at the coffee or the sentence, hard to say. "Which customer woke up this morning wanting the future of logistics empowered?"

I started to explain that it was aspirational, that it captured our ambition, that the workshop had a framework. He let me finish, which was worse than interrupting.

"Let me ask it a different way," he said. "What do people pay you to make go away?"

I opened my mouth to answer and found, to my genuine surprise, that the fancy sentence on the wall was no help at all. So I answered like a human instead. I told him about the dispatcher at our biggest client, a man named Ruud who arrived at ten to six every morning to a wall of orders, forty vans, three hundred stops, two drivers calling in sick, and a supermarket that fined you per minute if you missed the delivery window. I told him about the puzzle Ruud solved by hand for two hours every morning, and how the puzzle followed him home, and how his wife had opinions about that. I told him that when our software worked, Ruud drank his first coffee sitting down.

"The morning," I said finally. "We make the worst part of the morning go away."

Henk nodded, slowly, and pointed his coffee at the wall. "Then why does it not say that?"

I said something about how a mission needed to inspire. He repeated the word inspire the way other people say the word mold.

"That sentence up there is a banner," he said. "Banners hang above the door and look nice on photographs. You do not need a banner. You need a bouncer. A bouncer stands in the door and decides who gets in. Next year a big customer will ask you to build something that is not your job, and the money will be good, and that banner will smile and wave it through. A bouncer checks it against the list and says no." He drained the coffee, grimaced again, and handed me the cup. "Buy a bouncer."

The letters stayed on the wall for six more years, partly because removing vinyl costs almost as much as applying it, and partly because I came to value them as a monument. Not to our ambition. To the difference between the sentence you frame and the sentence you use. The one we actually used, we wrote about a month after Henk's visit, on paper, without a workshop, and it was about Ruud's morning.

This chapter is about that second kind of sentence.

The Job, Not the Poster

Here is the definition this whole chapter rests on, so let me put it up front where you can argue with it: your mission is the job your company does in the world.

Every word in that definition is carrying weight. Job means it is work, performed daily, for someone specific, who notices whether it got done. Your company means it belongs to the business, not to your personal sense of purpose, though the two should be on speaking terms. And in the world means outside your own walls: the job is measured in your customer's life, not in your roadmap. A mission written from the inside describes what you make. A mission written from the outside describes what changes for the person paying you. Only the second kind is any use.

In the last chapter we worked out who your company is, its values and its character, and if you skipped that chapter because values sounded soft, I will only say that a mission without an identity underneath it is a job application with no one attached. Identity is who you are. Mission is the work that someone with that identity shows up to do. A person of good character still needs an occupation, and so does a company.

Field note

If, while reading this chapter, you feel an itch to describe the destination, the world as it looks in ten years when the job is done at scale, you are itching for vision, and chapter 3 is entirely about it, including the one table in this book that untangles mission, vision and strategy for good. Park the postcard. This chapter is about the job.

The most useful mental move I know for finding the job is one you have already seen Henk perform: stop asking what you sell and start asking what you get hired for. Customers do not buy products, and they certainly do not buy missions. They hire companies to do jobs, and they fire them, quietly and without an exit interview, when the job stops getting done.

The butcher who gave me my first real invoice at nineteen did not hire me to build a website. He could not have cared less about websites, and he told me so, twice. He hired me because the supermarket at the edge of town had opened a fancy meat counter and for the first time in thirty years people who wanted good meat did not automatically think of him. He paid me 350 euros to stop becoming invisible. The website was just the tool I happened to use. If I had done the job with a newspaper ad and a better sign, he would have been exactly as satisfied. I have carried that lesson through three companies: the customer defines the job, and the job is almost never the thing on your price list.

I relearned it the expensive way at twenty-two, with the recipe app. Sam and I had a mission from day one, and I can still recite it, which tells you exactly where our effort went: to make the world fall in love with cooking again. It was a lovely sentence. It had one defect, which is that nobody had hired us to do it. The world, on closer inspection, had not fallen out of love with cooking. The world was tired on Wednesdays and wanted dinner to involve fewer decisions, and there were companies quietly doing that job, and we were not one of them, because we were busy with the love affair. We had written a mission about our own feelings and mistaken it for a job. Eighteen months later, when the app died, the mission was the only part of the company still in perfect condition: unused, gleaming, like a fire extinguisher recovered from a flooded house.

Why write the job down at all? Founders sometimes push back here, and it is a fair challenge. You know what your company does. You are there every day. The sentence lives in your head, so why laminate it?

Three reasons, in ascending order of expense.

First, speed. A written mission turns a certain category of debate into a lookup. Should we attend this trade fair, build this feature, chase this segment? Companies without a usable mission hold that debate fresh every time, from first principles, with whoever happens to be in the room. Companies with one check the sentence and move on. Over a year, that difference is measured in weeks.

Second, consistency. If the mission lives only in your head, then every employee is improvising their own version of it, in sales calls, in support tickets, in what they choose to work on when nobody assigns them anything. Six people improvising six missions is survivable. Twenty is not, and by the time you notice, the inconsistency has soaked into your customer base like water into a wall.

Third, and most expensive: without a written job, your company's actual mission becomes whatever the loudest customer wants this quarter. This happens gradually and feels like responsiveness. It is how a route-planning company wakes up owning a half-built warehouse module, a story I will tell you properly later in this chapter, because I lived it and the scar still itches when it rains.

A Banner Is for Admiring, a Bouncer Is for Deciding

Henk's distinction deserves a proper unpacking, because it is the sharpest test I know for whether a mission is real.

A banner mission exists to be seen. It goes on the wall, the website, the second slide of the pitch deck. It is written to impress people who are not in the company: investors, candidates, visiting mentors with strong opinions about coffee. Banner missions are optimized for how they sound, which is why they all sound the same. Empowering. Transforming. Unlocking. Reimagining. You can swap banner missions between companies in entirely different industries and no one will notice for weeks. I know this because a friend of mine once did it as a prank between two firms he advised, and the record was five weeks, and the person who finally noticed was an intern.

A bouncer mission exists to be used. It stands in the doorway of the company and checks every opportunity, every feature request, every partnership and pet project against the list. Its job is not to look good. Its job is to keep the room the kind of room it is supposed to be. And like an actual bouncer, it is not angry and it is not a wall. It lets in everyone the room is for, warmly. It is simply specific about what the room is for, and it works the door every single night, not just when the photographer is there.

How do you tell which kind you have? Two questions, and I encourage you to answer them out loud, alone, where no one can watch your face.

One: when did your mission last change a decision? Not accompany a decision, not appear in the slide where the decision was announced. Change it. If you cannot recall a moment when someone in your company said "we are not doing this, because of the mission" about something they otherwise wanted to do, then the sentence has never actually worked a shift.

Two: has your mission ever cost you money? This is the harder question and the better one. A working mission refuses things, and some of the things it refuses come with revenue attached. If every opportunity you have ever liked has also, conveniently, fit the mission, then the mission is not a filter. It is a mirror.

A mission that has never cost you money is not a mission. It is a poem you keep in the lobby.

I want to be fair to banners for a moment, because there is a seductive argument for them, and you will hear it from smart people. The argument goes: the mission is a marketing asset, it should be aspirational, it should make hearts beat faster, and the operational stuff can live in strategy documents. I half agree. Your company can absolutely have stirring language for the world; that is brand territory, and chapter 8 will treat it with respect. The mistake is letting the stirring sentence be the only sentence, because then, at the moment of decision, when the whale client is on the phone and the number has six zeroes, your company will reach for its mission and find only a poem. Poems are lovely. Poems do not say no to whales.

To make the door-work concrete, here is what a bouncer mission handles in an ordinary month at a small company. A customer asks for a feature that would serve only them. A partner proposes a co-branded something. A conference invites you to speak to an audience that contains none of your customers. A developer wants to rebuild the invoicing in this year's framework. A competitor's announcement makes everyone nervous for a week. None of these is a crisis, and that is precisely the point: each one is a small tax on your attention, and the mission's daily job is to pay those taxes fast, so that the slow, expensive thinking stays reserved for the few decisions that genuinely have no precedent.

The economics here are worth spelling out, because "say no" sounds like discipline for its own sake and it is not. A small company's scarcest resource is not money. It is the attention of its best people, and every yes rents that attention out. When your mission pre-decides the nos, you are not being purist. You are protecting the only compounding asset you have: consecutive months of your best people working on the same job. Companies that say yes to everything do not die of any single yes. They die of fragmentation, spread across a dozen half-jobs, master of none, and the autopsy usually blames the market.

So that is the standard for everything that follows. Not "does it inspire" but "can it work the door." The rest of this chapter is a practical manual for writing a sentence that can: where to dig for it, what altitude to fly it at, how to word it, how to smell-test it, and how to actually use it on the day the whale calls.

What Do People Pay You to Make Go Away?

Of all the questions I have been asked across three companies, by mentors, investors, consultants and one memorably blunt tax inspector, Henk's is the one I have reused the most. It looks simple. It is doing four jobs at once.

First, it forces the customer's point of view. You cannot answer it from inside your own building. "What do we sell" is answered at your desk; "what do people pay us to make go away" can only be answered by standing, at least in your imagination, in the customer's kitchen or warehouse or inbox at the moment the pain shows up.

Second, it forces pain over pleasure. This sounds cynical and is merely accurate. People occasionally pay for delight, but they pay reliably, repeatedly, and at better margins to be rid of things: risk, waste, dread, chaos, embarrassment, Monday mornings of a particular shape. If your business genuinely sells joy, and some do, the question still works with one word changed: what do people pay you to make sure never happens? The village bakery is selling pleasure right up until the Sunday you arrive at your in-laws with supermarket cake, and then you understand what it was really selling all along.

Third, it produces verbs. Answers to Henk's question arrive in words like stop, fix, prevent, take over, get back. These are load-bearing words, and later in this chapter, when we write the actual sentence, you will be glad to have a pile of them instead of a pile of adjectives.

Fourth, and most usefully, it is falsifiable. "We empower logistics" cannot be checked. "We make the dispatcher's two-hour morning puzzle go away" can be checked by walking into the dispatch office at seven and looking at Ruud. Either he is drinking his coffee sitting down or he is not.

Henk did not invent the question in a seminar. He mined it out of forty years behind a counter, and the story of how deserves telling properly.

Story

Somewhere in the early years of his machine-parts business, Henk told me, a farmer came in fifteen minutes before closing, filthy to the elbows, and asked for a bearing. Henk had the bearing. He also, being Henk, asked what it was for, and the farmer explained that his harvester had seized that afternoon, mid-field, with rain forecast for the day after tomorrow and roughly a year's income standing in the field.

"He did not want a bearing," Henk said. "No man alive wants a bearing. He wanted the harvest not to stop. The bearing was just the shape of the problem that day."

Most shopkeepers would have filed that under nice customer anecdotes. Henk rebuilt his company around it. He started opening at half past six, because machines break in the morning and every hour of downtime had a price he could calculate better than his customers could. He studied which twenty parts most often stopped an entire production line or harvest, and kept those twenty in triple stock, even when his accountant complained about the working capital. He hired a delivery driver with instructions that would horrify a logistics consultant: the van goes wherever the most expensive silence is. And behind the counter, where customers could not see it, he hung a hand-lettered sign for his own staff: Nobody wants parts.

He never wrote a mission statement in his life; he considered the phrase itself vaguely American. But every person who ever worked for him could recite the job in one breath: we get machines running again before the downtime costs real money. And here is the part I want you to notice, because it pays for this whole chapter: that sentence let him raise his prices. Nobody comparison-shops for a bearing while a year's harvest stands in the rain. His competitors sold parts and competed on price. Henk sold the end of expensive silences and competed on nothing at all.

Your business has an equivalent sentence, whether or not you have found it yet, and the raw material for it is lying around in three places.

The first is your last five sales. Not the pipeline, the closed ones. For each, reconstruct the moment the customer decided, and ask what was happening in their life or business at that moment. People buy at moments, and the moment is almost always a pain spiking, not a feature launching. The second is your complaints. When a customer is angry, they drop the polite vocabulary and tell you what job they actually hired you for, in the words they really use. Support tickets are mission research that your customers write for free. The third is the repair conversation: ask a long-standing customer, over coffee and away from any renewal date, "what would you have to go back to doing yourself if we disappeared tomorrow?" The answer to that question is your mission wearing work clothes.

To make this concrete across the range of businesses I promised you in the introduction, here is what the question does to five companies that think they sell products.

Try this

Today, with a pen: write down your last five sales, one line each. For each one, write what was going wrong in the customer's world in the week they said yes, in their words if you can remember them, not yours. No word that belongs on a poster is allowed; if you catch yourself writing "efficiency," stop and write what the inefficiency felt like at street level instead. Then finish this sentence once, covering as many of the five as you honestly can: "People pay us to make ___ go away for ___." That sentence is not your final mission. It is the ore. The rest of the chapter is the smelting.

Finding the Right Altitude

Once founders accept that the mission is a job, they tend to make one of two altitude errors, and I have personally made both, in opposite decades.

Missions fail high and they fail low. Too high, and you are in the clouds with every other company on earth, unable to refuse anything because everything arguably makes the world better. Too low, and you have written this quarter's to-do list in a serif font, and the mission expires the day you ship. The useful altitude is the narrow band in between, and it is worth drawing.

Too High: The Cloud Layer

Cloud missions are the ones about bettering, empowering and transforming. They have two structural defects. The first is that they are indistinguishable: run the swap test, replace your company's name with your nearest competitor's, and if the sentence still works, it was never about you. The second defect is fatal for our purposes: a cloud mission cannot refuse anything. Should the routing company build warehouse software? Well, would it empower the future of logistics? Obviously. Should it open a café? Logistics people drink coffee; empowerment all around. A sentence that permits everything decides nothing, and a bouncer who lets everyone in is just a doorman with opinions.

Cloud missions usually come from a decent impulse gone soft: the founder wants the work to matter, and so reaches for the biggest available words. But mattering does not live in the size of the words. Henk's business mattered enormously, to farmers standing in wet fields, and his sentence had no word in it longer than two syllables.

Too Low: The Task Layer

The opposite failure is writing your mission at the level of what you currently make. "Build the best route-planning software" was, for about a year, the routing company's real working sentence, after the vinyl letters and before Henk finished his renovation of my thinking. It felt refreshingly concrete after the empowerment era. It was also wrong, in a way that took me a while to see: it welded the mission to a product, and products are tools, and tools change. If a customer had found a way to fix Ruud's morning with three magnets and a whiteboard, our software would have deserved to die, and a mission that said "software" would have insisted we keep building it anyway.

The tell for a too-low mission is that it contains your deliverable: the word software, or bread, or campaigns, or audits. What you sell, to whom, at what price, is the work of your product strategy, and chapter 4 will spend its full length there. The mission sits one level up, where the deliverable could change without the sentence flinching.

The Working Altitude

The band in the middle has a simple boundary condition on each side, and together they make a rule you can apply in ten seconds. A mission should outlive your current product, but not your customer's problem.

Outlive the product: if you would have to rewrite the sentence after a major pivot in how you do the job, it is flying too low. Not outlive the problem: if the sentence would still make sense in a world where nobody had the problem anymore, it is flying too high, because it was never attached to a real job in the first place. "We give dispatchers their morning back" dies on the glorious day dispatching becomes effortless for everyone, and that is correct. Missions are allowed to be completable. In fact the faint possibility of completion is a good sign, the way a real destination distinguishes a journey from a stroll.

Writing the Sentence

Now the actual writing, and let me start with where not to do it: an offsite. I know, because I have run that offsite. Twelve people, a facilitator, a hotel with a name like a perfume, flipcharts sprouting adjectives by mid-morning. The problem is structural, not personal. Wordsmithing in a group optimizes for the sentence nobody objects to, and the sentence nobody objects to is the sentence that refuses nothing, which is the one thing your mission exists to do. Ten smart people will produce a sentence with ten clauses, each clause a small monument to one person's fear of being left out.

Write the draft alone, or with your cofounders, from the raw material of the last exercise: the go-away sentence, the customers' own words, the pile of concrete verbs. Then, and this matters, test it with the group instead of writing it with the group. Testing is something groups are excellent at. Give your people the draft and ask them to attack it with real decisions from the last quarter, and you will get honest, useful violence. Ask them to help write it and you will get a committee poem.

Warning

If your draft mission needs the word "and" more than once, you do not have a mission with subclauses. You have two or three missions in a raincoat, and each was added to keep someone from feeling excluded. Cut until it hurts one person in the room. If it hurts nobody, it includes everything, and a mission that includes everything excludes nothing, which is the whole failure this chapter exists to prevent.

As for the sentence itself, it has three load-bearing parts: a verb, a customer, and a boundary. Everything else is decoration.

The Verb Does the Work

Mission verbs come in two families. There are working verbs, the kind you could mime: take away, get back, keep open, stop, fix, put, deliver, answer. And there are lobby verbs, the kind that only exist on walls: empower, enable, unlock, elevate, transform, inspire, leverage. The test between them is brutal and takes one second: could a customer catch you failing to do it? A café owner can catch the bakery failing to fill her counter by six. Nobody has ever caught anyone failing to empower.

Name the Customer

The second load-bearing part is a person. Not a market, a person. "The logistics industry" cannot hire you, get angry with you, or drink coffee sitting down. Ruud can. When your mission names someone specific enough to picture, three good things happen. New employees know whose face to imagine when they make small decisions, which is most of what culture is on a working day. Salespeople know instantly whether the prospect on the phone is our kind of customer, which shortens both the good calls and the bad ones. And you gain a quiet second filter: opportunities that serve someone other than your named person have to argue their way in, instead of drifting in unexamined.

Founders resist naming the customer because it feels like shrinking the market, and I want to push on that directly, because the fear is real and the logic is backwards. A named customer does not shrink your market. It shrinks your message until it actually penetrates one group deeply, and depth is what small companies have instead of reach. The routing company's mission named dispatchers. We happily sold to fleet owners, finance directors and one memorable flower auction, but every one of those deals began with a dispatcher whose morning we had fixed telling someone about it. The named person is not your only customer. They are your beachhead and your compass, and when in doubt, the company points at them.

The Boundary Clause

The third part is optional in the wording and mandatory in the meaning: the sentence should imply an edge, a place where your company visibly stops. Sometimes the edge is baked into the naming. "We get machines running again" quietly excludes selling new machines, and Henk turned away exactly that trade for decades, to the bafflement of manufacturers who courted him. Sometimes you need the edge said aloud, and the cleanest way I know is a second sentence beginning with the words "we do not." We give dispatchers their morning back. We do not run warehouses. Two sentences, eleven words, and the second one, as you are about to see, turned out to be worth more than the first.

One last craft note before the smell test. Keep the whole thing sayable in one breath, by one person, standing in a doorway. That is not a poetry rule, it is an operations rule: a mission that cannot be recited from memory cannot be used in a meeting, and a mission that cannot be used in a meeting reverts to being a banner, no matter how good the verbs are. When we finally had ours, I tested it by asking people at random, in the corridor, without warning. The day the newest support engineer answered without blinking, and then added "which is why I told that prospect this morning we were the wrong choice for them," I knew the sentence had cleared probation. The wrong-choice prospect, incidentally, sent us two right-choice referrals within the year. Bouncers, it turns out, get a reputation.

The Mission Smell Test

Before a mission is allowed to work the door, it should pass inspection itself. Over the years, first for my own companies and then for friends who kept asking, I have collected the recurring smells: the little tells that a mission is decorative, committee-built, or quietly dead. None of them is proof on its own. Three or more and you should open a window.

Run this test twice a year, calendar it, and run it on the real mission, meaning the sentence people actually say, which after a few years is not always the one in the onboarding deck.

Two of these smells deserve a paragraph of their own, because they are the ones founders argue with.

The launch-campaign smell first. If your mission requires a rollout, posters, a town hall and a mug, that is the sentence telling you it is news to your own company, and a mission that is news is a wish. The strongest missions I have encountered were, at the moment of writing, almost boring to the people inside: a crisp description of what the best people already did on the best days. The writing did not change the company. It made the existing job defensible, teachable and, crucially, refusable-against. If there is a gap between the company you are and the company in the sentence, close it from the company side. Growth toward a true sentence works. A sentence stretched over an untrue company just flaps.

Second, the apology smell, which sounds soft and is the most reliable of the lot. Say your mission, out loud, to a real customer, in a real conversation, and watch yourself. If some part of you winces, hedges, or hurries past it, your body has already run a smell test your slides cannot fool. I have watched founders discover in one sentence, mid-meeting, that they did not believe their own mission, and it is always the same tell: they say it in a slightly different voice, the way people read terms and conditions. The fix is never elocution. The fix is a truer sentence.

Saying No Like You Mean It

Everything so far has been the training. This section is the fight. A mission earns its keep on perhaps five or six occasions a year, the moments when something attractive and wrong arrives at the door, and I want to walk you through the anatomy of those moments, because they never feel the way the textbook implies. The textbook implies a clean choice, mission on one side, temptation on the other. In real life the temptation arrives dressed as good news, carried by someone you like, and the mission's voice is the quietest one in the room.

Let me show you, at my own expense, both versions: the yes I should not have said, and the no that saved us.

Story

The yes came first, in the years before the mission had teeth. A mid-size client asked us to build a customs paperwork module. It was adjacent, sort of: their vans crossed a border, paperwork slowed the vans, we were the van people. The deal was worth about 80,000 euros and I said yes inside a week, feeling flexible and customer-obsessed. That module took eighteen months to build instead of five, because customs rules turned out to be a career and not a feature, and it consumed, at various points, my two best engineers, one of whom told me years later it was the closest he came to quitting. It sold to exactly one other customer. When we finally wrote it off, I calculated the real cost, salaries, delay to the core product, and one competitor catching up in a market we had led. The 80,000 euro yes cost roughly half a million, and that arithmetic, every yes has a denominator, is the cheapest expensive lesson in this book.

The no came three years later, and it was much bigger. Our largest client, a retail chain, asked us to build their warehouse management. Not asked, really; courted. Dinner, a tour of the distribution center, a projected contract that would have grown our revenue by about forty percent in one signature. I wanted it. My sales lead wanted it so much he had already sketched the org chart. We spent two weeks in a low-grade civil war, and the spreadsheet, I must be honest, was firmly on the side of yes.

What tipped it was the second sentence. We give dispatchers their morning back. We do not run warehouses. Someone, not me, our quietest engineer, actually, read it out loud in the third meeting and then said, "So which sentence are we deleting?" The room went silent in a way I still remember, because that was the actual decision, said plainly: not "shall we take the money" but "shall we delete the sentence." Framed that way, nobody wanted to delete the sentence.

We said no. We said it with a referral to a warehouse software firm we trusted, we built a clean integration to them at our own cost, and I flew to the client personally so the no arrived with a face on it. The client stayed eleven more years. The warehouse firm sent us every routing lead they ever met. And the forty percent revenue we declined arrived anyway, about two years later than the spreadsheet promised, from dispatchers, doing the job the sentence said we did.

I want to pull four practical lessons out of that story, because they generalize.

First, the no needs a form of words that makes the real trade visible. "Which sentence are we deleting" did in six words what two weeks of spreadsheets could not: it converted an abstract mission question into a concrete act everyone could feel the weight of. Steal it. When an opportunity conflicts with the mission, ask the room which sentence gets deleted, and watch the conversation change temperature.

Second, a good no has aftercare. A bare no burns the relationship and teaches your team that the mission is expensive. A no with a referral, an integration, an introduction or a face on it strengthens the relationship, because it proves you know exactly what you are and are not, which is precisely the quality the client hired you for in the first place. In eleven subsequent years, that client never once tested our boundary again. Bouncers, as I said, get a reputation.

Expect the no to have a domestic cost as well as a commercial one, and budget for it. My sales lead barely spoke to me for a month after the warehouse decision, and he had a point: the company had just declined the easiest forty percent he would ever find, and nobody had adjusted his targets to match the principle. We repaired that late, and I would tell you to repair it early. If the mission makes a decision that costs a specific person their bonus, their pet project or their easiest quarter, then the mission, meaning you, owes that person a visible repair. Otherwise you teach your best people that the sentence on the wall eats their lunch, and they will quietly stop bringing you opportunities altogether, which is a worse disease than the scope creep you were defending against.

Third, most nos are not permanent, and pretending they are makes them harder to say. The honest categories are three: no, because it is not our job and never will be; not yet, because it might become our job and we will look again on a named date; and no for us, but here is who you should call. Sorting an opportunity into a category is easier, emotionally and politically, than issuing a verdict on it.

Fourth, and this is the discipline that separates companies that say no once from companies that can keep saying it: write the nos down. Memory, as we established in the introduction, is a helpful colleague who throws away your mistakes, and it is just as cheerful about throwing away your refusals. Six months after a hard no, when the mood has shifted and someone new is in the room, the opportunity will come back, and without a record you will re-litigate it from zero, minus the people who remember why. The fix costs one page. I call it the no ledger, and at the routing company it lived in the same folder as the mission itself.

The ledger has a second, sneakier benefit. Read back over a year of it and you have an X-ray of your mission's health. Lots of rows means the sentence is working. Empty pages mean either your market is unusually polite or, far more likely, everything has been getting in, and it is time to reread the smell test with the lights on.

Try this

The retroactive bouncer, twenty minutes, pen and one sheet. List the last five significant yeses your company said: deals, features, hires into new areas, partnerships. Hold each one against your draft mission and mark it in or out, honestly. If all five pass, do not celebrate. A mission that blesses everything you already wanted is a mirror, so tighten the sentence until at least one past yes would have been a no, and then sit for a moment with what that yes has actually cost you since. That discomfort is the feeling of a mission growing teeth.

Missions for Businesses That Do Not Want to Change the World

I need to spend a section pushing back on an inflation, because it quietly excludes most of the people this book is for.

Somewhere in the last few decades, the mission statement got annexed by companies that intend to reorganize civilization, and the vocabulary inflated to match. Missions now come with planets in them. And the practical consequence, which I have watched over many kitchen tables, is that the owner of a nine-person installation firm, or a village bakery, or a bookkeeping practice, concludes that missions are not for them, because they have no intention of disrupting anything. They intend to fit boilers properly, which apparently no longer qualifies as a purpose.

Let me say this with my whole chest: the size of a mission has nothing to do with the size of the words. A mission is a job, and jobs are dignified at every scale. "Nobody in this village starts a Sunday with bad bread" is a complete, working, load-bearing mission. It names a customer, it is checkable by seven a.m., and it can say no: no to the supermarket wholesale deal that would gut quality, no to the second location two towns over that would break the word village, no to the food-service contract that pays well and turns craft into logistics. I know the baker behind that sentence, more or less; you will formally meet Jonas, my brother-in-law, in chapter 4, and how one bakery became nine without deleting its sentence is his story to carry there.

The same goes for the trades and the practices. "No family in our postcode spends a second night without heating" is a mission; the emergency van, the stocked parts, and the premium price all fall out of it logically, the same way Henk's half-past-six opening hours fell out of his. "Our clients open letters from the tax office without their pulse changing" is a mission, and it tells the bookkeeping firm exactly which clients to resign: the ones who create the letters faster than anyone can prevent them.

Notice something else about these small missions: not one of them needs to be published. There is a persistent confusion between the mission and the slogan, and it dissolves the moment you remember who each is for. The slogan is for customers, and belongs to your brand, which gets its own treatment in chapter 8. The mission is for the people inside, a decision instrument, and it is allowed to be as unglamorous as a good tool usually is. Henk's mission hung behind the counter, facing the staff. The customers never read it. They felt it, every time the van showed up before the silence got expensive, and feeling it is the only publication a mission needs.

If your ambition is genuinely planetary, wonderful; write the checkable version anyway and let the planet be implied. And if your ambition is a full counter in one village, write that, in exactly those words, and put this book down for a moment if anyone tries to make you feel small about it. They are confusing altitude with worth. Some of the best-run companies I have ever seen fit their entire mission inside a breath and their entire market inside a postcode, and they say no with a fluency the planet-reorganizers could learn from.

When the Mission Changes

Everything I have said so far argues for holding the line, so let me now argue honestly for the other side, because missions do change, and pretending otherwise turns a working tool into a relic.

The question is never whether a mission may change. It is how. And the answer has three words in it: rarely, deliberately, and out loud. The failure mode is not change. The failure mode is drift, which is change without a decision: one accommodating yes at a time, each defensible, none announced, until the company wakes up doing a different job than the one on the wall and cannot name the day it started. Drift is how the customs module happened to us. Nobody ever decided the routing company was in the customs business. We just never quite decided it was not.

A deliberate change is the opposite animal. It happens in the open, it has a date, and it comes with the same courtesies as any serious change of occupation: you tell the people it affects, in person where it matters, and you update the ledger, because some old nos may deserve a fresh hearing under the new sentence. If changing your mission does not feel slightly ceremonial, slightly expensive, and slightly sad, you are probably not changing it. You are drifting with paperwork.

How do you know when a deliberate change is actually due? Three signals, in rising order of seriousness. The mildest: your no ledger shows the same "not yet" coming back season after season, each time with better evidence, until refusing it starts to require ignoring your own customers. The middle signal: the honest version of the go-away exercise, rerun with fresh sales, produces a different sentence than the one on file, meaning the company has already moved and the words are lagging. And the loudest: the customer's problem itself is dissolving or migrating, technology or regulation or habit slowly closing the job down. That last one is the existential case, and it is exactly where Henk, of all people, earned his opinion.

Story

In the nineties, cheap imported parts arrived in Henk's trade like weather. Catalogues first, then fax orders, then the early internet, and suddenly the bearing he stocked at a proud margin could be ordered from abroad for a third of the price by anyone with patience. Two of the three wholesalers in his region responded by cutting prices and doubling catalogue thickness. Both were gone within a decade.

Henk sat with it for a while, in his customs-clearance way, and then did something that looked like nothing. He reread his own counter sign, the one that said nobody wants parts, and concluded that absolutely nothing about his job had changed. Farmers still paid to make expensive silences go away. The imports were only a change in tools, and cheaper tools, he reasoned, were arguably good news for a man whose actual product was speed. So he kept his prices, stopped competing on the catalogue entirely, and put the money into the two things the fax machines could not do: stock within an hour's drive of every customer, and a phone that a human answered at half past six. He even started ordering from the cheap importers himself, cheerfully, for anything that could wait.

"The others thought the mission was selling parts at a margin," he told me, decades later, in the kitchen. "So when the margin died, they died with it. My job was silence." He tapped the table twice, his version of italics. "The job stays. The tools change. And you do not repaint the sign just because you bought a new van."

That last line is the whole doctrine, and it gives you the test to run before any rewrite: is it the job that changed, or only the tools? Tool changes, however dramatic they feel from inside, new technology, new product, new channel, do not touch the mission, and rewriting it for them is a way of playing with the sign instead of driving the van. Job changes are rarer and deeper: the person changed, or the pain changed, or the pain is going away on its own. Those deserve the ceremony. Everything else deserves a calm rereading of the sentence you already have, which, if you wrote it at the right altitude, was built to survive exactly this.

Where the Mission Lives

Last thing, briefly, because a mission that has passed every test in this chapter can still die of loneliness. A sentence only stays sharp if it gets used, and using it means giving it standing appointments in the ordinary machinery of the company. Not a framework, just habits. Four of them carried most of the weight for us.

In hiring, the mission opened every first interview, one breath, no slides, followed by a question: "what would you want to know about that?" What a candidate asks about your mission tells you more than anything they claim about their passion for it. In sales, it worked as the qualifying gate: prospects whose problem was not our job got a fast, friendly no with a referral, which cost us nothing and built us a strange, durable reputation for honesty that eventually became a sales asset in itself. In the weekly planning meeting, it sat at the top of the page, less as inspiration than as jurisdiction, the answer to "why is this on our list at all." And twice a year it stood inspection: the smell test, the ledger review, and one honest hour on whether the job had moved.

You will notice none of that requires the wall. If the machinery for turning a mission into quarterly goals, metrics and meeting rhythms is what you are hungry for, that is chapter 13's territory, and it will still be there when you arrive. The mission has to be true and used before it is worth cascading; cascade a poem and you get a very organized poem.

As for our vinyl letters: they finally came down years later, when we repainted the office, and I stood there with a heat gun expecting to feel something. Nothing. Nobody so much as mentioned it for a month, and the day someone did, a new hire asking what used to be on the wall, three people answered by reciting the real mission instead, the one that was never printed anywhere. That is where a mission lives, in the end. Not on the wall. In the doorway, working.

The job, then, is decided. What remains, before we get anywhere near products and customers, is the question the job quietly raises: if we keep doing this well, for years, where does it all end up? That postcard from the future is vision, and writing one that is not a hallucination is the next chapter's work.

The Short Version

  • Your mission is the job your company does in the world, in the customer's terms, not the poster in the lobby.
  • Start with Henk's question: what do people pay you to make go away? Dig the answer out of your last five sales and your angriest support tickets.
  • A mission is a bouncer, not a banner. If it has never changed a decision, and never cost you money, it is decoration.
  • Fly at the working altitude: the sentence should outlive your current product, but not your customer's problem.
  • Build it from a working verb, a named customer, and a boundary. One breath, sayable in a doorway. "We do not" is a legitimate second sentence, and often the more valuable one.
  • Draft alone or with cofounders; let the group attack it with real decisions, never write it by committee.
  • Run the smell test twice a year. Three smells or more, open a window and rewrite.
  • Sort every no into a category: never, not yet with a date, or no-but-here-is-who-to-call. Deliver big nos with a face on them.
  • Keep a no ledger. An empty ledger means the door is letting everything in.
  • Change the mission rarely, deliberately, and out loud. The job stays, the tools change, and you do not repaint the sign because you bought a new van.
