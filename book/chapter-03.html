Chapter 3

Articulating Vision

A vision is a postcard from the future, and this chapter is about writing one that people actually want to visit.

Priya keeps a whiteboard in her office that nobody is allowed to erase.

The whiteboard is small, one of those sixty-by-ninety ones you buy at an office supply store when you do not yet trust yourself with a wall-sized one. It hangs crooked next to her desk, and across the top, in red marker and capital letters, it says DO NOT ERASE, followed by a date. Below that, in her ordinary handwriting, sits a single paragraph. When I visited her office a few years ago, the paragraph had been there long enough that the marker had gone from black to a tired grey, and someone had traced over the fading letters with a fresh pen, the way you retouch a fresco.

You should know who Priya is, because she will keep turning up in this book. She was one of the first product people at the routing company, back when our idea of an office plant was a cactus somebody had abandoned, and she was better at her job than the company deserved. Then she did the thing your best people eventually do: she left to start her own company. Hers is a B2B software business that helps mid-size manufacturers catch quality problems on the factory floor while they are still small, instead of discovering them months later in a spreadsheet, at which point they have become recalls, lawsuits, or very long meetings.

The paragraph on the whiteboard says, roughly, this. I am quoting from memory and she would correct three words, but the shape is right:

"It is a Tuesday in March, six years from now. At 7:40 in the morning, a production manager walks onto a factory floor in Eindhoven. A machine on line three started drifting out of tolerance at 6:15. By 6:25, the one technician who could fix it knew about it, saw what the machine saw, and fixed it before the first batch was scrapped. Nobody filled in a form. Nobody will reconstruct the problem in October from a spreadsheet. In every factory like this one, from Porto to Gdańsk, quality is something the floor sees in minutes, not something the office discovers in months. We built the thing that made that normal."

Here is how that paragraph came to exist. In her second year, Priya did what second-year founders with a bit of money do: she hired help. A strategy consultancy ran a two-day offsite in a hotel near Utrecht, with breakout groups and flip charts and a facilitator who said "alignment" the way other people breathe. At the end of it, the consultancy delivered, among other laminated artifacts, a vision statement. It read: "To become the leading European platform for smart manufacturing quality solutions."

Priya told me she read that sentence out loud to her team, watched eleven intelligent people nod the way you nod at a stranger's holiday photos, and felt something in her chest that she later identified as shame. The sentence was accurate, defensible, and nothing. It could have hung in the lobby of any of her competitors without a single word changed. It described no morning, no factory, no person, no changed world. It described a ranking. Nobody starts a company to hold a ranking.

She came back to the office alone on the Saturday after the offsite, still angry, mostly at herself, because she had paid real money for the sentence. She stood at the little whiteboard and asked herself the only question that ended up mattering: if this works, completely works, what does an ordinary Tuesday look like for the person we built it for? Then she wrote the paragraph, dated it, wrote DO NOT ERASE above it, and went home.

The laminated card went into a drawer, where it remains, presumably still leading Europe.

The whiteboard, meanwhile, got a job. Engineers started walking over to it mid-argument, reading it, and settling feature debates without her in the room, because "does this get us closer to 6:25?" turns out to be a question two engineers can answer alone. New hires read it on their first morning, and Priya says she can tell within a minute, from their faces, whether the hire was right. When a big customer asked her to build a general-purpose reporting module, a good, sensible, revenue-bearing request, she looked at the whiteboard and said no, and the whiteboard took half the pain out of it. And on the genuinely bad days, of which her company has had several worth their own chapter, the paragraph did the one thing a spreadsheet cannot do: it reminded everyone what all the suffering was supposed to purchase.

That paragraph is a vision. The laminated sentence was lobby art.

This chapter is about writing yours: what a vision is, how it differs from the mission you wrote in the last chapter and the strategy you will build in the next one, how far into the future it should look, what the good ones are made of, and how to keep one alive for years without it curdling into wallpaper. By the end you should have a postcard from your own company's future, in your own handwriting, worth writing DO NOT ERASE above.

A Postcard, Not a Prophecy

Let me give you the definition I will use for the rest of the book, and then spend a chapter earning it.

A vision is a postcard from the future. It is a short, concrete, sensory description of the world as it looks if your company succeeds, written as if you are already standing there, addressed to the people who have to do the walking.

Every word of that definition is load-bearing, so let me take the postcard apart the way you would an actual one from a friend.

A postcard is written from a place. Nobody writes "we intend to be among the leading visitors of the Italian coast." They write "the water is so clear you can see your feet, and the man at the bakery knows our order now." The writer stands in the scene, sand still on their shoes. That is the tense of a vision: it is six years from now, and here is what is true. The moment you write from inside the future instead of pointing at it from the present, the future starts demanding decisions about what is actually there, and that demand is most of the value.

A postcard is specific. It mentions the bakery, the water, the feet. Priya's paragraph has a time of day in it, twice. It has Eindhoven in it. It has a technician in it. Specificity is what separates a vision from a horoscope, and I will be rude about horoscopes later in this chapter, because most published vision statements are exactly that: sentences engineered to be true of anyone and falsifiable by no one.

A postcard is short. One card, one side, room for a stamp. A vision that needs a slide deck is a plan wearing a costume. A paragraph is the right size. A single sentence can work if it is muscular enough, but in my experience one sentence buys brevity by selling detail, and detail is what your people will actually use.

A postcard is addressed to someone. This is the part founders forget. The postcard goes to the people who have to walk to the place on the card: your team, your future team, and on the bad nights, you. The market, the press, and the pitch deck can read adaptations later. Write the vision knowing exactly who will read it and when, and the when is usually a moment of doubt.

And a postcard says, implicitly, wish you were here. It describes somewhere worth the journey. This is the emotional job of a vision, and it is a real job, not decoration. Humans do not sustain effort for abstractions. Nobody has ever stayed late, twice, for "leading European platform." People stay late for the technician at 6:25, for the queue outside the second shop, for the dispatcher who gets home for dinner. If the place on your postcard is not somewhere your own people would want to live, no amount of wordsmithing will fix it, because the problem is the destination, not the description.

The postcard has edges, and the edges matter as much as the picture. You are choosing a future, out of all the futures you could aim at, and committing the company's walking in that direction; whether you chose right stays an open question until the grading ceremony at the end of this chapter, and prophecy never enters into it. The roads belong to strategy, as we are about to see; the postcard stays silent about them. And nothing on the card gets assigned to anyone. The moment your vision contains the word "Q3," something has gone wrong that this chapter will try to prevent.

One more reassurance before the machinery. You may be reading this as the owner of a bakery, an agency, a plumbing firm, a two-person consultancy, and thinking: postcards from the future are for the software people, I just want a second shop. I promise this chapter is for you, arguably more than for the software people, who at least get vision talk forced on them by investors. "It is five years from now. There is a queue outside both shops on Saturday morning, half the town knows our bread by name, and three kids who started as weekend help are running counters of their own" is a complete, working vision, and it will do for a bakery exactly what Priya's whiteboard does for her factory software: settle arguments, sort job applicants, and make the fourth year of hard mornings mean something.

The same goes for the agency, the consultancy, the installation firm, any business where the temptation is to say "our vision is to do good work and grow a bit." I understand the modesty, and the modesty is the problem. "Good work" is a standard, not a destination, and it gives your best people nothing to aim their ambition at, so they will eventually aim it somewhere else, usually at a competitor or a company of their own. An agency postcard might read: "It is three years from now. Clients come to us for the one thing we are named for, before they know our prices. We have said no to two kinds of work all year, publicly, and both noes brought us better clients than the yeses would have. Nobody here works for a client they are embarrassed to name at a birthday party." That is modest in size and immodest in clarity, which is the correct ratio.

Vision, Mission, Strategy: Once, and Never Again

Now the untangling I promised in the introduction. These three words cause more confusion per syllable than anything else in business vocabulary, partly because consultancies benefit from the confusion and partly because ordinary usage genuinely overlaps. We are going to pin them down here, in one table, and then this book will never re-explain them. Later chapters will simply use the words and trust you to have been here.

The fastest way in is through a question each one answers.

Your mission answers: what job do we do in the world, every day, for whom? You write it in the present tense because it is already true, on your very first day, at your very smallest. The previous chapter was about writing one sharp enough to say no with, and I will not repeat its mechanics here; if you skipped chapter 2, that is where mission lives.

Your vision answers: if we keep doing that job well, what does the world look like in the end? You write it in the future tense, or better, in Priya tense: the future described as present. The mission is the walking; the vision is the place.

Your strategy answers: given where we stand, what is in the way, and what we are choosing not to do, how do we get from here to there? It is conditional, argumentative, and mortal. It changes when the facts change, and the facts change often. Chapter 4 builds your first one for product and service, and chapter 7 deals with keeping plans alive, so here I only need strategy as a contrast color.

A worked example makes the rows breathe. Take Priya's company. Mission: we help mid-size manufacturers see and fix quality problems on the floor within minutes. Present tense, true when she had one customer. Vision: the whiteboard paragraph, the Tuesday, the 6:25. Strategy, at the time I visited: win the Dutch and German mid-market first, integrate with the three machine vendors that cover most floors, stay out of enterprise deals we cannot service, charge per line rather than per seat. Notice how mortal that third sentence is. Two of those four commitments have since changed, and rightly. The whiteboard has not.

Or take the bakery, because the software people have had enough attention. Mission: we bake bread good enough that the town plans its Saturday around it. Vision: the queue outside two shops, the weekend kids running counters. Strategy: second shop on the north side rather than the market square, because the rent there buys three years of patience; hire a second baker before a second seller; no franchising, ever, or at least no franchising this decade. Same species of thinking as Priya's, different flour.

The three form a chain, and the chain has a direction. Identity, from chapter 1, tells you who is walking. Mission tells you what the walker does. Vision tells you where the walking leads. Strategy picks the road. When the chain is intact, decisions at the bottom can be traced to beliefs at the top, and a new employee can learn the whole of it in an afternoon. When a link is missing, you get the classic failure smells: a company with vision and no mission is a dreamer that cannot describe its own workday; a company with mission and no vision does honest work that accumulates into nothing in particular; a company with strategy and neither of the others is a mercenary, briefly effective and permanently for sale.

Two confusions are worth clearing while we are here, because they generate most of the bad documents I get asked to read.

First, the direction of dependence. Strategy serves vision, never the reverse. If your vision has become "whatever our current strategy achieves if it works," what you own is a forecast with lighting. The tell: every strategy pivot triggers a vision rewrite. Real visions survive pivots; they are the reason pivots have a direction.

Second, the boundary with goals. A vision is not a big goal, and stacking goals does not add up to a vision. "One hundred million euros in revenue by 2030" is a milestone someone might set on the road to a vision, but it describes nothing about the world except your bank account, and your bank account inspires exactly one person, sometimes. How visions decompose into annual and quarterly goals is chapter 13's business, and I will not poach it here. For now, hold the distinction the simple way: a goal is what you check off, a vision is what you check against.

How Far Away Is the Horizon?

The first practical question everyone asks, usually within a minute of agreeing to write a vision, is: how far out? Three years? Five? Ten? Twenty-five, like the corporate sustainability reports? And the honest answer is that "the future" is not one place. It is several distances, and each distance is good for a different thing, and most vision documents fail by treating them as interchangeable.

Henk, who you met in the introduction and who never wrote a vision statement in his life while possessing one of the clearest visions I have ever encountered, put it in his usual way when I once tried to impress him with a ten-year plan for the routing company. He listened, sipped his coffee, and said: "You are describing next year's weather in ten-year words. From the dyke you can see the sea. You cannot see the waves." I asked him, a little wounded, what he could see at ten years when he ran the wholesale business. "Direction," he said. "Every workshop in the region orders from us before they check anyone else. That was the whole thing. I could not have told you the turnover, the building, the number of vans. I could tell you the direction with my eyes closed, and I steered twenty years of Tuesdays by it." Then he pointed out that my ten-year plan had a headcount table in it, and asked whether I also knew what I would be having for lunch on the fourth of March in nine years. It took me an embarrassing number of years to fully unpack the dyke line, so let me save you the time.

Stand where you are and look forward. There are three usable distances, and a fourth that fools people.

Now is where your feet are. It is made of facts: your customers, your cash, your capabilities, your reputation, this morning's inbox. It is not part of the vision, but every vision is written by someone standing here, and pretending otherwise produces fantasy. The now is chapter 6's and chapter 7's raw material.

Three years is the distance of the photograph. Close enough that you can describe it in checkable detail: roughly how many people, which kind of customer, what a Tuesday looks like, what you are known for. Far enough that it is not just this year's plan with better lighting. Three years is where Priya's paragraph mostly lives, whatever its nominal date says, and it is the distance I recommend for the postcard itself. You can see individual waves at three years. You can be wrong at three years, which is a feature: a vision you cannot fail to reach was never pulling you.

Ten years is the distance of the direction. At ten years, detail is dishonest. You do not know your headcount in ten years, and anyone who writes it down is decorating. What you can know at ten years is a heading: the change in the world you are walking toward, the kind of company you refuse to become on the way. "Quality is something the floor sees in minutes, everywhere" is a ten-year heading. It is a compass bearing, not an address, and it should survive the failure of any particular three-year photograph.

Beyond ten years is the fog of someday. Statements set there are unfalsifiable by construction, which is exactly why a certain kind of founder loves them: nobody living will be held to account. If your vision only works at the twenty-five-year distance, it is not a vision, it is an alibi.

So a complete vision, in practice, has two moving parts: a ten-year heading in one sentence, and a three-year postcard in one paragraph, written in the direction of the heading. Priya's whiteboard happens to contain both, which is why it works: the Tuesday morning is the photograph, and "from Porto to Gdańsk, quality is something the floor sees in minutes" is the bearing. When the three years pass, and they will pass faster than any spreadsheet suggests, you write a new postcard from further up the same road. The heading stays. The photographs succeed each other like stages of a long walk.

In twenty years of watching small companies, the single best predictor I know for whether year four's grind gets survived is whether anyone can describe year six out loud without checking notes.

What a Good Postcard Is Made Of

Suppose you accept the assignment: one paragraph, three years out, written from inside. What separates a paragraph that becomes a whiteboard from a paragraph that becomes a drawer? Having now read more company visions than any person should, including several dozen I was paid to be polite about, I can report that the good ones share five properties, and the bad ones fail the same five ways with remarkable discipline.

It contains a person who is not you

The strongest visions have a character in them, and the character is a customer, a user, a person on the receiving end of the work. The production manager at 7:40. The dispatcher home for dinner. The Saturday queue. When the only actor in your vision is the company itself, "we will be, we will have, we will dominate," you have written a trophy inscription, not a postcard. The world of your vision should be recognizably better for someone with a name-shaped hole in the sentence, because that person is what your team is actually working for, whatever the pitch deck says.

It could be observed

A good vision is made of things a camera could film or a visitor could notice. Times of day. Queues. Rooms. Numbers of shops. Sentences customers say. "Trusted partner" cannot be filmed. "The three biggest machine vendors ship with our integration in the box" can be checked with a phone call. The test I use: could a sceptical journalist visit your company in three years and verify, or falsify, most of the paragraph in an afternoon? If nothing in the vision could ever be witnessed, nothing in it will ever be worked toward, because no one can tell the difference between progress and drift.

It would frighten the current company a little

A postcard describing somewhere you have essentially already arrived is not a vision, it is a review. There should be at least one sentence in the paragraph that your current operation cannot deliver, that makes the honest reader swallow: every factory from Porto to Gdańsk, when today you have eleven customers and nine of them are within cycling distance of Eindhoven. The gap is the engine. A vision with no gap generates no pull, and a vision that is all gap, colonies on Mars for a company that has not shipped, generates no belief. You are tuning a distance: far enough to pull, near enough to believe.

It excludes futures you could plausibly have

This is the least understood property and the most valuable. A vision is a choice among available futures, and a choice that rejects nothing is not a choice. Priya's paragraph quietly excludes several companies she could have built: the enterprise-only vendor, the general analytics platform, the consultancy with software on the side. All were available. All had money in them. The paragraph closes those doors, which is exactly what allowed her team to settle arguments at the whiteboard without her. When you read your draft, ask: what attractive company does this sentence forbid us from becoming? If the answer is none, you have written weather, not a destination.

Your own people would want to live there

Finally, the wish-you-were-here property. Read the paragraph and ask whether the people who work for you, the real ones, with their actual mortgages and their actual reasons for joining, would find the described world worth their next three years. Not whether it impresses strangers. Whether it moves insiders. A vision that excites the founder and bores the team is a vanity plate. This is also the property that keeps visions humane: somewhere in that future Tuesday, implicitly, your own people are doing work they are proud of, and if you cannot see them in the picture, they will notice their absence long before you do.

Because the failures are so uniform, you can diagnose most weak visions by phrase. Here is the field guide I wish someone had handed me at the routing company, in the years when our vision was a revenue number with a halo.

Writing Yours

Now the pen part. I am going to walk you through the process I use with founders, which is also, unromantically, the process Priya reverse-engineered on her angry Saturday. It takes about two hours the first time, most of which is spent deleting. You do not need an offsite, a facilitator, or a hotel near Utrecht. You need a door that closes and your own handwriting, because handwriting slows you down to the speed of meaning, and typing lets you produce "leading European platform" without your brain being consulted.

Step one: pick the day, not the achievement

Do not start with what the company has achieved. Start with a calendar. Pick an ordinary day roughly three years out, a Tuesday, deliberately not a launch day or an award ceremony, and write the date at the top of the page. The discipline of ordinariness matters: anyone can imagine their triumphant day, the funding announcement, the ribbon on the second shop. Triumphant days are rare by definition and tell you nothing about what the company is like. The vision question is what an unremarkable day looks like when the work has succeeded, because unremarkable days are what a company is made of. If the ordinary Tuesday is good, the company is good.

Step two: choose whose morning you are watching

Pick one person on the receiving end of your work, as concrete as you can make them. Not "our customers," but the production manager, the dispatcher, the woman third in the Saturday queue. If you did the mission work in chapter 2 you already know who this is, because it is the person whose problem you exist to make go away. Now watch their morning, three years from now, in the world where you succeeded, and write what happens to them. What time is it? What do they no longer do? What happens in minutes that used to take months? What do they say about you to a colleague, in their own words, when you are not in the room? That last one is dangerous and useful; it is also the seed of chapter 8, where reputation gets a whole chapter to itself.

Step three: widen the lens twice

A single morning is a scene, not yet a world. Widen twice. First to the company: on that same Tuesday, what is true about you? Roughly how many people, in what kind of rooms, doing what kind of work, known for what? Resist precision theater; "about thirty of us" is honest, "34 FTE" is astrology with a spreadsheet. Then widen to the field you play in: what has changed out there because you exist? This is where the ten-year heading first shows its face, the Porto-to-Gdańsk sentence, the change that would remain worth pursuing even if this particular photograph turns out wrong. Write one sentence at that widest setting, no more. One is a bearing. Three is a manifesto, and manifestos are where headings go to gather adjectives.

Here is the widening done for the bakery, so you can see the mechanics rather than take them on faith. Scene: it is a Saturday, three years out, and the woman third in the queue has driven from the next town, because her sister served our bread at a birthday and she has been coming since. First widening, the company: two shops, a night baker in each, and the Saturday shift is run entirely by the two weekend kids who started three years ago, now trained, now trusted, now paid like it. The owner is not behind either counter; she is at the market negotiating for spelt, which is the first Saturday morning of her adult life she has ever had free. Second widening, the field: in this town, "we will just get bread there" has become a default sentence, the way it used to be a decision. One bearing: bread good enough to plan a Saturday around, in more towns, without ever becoming a factory. Notice what got written down: a driver from the next town, a free Saturday, a spelt negotiation. Furniture, all of it, and every stick of it checkable.

Step four: delete until it fits on the card

You now have a page or two. The vision is the paragraph hiding inside it. Delete every adjective that a competitor could also claim. Delete every sentence about being rather than doing. Delete the parts that are secretly this year's plan, and put them where plans live, in chapter 7's machinery. Delete the number that is secretly a goal, and give it to chapter 13. What survives should be five to eight sentences with a person, a time of day, a company you can glimpse, and one bearing. Read it against the five properties from the previous section, especially the fourth: name, out loud, the attractive company this paragraph forbids you from becoming. If you cannot, sharpen until you can.

Step five: read it aloud to the wrong person

Finally, the test almost nobody does. Read the paragraph aloud to someone outside your bubble: your partner, a friend with a normal job, Henk if you can find one. Not for approval, for comprehension and appetite. You are listening for two reactions. The first is whether they can repeat the gist back to you an hour later, because a vision that cannot survive one hop of retelling will not survive an organization. The second is whether some flicker crosses their face that says "that would be good," even though they have no stake. Strangers are immune to your sunk costs. If the future on your postcard cannot mildly interest a stranger, it is unlikely to sustain an employee through a bad February.

Try this

Tonight, by hand, write the postcard: a date roughly three years out at the top, then five to eight sentences describing an ordinary Tuesday in the world where your company has succeeded. Rules: at least one person who is not you, at least one time of day, no adjectives a competitor could also claim, no revenue numbers, and one final sentence of pure direction, the change you are walking toward. Tomorrow morning, before coffee, read it once and cross out everything that embarrasses you. What survives is your first draft, and it is already better than most of what hangs in lobbies.

Who the Vision Is For

Here is the mistake that ruins more visions than bad writing ever will: writing the postcard to the wrong address.

The moment a vision exists, everyone wants a copy. Investors want it for the deck. Marketing wants it for the website. Sales wants it for the first slide, the one nobody remembers. And because those audiences are loud, and because their approval feels like progress, the gravitational pull is to write for them: bigger market words, rounder numbers, a future selected for applause rather than for truth. Resist this with everything you have, because a vision optimized for outsiders is a vision your insiders will quietly stop believing, and insiders are the only people who ever actually walk toward one.

Story

Priya raised money exactly once, a single round, and she will tell you over one drink that it saved the company and over two drinks what it cost. One cost was the vision. Her lead investor, a decent man with a fund to return, kept nudging the postcard outward during the raise: the factory floor became "industrial operations," Europe became "global," the technician at 6:25 became "an AI-driven quality intelligence layer." None of the edits were lies, exactly. Each one traded a picture for a market size. The deck version ended with a slide she once recited to me from memory, in the flat voice of someone reading their own hostage statement: "the operating system for global manufacturing excellence."

The raise worked. Then the deck version started leaking backwards into the company. It appeared in a job ad. A new sales hire, reasonably, chased two enormous American enterprise deals that matched the slide and not the whiteboard, and both deals ate nine months of engineering roadmap before dying of procurement. At the next all-hands, one of her earliest engineers asked, without malice, which company they worked at: the whiteboard one or the deck one. Priya says it was the single most useful question anyone ever asked her, and that her answer, after a horrible pause, was to walk over to the whiteboard, tap it, and say "this one pays your salary." She kept the deck for investor updates, relabelled internally, with characteristic precision, as "the postcard we sold, not the one we send." The regret she actually names from that round is not the dilution. It is the eighteen months during which her own company was not entirely sure which future it was walking toward.

The lesson is not that investors are villains; Priya's investor did his job, and chapter 6 covers when taking money is right. The lesson is about addressing. A vision has one primary audience, your present and future team, and one primary reading condition, doubt. It gets read for real when a customer churns, when a rival raises a huge round, when a good engineer wonders whether to accept the offer, when it is February and everything is beige. Write for that reader, in that weather. Everyone else gets adaptations, and adaptations are fine, as long as everybody inside knows which text is canonical.

There is a second audience worth naming, and it is you. Founders are exposed to more whiplash than anyone in the building: every conference, every competitor announcement, every clever stranger offers a new future by Friday. The written vision is your own keel. At the routing company I eventually learned to run new opportunities against the postcard the way sailors check a compass, not because the compass is always right, but because it is the only instrument on board that is not excited. Shiny things that survived the paragraph got considered. Shiny things that required rewriting the paragraph had to clear a much higher bar: they had to be worth changing the destination for, and almost nothing is.

And the third audience is people who do not work for you yet. A concrete vision is the cheapest recruiting instrument that exists, because the labor market for believers clears differently than the market for mercenaries. You cannot outbid a corporate salary with a bakery or a nine-person software firm, and you should not try. What you have that the corporate cannot print is a specific, sincere future with room in it for the person you are talking to. In my experience the candidates worth having ask about the future unprompted, and what they are testing is not the future's size but its resolution. Vague answers read as "this founder is winging it." A Tuesday at 6:25 reads as "this founder has thought about where I would be spending my years." Chapter 10 covers hiring properly; here I will only say that every strong hire the routing company made after year five quoted the vision back at me in their interview, and not one of the regrettable ones did.

Nobody gets out of bed early for "leading provider." People get out of bed for a Tuesday they can picture and want.

What a Vision Does All Day

A fair objection at this point: this is all very moving, Wouter, but I run a business, and paragraphs do not load vans. What does a vision actually do, on an ordinary working day, that justifies the two hours and the wall space?

I kept notes on this at the routing company, in the years after we finally wrote a real one, because I was sceptical too and wanted to catch the paragraph earning its keep or expose it as decoration. Here is the honest inventory of what it did, in descending order of frequency.

It settled arguments below me. This is the biggest one and the least glamorous. Companies burn astonishing hours on disputes that are secretly disputes about destination: which feature, which customer segment, which trade-off between speed and polish. When the destination is written down and specific, a meaningful share of those disputes can be settled by two people and the paragraph, without escalation. The escalations that still reached me were the genuinely hard ones, which is what I was for. Before the vision, everything reached me, because I was the only place the destination existed, and I did not scale.

It made saying no cheaper. The mission, as chapter 2 laid out, is your bouncer for requests that are not your job. The vision handles a subtler case: requests that are plausibly your job but point down a different road. Somebody offers you a lucrative custom project, an adjacent product, a partnership with someone large and flattering. The mission often cannot refuse these; they are, technically, the job. The vision can, because the vision knows which of your possible companies you are building. No becomes "not on the way to where we are going" instead of "I have a bad feeling," and people accept the first from a leader far more gracefully than the second.

The clearest case I have: in year seven, a large fleet-leasing company offered the routing company a partnership that would have put our software in front of thousands of vans overnight, provided we built and maintained a white-label version under their brand, tuned to their leasing contracts. The numbers were genuinely good. Half my management team wanted it, and their arguments were sound, and the meeting was heading the way meetings head when the spreadsheet is the only text in the room. So I read the postcard out loud, which felt theatrical and took forty seconds, and then asked one question: in this deal, does routing become electricity, or does it become a leasing feature? The room went quiet in the specific way that means everyone has arrived at the same answer and is deciding who says it first. We countered with a plain integration instead, kept our name on the product, and lost most of the upside and none of the company. I still think about that deal sometimes. I have never once thought we chose wrong.

It gave bad quarters a shape. Every company has seasons where the present tense is simply unpleasant: a lost customer, a failed launch, a competitor with better luck. Companies without a future tense experience these seasons as identity verdicts, and morale follows. Companies with a vivid future experience them as terrain, a bad stretch of a road that still leads somewhere. Same facts, different weather. I do not want to oversell this; a postcard does not make payroll, and chapter 6 is about the thing that does. But I have watched two teams take equivalent punches, one with a destination and one without, and they got up differently.

Story

The routing company was five years old and about twenty-five people before it had a vision anyone could recite, and I know the exact evening we got one, because it began with me failing an interview. We were trying to close a senior engineer, the kind you build a hiring process around, and at the end of a good conversation she asked the obvious question: where is this going? And I gave her, God forgive me, the growth answer. Revenue trajectory, headcount plan, maybe an office in Germany. I watched the light go out of her face in real time, politely, like a hotel corridor. She declined the offer that Friday with a sentence I kept: "It sounds like a company that is getting bigger, and I could not tell what it was getting bigger at."

I spent that weekend at the kitchen table with a pen, doing badly what this chapter now teaches, and by Sunday I had a paragraph. Ours was about a dispatcher in Liège, a woman we had actually met on a customer visit, who planned routes for two hundred stops with a cigarette lighter holding down the corner of a paper map. The postcard said, roughly: it is four years from now, she plans those two hundred stops in ten minutes, the last driver is home for dinner, and the companies that own the vans think about routing the way they think about electricity, which is to say never, because it simply works. We put it on the wall, on paper, no lamination, and I called the engineer back and read it to her. She did not join, that bridge was burnt, but she paused long enough that I knew the paragraph would work on the next one. It did, for years. People I interviewed in year eight quoted the dispatcher back to me, and by then she had a name around the office, though I never told anyone her real one.

It filtered strategy without doing strategy's job. When we later built proper product strategy, the chapter 4 kind, and later still an operating rhythm, the chapter 13 kind, the vision sat above both as the acceptance test. A proposed strategy either walked toward the dispatcher or it did not. The vision never told us how to win the German market; that is not its work. It told us that a strategy which won the German market by becoming a fleet-leasing company had misread the postcard, however good the margins looked. Keep this division of labor clean and both documents stay honest. Let them blur and you get strategy decks with sunsets on them, which serve neither purpose.

And once or twice a year, it did the ceremonial thing. New employee onboarding. The all-hands after a hard month. The anniversary. I am Dutch enough to be suspicious of ceremony, and I will admit the paragraph earned its place at those moments too. There is something in watching a room hear the destination said out loud, together, that spreadsheets have never once achieved.

Six Ways Visions Die

Visions rarely die dramatically. Nobody holds a meeting to cancel the future. They die the way houseplants die, through a sequence of small neglects that each looked survivable, and by the time anyone notices, the thing on the wall is dry. Having watched this happen from inside once and from nearby many times, I can name the six most common causes of death, and more usefully, the tell that each one is underway.

A field guide to dying visions: the six failure modes, their tells, and the repair.

Two of these deserve a closing word beyond the table, because they kill the most companies.

The Mirage is the characteristic disease of energetic founders, and I say that with the tenderness of a recovering patient. The founder returns from a conference incandescent about a new future, and because founders are persuasive, the company obligingly turns. Then it happens again. Each individual turn is defensible; the sum is a company that has walked a circle while exhausting the very people who most wanted to believe. If you have rewritten the destination twice in two years, the problem is not your vision drafts, it is that you have not yet chosen, and choosing is the founder work that no facilitator, book, or offsite can do for you. Do it once, properly, and defend it even against your own enthusiasm, especially against your own enthusiasm.

The Wallpaper is the characteristic disease of sensible companies, and it is sneakier, because nothing visibly goes wrong. The vision was written, everyone approved, it hangs in reception, and it does nothing, forever. The failure is not in the text but in the habit: a vision is a tool, and tools that are never picked up are indistinguishable from ornaments. The repair costs one sentence: the next time you make a real decision, say in front of the team which way the postcard pointed and that you followed it, or that you overrode it and why. Visions do not stay alive through repetition at all-hands. They stay alive through witnessed use, the way case law stays alive: every cited decision makes the next citation more natural.

Warning

The most dangerous week in a vision's life is the week you seek feedback on it. Shown a draft, every intelligent person will suggest making it bigger, safer, rounder, or more inclusive of their department, and each suggestion individually will seem gracious to accept. Accept them all and you will have laminated your way back to "leading European platform." Collect the feedback, thank everyone sincerely, then rewrite it alone, keeping only the edits that made the picture sharper rather than wider. A vision is one of the few documents in a company that should not be written by consensus, because its entire value is that it chooses.

Keeping the Postcard Pinned Up

The last practical question: what do you do with a vision across the years? It is a strange kind of document, too permanent to manage like a plan, too consequential to frame and forget. Here is the maintenance schedule that has worked, for me and for the founders I trust, and it is lighter than you might fear.

Read it out loud once a year, on purpose. Pick a fixed occasion, the annual kickoff, the anniversary, the first Monday after the summer holidays, and read the actual text to the actual company. Not a slide about it. The text. Two things happen. New people hear the canonical version from a human voice, which inoculates against the website adaptation. And you, reading it aloud, will feel immediately whether it is still true in your mouth. A vision that has quietly died is almost impossible to detect on a wall and almost impossible to miss when read aloud to forty people. Your own voice will tell you before your judgement does.

Check the photograph against reality when its date comes due. The three-year postcard has a date on it, and the date will arrive. When it does, hold the honest little ceremony almost nobody holds: put the paragraph next to the actual company and score it, out loud, sentence by sentence. You will find you reached some of it, missed some of it, and were wrong about the world in at least one instructive way. All three findings are valuable, and the process does something subtler: it teaches your company that the future tense is accountable, that postcards get checked, which makes the next postcard mean more. Then write the next one, from further up the road, in the direction of the same heading.

Change the bearing only for structural reasons. The ten-year heading should survive missed photographs, failed strategies, even pivots; that is its entire point. But it is not scripture. The world occasionally moves structurally: a technology makes the destination trivial, a regulation makes it impossible, or you learn something about the terrain that no amount of walking will fix. Then you change the heading, deliberately, once, with the whole company watching, and you say plainly what you learned and what it cost. Done that way, rare and explicit, a changed vision spends credibility but does not bankrupt it. Done casually, it is the Mirage, and you know how that ends.

Story

Two years ago I was back in Priya's office, and the whiteboard had changed. The old paragraph was gone, and I felt an absurd stab of loss, as if a landmark had been demolished. In its place was a new paragraph, new date, same red DO NOT ERASE, and pinned to the wall beside the board, in a cheap black frame, was a photograph of the old paragraph, with a caption in her handwriting: "Postcard one. Arrived, mostly. Three sentences early, one sentence wrong."

She talked me through the accounting like an engineer reviewing a shipped release. The 6:25 morning existed; she had stood on a floor in Brno and watched a version of it happen. Porto to Gdańsk was still fiction, and the sentence about no forms had turned out to be wrong in an interesting way, because it emerged that inspectors did not want zero forms, they wanted forms that wrote themselves, and the product had bent accordingly. The new paragraph was written from further up the same road: same technician, same heading, a bigger map, one humbler clause. What struck me most was the frame. Most companies hide their old visions the way people hide old haircuts. Priya hung hers on the wall, graded, because, as she put it, "the point was never being right. The point was that everyone walked the same way for six years. Look what that bought." I have thought about that frame more than about most books on strategy, this one included.

That is the whole lifecycle: write it alone, address it inward, read it aloud, use it in witnessed decisions, grade it when its date arrives, and write the next one from further up the road. Do this and the vision stops being a poster and becomes what it was always supposed to be, the company's shared answer to the question every human in the building is quietly asking anyway: where is all this effort going, and is it somewhere worth my years?

Try this

Take the postcard you drafted after the earlier exercise and give it a job within seven days: find one live decision, a feature, a hire, a customer request, an opportunity, and decide it explicitly against the paragraph, in front of at least one other person, saying the words "this gets us closer to the postcard" or "this does not." Then notice two things: whether the paragraph was actually able to decide, and how the other person reacted to hearing it used. If it could not decide, it is not finished, and you now know exactly which sentence is too soft. If it could, congratulations: your vision has survived first contact with a Tuesday, which is more than most visions ever manage.

The Short Version

  • A vision is a postcard from the future: short, concrete, written from inside the destination, addressed to the people who have to walk there. It is a choice among futures, not a prediction.
  • Mission is the job you do today, vision is where the work leads, strategy is the road you pick. That is untangled once, in this chapter's first table, and never again in this book.
  • Write two moving parts: a three-year postcard, one paragraph, checkable and slightly frightening, and a ten-year heading, one sentence of pure direction. Everything past ten years is fog; do not give it a font.
  • Good visions contain a person who is not you, a scene a camera could film, a gap that scares the current company a little, and at least one attractive future they forbid. Fix weak drafts by trading adjectives for furniture.
  • Address it to insiders reading it on a bad day, not to investors reading it in a deck. Adaptations for outsiders are fine as long as everyone knows which text is canonical.
  • A working vision earns its wall space daily: it settles arguments below the founder, makes saying no cheaper, gives bad quarters a shape, and quietly recruits the believers you cannot outbid for.
  • Visions die as trophies, horoscopes, hostage notes, spreadsheets, mirages, or wallpaper. The rarest and most fatal error is rewriting the destination every time the founder gets excited; choose once and defend it, especially from yourself.
  • Keep it alive with use, not repetition: read the real text aloud once a year, cite it in witnessed decisions, grade the postcard when its date arrives, then write the next one from further up the same road.
  • A revenue target is not a vision, it is a milestone; give it to chapter 13. If your vision has a currency symbol in it, you have written a spreadsheet in a cape.
