Chapter 4

Building the Product or Service Strategy

What you sell, to whom, and why they care, decided on purpose instead of discovered by accident three years in.

My brother-in-law Jonas fired sixty-eight products on a Tuesday morning, and I was there to watch, mostly because I had said something stupid at a family dinner.

The dinner was at my sister's place, and Jonas had arrived late, straight from the bakery, with flour still in the creases of his knuckles. He had bought the bakery eight months earlier from a retiring baker named Theo, and with it he had inherited Theo's full assortment: one hundred and forty products. Seven kinds of rye. A celebration cake in eight variants. Something called a "farmer's twist" that took forty minutes of hand work and sold, on a good week, four times. Jonas was working ninety-hour weeks and making, by his own accounting, slightly less per hour than the sixteen-year-old who ran his register on Saturdays.

I was a few years into the routing company by then, freshly fluent in a vocabulary I had paid a lot of tuition for, and over dessert I told him, with the confidence of a man who has read exactly enough books to be dangerous, that he could double his hours and it would change nothing, because he had no product strategy.

Jonas looked at me for a long moment. Jonas is a large, calm man who distrusts any word that would look at home on a conference lanyard, and I watched "product strategy" clear customs behind his eyes and get pulled aside for inspection.

"Fine," he said. "Come to the bakery Tuesday. Four thirty. If you can still say that sentence at four thirty in the morning, I will listen to it."

So I went. And here is the part that still embarrasses me: I had come to teach, and Jonas had already done the work. Taped inside a cabinet door, away from customer eyes, were six weeks of pink order slips he had repurposed into a ledger. Every product. Units sold per day. Price. Ingredient cost. Oven minutes. And a fourth column I have since stolen for every business I have touched: hand minutes, the human time each item ate before it earned a cent.

The pink slips told a brutal story. The croissant line, which in Jonas's shorthand meant the plain croissant, the almond one, and the ham-and-cheese one, was about a tenth of the assortment and well over half of the margin. People crossed the street for those croissants. They queued in the rain. Meanwhile the bottom half of the product list, all seventy of Theo's lovingly accumulated specialties, contributed a sliver of revenue and consumed nearly half the oven schedule and most of the 4 a.m. panic.

"Every product is a promise plus a chore," Jonas said, tapping the slips. "I am drowning in chores nobody is paying for."

That morning he wrote the kill list. Sixty-eight products gone, effective the following Monday. The seven ryes became two. The eight-variant celebration cake became one good one. The farmer's twist died unmourned by everyone except one man named Gerrit, who complained about it every Saturday for a year while buying, I want this noted, two croissants and a loaf every single time.

Revenue dipped for about a month. Then it recovered, and then it grew, because the croissants got better when the people making them stopped juggling seventy distractions, and because "the bakery with the croissants" turns out to be a thing people say to each other, while "the bakery with a hundred and forty products" is not. Margins went up by more than a third. Jonas got his Sundays back. Two years later he opened the second bakery, and today there are nine, and every one of them runs on the descendants of those pink slips.

In our family, "the croissant line" is now shorthand for the whole discipline this chapter is about. Because a product strategy, whatever the books with lanyard words tell you, is the answers to a small set of questions: what you sell, to whom, why they care, why you and not someone else, at what price, and, the question that pays for all the others, what you refuse to make. Jonas answered them on pink slips at 4:30 in the morning. You can answer them anywhere. But you do have to answer them, because if you don't, your customers, your competitors, and your own exhaustion will answer them for you, and their answers are worse.

What a Product Strategy Actually Is

Let me first take the fear out of the word "strategy," because people who charge by the hour have ruined it.

Forget the document, the deck, the forty-page PDF with a SWOT diagram on page nine. A product strategy is a small set of choices, made on purpose, that fit together. What you sell. To whom. Why they care. Why you. At what price. What you say no to. That is the whole thing. You met vision in the previous chapter, the postcard from the future, and if you want the full untangling of vision, mission and strategy, chapter three did it once in one table so nobody ever has to do it again. For our purposes here: the vision is where you are going, and the product strategy is what you are loading onto the truck.

The word that matters most in my definition is choices. A choice is only a choice if you could have picked otherwise, and it only counts as strategy if picking it costs you something. "We sell great products to customers who value quality" chooses nothing, because nobody would ever pick the opposite. Nobody positions themselves as mediocre products for people with no standards, although I have been a customer of several companies apparently run on exactly that basis. A real strategy makes some people happy and other people go home disappointed, and the disappointment is how you tell it is real. Jonas's strategy disappointed Gerrit. Gerrit stayed anyway. Most Gerrits do.

There is a second thing to notice in the definition: the choices have to fit together. Here is where most self-made strategies fall apart. Each choice on its own can be sensible, and the combination can still be nonsense. Handmade quality, at a rock-bottom price, for the mass market, with same-day delivery: every clause is attractive and the sentence is impossible. A good product strategy reads like a recipe where the ingredients belong in the same dish. When Jonas chose the croissant line, everything else got easier to choose: the morning-commuter customer, the before-work opening hours, the price that says "small daily luxury," the counter layout, even which corner of town the second bakery went into. One honest choice at the centre, and the rest of the choices line up behind it like ducklings.

And a third thing, the one this chapter will keep returning to like a tongue to a sore tooth: strategy is mostly subtraction. Adding things requires no strategy at all. Any enthusiastic founder can add products, add segments, add features, add a services arm, add a second brand. Addition feels like progress and photographs well. Subtraction is the hard discipline, because every subtraction has a name and a face: the customer who wanted it, the employee who built it, the version of you that was excited about it two years ago. We will spend a whole section on saying no later, but I want the frame planted now. A strategy, however informal, includes a list of things you deliberately refuse to do. Without that list, you are holding an appetite.

One more clarification before we get to work, so you know where this chapter stops. Strategy is deciding what is on the truck and where it is driving. Actually selling what is on the truck, the conversations, the pipeline, the follow-up, is chapter nine's whole job. And how the truck looks going down the road, the name and the voice and the reputation, that is chapter eight. Here we are in the warehouse, before dawn, with the pink slips, deciding what gets loaded.

Start with the Problem, Not the Product

Every product strategy starts in one of two places: with a problem someone has, or with an idea you love. Both feel identical from inside. Only one of them leads to money.

I know this distinction the expensive way. The recipe app Sam and I started at twenty-two was founded on the sentence "people should cook more adventurously," which is an opinion about how other people should live, dressed up as a problem, and we spent our whole run trying to market our way around the fact that nobody had it. Chapter five performs the full autopsy on that wreck. The strategy lesson is the only part we need here: we started from an idea we loved instead of a pain someone felt, and everything downstream of that choice was already lost.

Compare the founding observation of the routing company, years later: dispatchers at delivery firms were spending two to three hours every single morning re-planning routes by hand, with laminated maps and grease pencils, and the good ones were quitting because the job was misery, and every hour of it was payroll going up in smoke. Notice the difference in texture. That sentence describes something already happening, already hurting, and already costing money somebody could count. When we showed up with software, the only convincing left to do was that we could make an existing pain stop. That is a sales conversation you can have before ten in the morning.

This is what people mean by problem-first thinking, and I want to rescue the idea from the jargon that usually buries it. It is very simple: the problem is the interesting part, and your product is just your current best answer to it. Hold the problem tightly and the product loosely, and you can change the answer as you learn without losing your footing. Hold it the other way around, product tightly and problem loosely, and every piece of bad news becomes a threat to your identity instead of information.

The four tests of a problem worth building on

Not every real problem can carry a business. Plenty of problems are genuine and still commercially useless: too rare, too mild, or felt by people with no budget. Over the years I have boiled my own filter down to four tests, and I apply them to every idea that gets pitched to me at a barbecue, which is where all ideas are pitched.

A problem that passes all four tests is rare and precious, and here is the uncomfortable corollary: it is worth more than your idea. Founders guard their ideas like state secrets and hand out their problem insight for free, and it should be exactly the other way around. Ideas are cheap and everywhere. A sharply understood problem, felt often, by reachable people, with money, is the actual asset. The routing company changed its product three times in the first four years. The problem never changed. That is what let us change everything else without dissolving.

Notice also what the four tests quietly forbid: building for a customer you cannot describe. "Everyone eats" was, I am ashamed to say, an actual sentence on an actual slide of the recipe app deck. Everyone eats, and no one in particular needed us. If the answer to "who has this problem" is a demographic the size of a continent, you have found a category of humans, and categories do not buy anything.

Story

Two years into the nine-bakery run, a kitchen-equipment salesman spent twenty minutes showing Jonas a machine that could laminate dough in "one third of the time." Jonas listened patiently, which is his version of a trap, and then asked one question: "Which of my problems does it solve?" The salesman, sensing victory, said it would free up hours of morning labour. Jonas nodded and said his mornings were the only part of the schedule that ran perfectly, that his actual problems were afternoon staffing and a landlord in Zwolle, and did the machine do anything about landlords in Zwolle? He tells this story as a joke about salesmen. I retell it as a lesson about founders, because most of us are that salesman to our own customers: deeply informed about our solution, incurious about their actual list of problems, answering a question nobody in the room asked. The customer always has a ranked list of pains, and your product is auditioning for a spot on that list.

Try this

Write down the problem you solve in one sentence, with a rule: you may not mention your product, your technology, or any feature. Only the customer, the situation, and what it costs them. "Dispatchers lose three hours every morning re-planning routes by hand" passes. "People need an easier way to use our platform" fails twice in one sentence. Then read your sentence to three people who actually fit the description and count how many interrupt you to complain about the problem before you have finished. Interruptions are the highest form of validation. Polite nodding is a funeral.

To Whom: Choosing a Customer on Purpose

Once you can say the problem cleanly, the next choice is whose version of it you serve first. Note the wording. Not "who is our target audience," which invites you to describe a fog, but whose version, singular, first. Because the same problem wears different clothes on different people, and you cannot dress them all at once.

Take Jonas's croissants. The problem, if we are being honest about what a croissant solves, is something like: the morning is grim and short and I deserve one warm good thing that requires nothing from me. Office commuters have that problem at 7:20 on weekdays, in a hurry, alone, price-insensitive up to about a euro fifty. Families have it on Saturday at ten, unhurried, in groups, buying a bagful, and suddenly price-aware because the quantity is six. Same pastry, same oven, two entirely different businesses. Opening hours, staffing, counter layout, even where you put the shop: every operational choice serves one of those customers a little better and the other a little worse. Jonas chose the commuter, deliberately, and let the Saturday families be a pleasant bonus rather than a design constraint. When he later scouted locations for bakeries two through nine, he did not look for nice neighbourhoods. He counted foot traffic between 7 and 9 a.m. That is what choosing a customer looks like: it shows up in the boring decisions.

Software founders, who like to believe their trade is more sophisticated than pastry, make the same choice or fail to. At the routing company we spent an embarrassing first year selling to "delivery fleets," which in practice meant anyone with more than one van, which meant our product had to please a florist with three vans and a parcel network with three hundred, which meant it pleased neither. The florist found it bloated. The network found it flimsy. The day we chose, and we chose fleets of ten to fifty vehicles, regional, owner-managed, the whole company exhaled. The product roadmap got shorter. The demo got sharper. Even our pricing page stopped apologizing. Half our existing pipeline no longer fit the description, and letting those deals go felt like setting fire to money. It was the single most profitable thing we did that year.

While we are choosing customers, one distinction that saves B2B founders years of confusion: the buyer and the user are often different people, and you must choose to understand both, because they care about different things and only one of them signs. At the routing company, the user was the dispatcher, and the dispatcher cared about his morning: fewer screens, fewer surprises, software that did not humiliate him in front of drivers who had been waiting since 7:15. The buyer was the owner, and the owner cared about diesel, payroll, and whether the whole operation still functioned when the dispatcher took his first holiday since 2011. Our early demos were built entirely for owners, all savings charts and dashboards, and we could not understand why deals kept dying after the trial. They died because the dispatcher, whom we had treated as furniture, quietly told the owner the thing was a headache, and no owner on earth overrules the person who gets the vans out. We learned to win the dispatcher first and give the owner the arithmetic second, and the trials started converting. Bakers get a version of this too: the commuter buys the croissant, but the office manager who orders forty for a Friday meeting is a different customer with a different problem, invoicing and reliability rather than warmth and queue speed, and deserves a deliberate decision about whether you serve her, rather than an accidental one. Whoever your equivalent pair is, write both of them down, and be honest about which one your product is currently flirting with and which one it is ignoring.

The beachhead, or why small is not a compromise

There is a military metaphor that has survived in business language because it is genuinely useful: the beachhead. You do not invade a continent along its entire coastline. You take one beach, hold it, and expand from strength. In product strategy, the beachhead is the narrow customer group you can win decisively, even if it looks almost comically small from outside.

Founders resist narrowing because it feels like shrinking their dream. It is the opposite. The dream is the continent, and it is still there. Narrowing is just refusing to fight everywhere at once with three people and a borrowed boat. There is a practical magic to a well-chosen beachhead that spreadsheets never capture: the customers know each other. Regional fleet owners drink coffee at the same industry days. Bakery commuters literally stand in a queue together, which is the highest-density referral environment ever devised. When your first fifty customers can gossip about you to your next fifty, marketing becomes a rumour you started once. When they are scattered across unrelated worlds, every single customer has to be won from a standing start, forever.

How narrow is narrow enough? My working test: you should be able to name ten real, specific prospects tonight, without opening a laptop. Actual companies, actual shops, an actual corner of an actual city. If you can only describe your customer statistically, you have a segment. When you can list them by name, you have a beachhead. And one warning from a man who has drawn this wrong on a whiteboard: pick the beachhead you understand, not the one that impresses. The customer whose Tuesdays you can describe in detail is worth three customer groups you admire from a distance. Sam and I built for imaginary adventurous cooks we had never met, while both of our mothers, who cooked from the same fifteen recipes on a weekly rotation and were perfectly happy, sat across from us at dinner being a real market we found too boring to see.

Why They Care: The Value Proposition

So you know the problem and you have chosen whose version of it you serve. Now comes the question that sounds soft and is actually the hinge of the whole strategy: why does this customer care about your answer? Not why should they care, which is your opinion again in a different coat. Why do they, in their world, with their alternatives, with their ranked list of pains you learned about two sections ago.

The lanyard word for this is "value proposition," and I once used it on Jonas, at the second bakery, while he was decorating the one surviving celebration cake. He did not look up. "You mean why people cross the street for you," he said. I have never found a better translation, so that is the one this book uses. Your value proposition is why people cross the street for you, when staying on their own side of the street is free and requires no decision at all.

The crossing-the-street frame does something useful that the textbook frame does not: it puts effort and alternatives into the picture from the start. A customer who buys from you is not choosing you versus nothing. They are choosing you versus whatever they do today, and whatever they do today has one crushing advantage over you: it already exists, it is already understood, and switching away from it costs attention, which is the scarcest thing your customer owns. The dispatcher's laminated map was slow and miserable, but it never crashed, never needed a password, and never made him feel stupid in front of the drivers. We lost early deals to that map. You will lose deals to your customer's version of it, and every one of those losses is your value proposition telling you it is not yet strong enough to pay for the walk across the street.

Here is the discipline I recommend, and it fits on one sheet of paper divided down the middle. On the left side, the customer's world, written in their words: the job they are trying to get done, the pains that make it miserable, and the gains they would honestly like, ranked the way they rank them, not the way you wish they did. On the right side, your offer: what it is, which pains it kills, which gains it delivers. Then look at the overlap, because the overlap is the only part anybody will pay for. Everything on the right that maps to nothing on the left is cost you are carrying for your own amusement. Everything big on the left that maps to nothing on the right is either your next move or your honest reason to walk away.

Two habits make this sheet honest instead of decorative. First, fill in the left side from evidence: things customers actually said, did, or paid for, with their phrasing, not yours. If the left column is written in your vocabulary, you interviewed yourself. Second, redo the sheet twice a year, because both sides move. Customers' pains shift, alternatives improve, and your own offer accumulates barnacles. That "unmapped extras" corner in the diagram, the features connected to nothing, is not a hypothetical. At the routing company we once counted seventeen report types in the product. Customers used four. The other thirteen were the fossil record of every anxious yes we had ever said, and each one still cost maintenance, testing, and explanation. We will return to how those barnacles get aboard, and how to scrape them off, in the saying-no section, where I have prepared a confession.

A word on the emotional layer, because founders of practical products love to skip it. People cross the street for feelings at least as often as for arithmetic. The commuter buying Jonas's croissant is not optimizing calories per euro. She is buying ninety seconds of warmth in a cold morning. The fleet owner who bought our software was saving kilometres, yes, but what closed the deal, more than once, was some version of: I want to stop being the only person who can plan the routes, so I can take a holiday for the first time in six years. If your value proposition has no feeling in it anywhere, you have probably described your product's behaviour and missed its meaning. Both layers are real. Price against both.

Testing the overlap before you bet the year on it

A value proposition on paper is a hypothesis wearing a suit, and the good news is that hypotheses can be tested for almost nothing, long before you commit the year. The cheapest test is the pitch-back: explain the offer to a real prospect in two sentences and then shut up. If they finish the thought, ask what it costs, or start describing their own situation back to you in detail, the overlap is real. If they say "interesting," you have learned the polite Dutch word for no, which exists in every language. The next test up costs slightly more: ask for a small commitment that is not money but is not nothing. A signed letter of intent, a booked pilot slot, an hour of their dispatcher's time, their place on a waiting list with their actual phone number. People spend compliments freely and commitments carefully, and the gap between the two is the most accurate market research you will ever collect.

Jonas ran his version of this with a tray. Before he committed a whole oven schedule to the almond croissant, he baked forty on a Thursday, put them by the register at full price with a hand-written card, and counted two things: how many sold by 8:30, and how many people asked for one on Friday when the tray was not there. The Friday question, he says, is the whole test. Anyone will buy what is in front of them. The customers who come back and ask for the thing that is missing are the ones telling you the overlap exists. Sam and I, by painful contrast, tested the recipe app by asking friends whether they liked it, a method that measures friendship with perfect accuracy and demand not at all. Whatever your trade, there is a forty-croissant version of your next big bet, and the discipline of running it first, at full price, with a way for the no to reach you, will save you more money than any other habit in this chapter.

Why You: Positioning, or the Corner You Can Defend

There is one more question a customer asks before crossing the street, usually silently: why you, and not any of the other people offering to solve this? Your answer to that question is your positioning, and I want to keep this section honest and short, because positioning is the most over-mystified idea in commercial life. It is not magic. It is picking the corner of the market you can actually defend, and then arranging everything you do to make that corner unmistakably yours.

The first honest step is listing your real alternatives, and I choose the word "alternatives" over "competitors" deliberately. Your competitor list is companies that resemble you. Your alternatives list is whatever the customer would actually do instead of buying from you, and it is always longer and stranger. Jonas's croissant competes with the supermarket's plastic-wrapped six-pack, yes, but its most dangerous alternative is skipping breakfast entirely. The routing company's deadliest rival was never the other software vendors we obsessed over in year one. It was the laminated map plus Piet, the veteran dispatcher who had the whole region in his head. You position against what the customer would really do, not against the companies you would like to be photographed next to.

Then you choose your corner, and the choice usually comes down to being cheapest, being best on some dimension the customer already cares about, or being the only. Cheapest is a real position and almost certainly not yours: it belongs to whoever has the deepest pockets and the most brutal operations, and it can only be held by one player at a time. Best is defensible when the dimension is specific: fastest to implement, gentlest learning curve, best for exactly this trade. "Best quality" in general is not a position, it is a compliment you pay yourself. The strongest corners, especially for small firms, are the only-statements: the only route planner built for regional fleets of ten to fifty vans, the only bakery on the station square with a queue that moves fast enough to risk at 7:40. When you can complete the sentence "the only ___ for ___ that ___" without lying, everything downstream, from your homepage to your hiring, gets easier to write.

Notice that a good only-statement is just your earlier choices, compressed. The customer you chose, the problem you chose, the overlap you chose: positioning is where they harden into a claim. That is why you cannot outsource it and why it cannot be produced in a branding workshop, whatever the agency deck promises. How that claim gets dressed, the name, the look, the voice, what people say about you when you leave the room, is chapter eight's territory, and I will leave it there. Here, only the skeleton matters: a corner, chosen because you can defend it, stated plainly enough that a stranger could repeat it after hearing it once. If your positioning cannot survive being repeated by a stranger, it is not positioning. It is wallpaper.

And a warning about the most common way founders wreck this: positioning by fear. You will feel a constant gravitational pull toward the middle of the market, because the middle looks safe, and every narrowing looks like locked doors. Resist it with arithmetic. You need some number of customers to thrive, and that number is almost certainly small: a few hundred fleets, a few thousand daily croissants. The question is never "how many people does this position exclude." It is "does the corner contain enough people to feed us, and can we win them decisively there." A corner that feeds you, held firmly, beats a boulevard where you are one of forty stalls, every time I have watched it play out.

The Strategy One-Pager

By now we have collected a stack of choices: problem, customer, value, position, and, coming shortly, price and the no-list. The stack needs to live somewhere, and I am going to insist it lives on one page. Not because brevity is fashionable, but because one page is the largest strategy that gets used. Anything longer becomes a ceremonial object: written once, admired twice, opened never. The one-pager, in contrast, can be taped inside a cabinet door, which is, as established, where real strategy lives.

A few notes on using this table, from watching it succeed and fail in the wild. Fill it in pencil, out loud, with whoever shares the decisions, because the arguments it starts are the entire point. Every line you cannot fill is not a formatting problem, it is a decision you have been dodging, now conveniently labelled. The "today's alternative" line is the one founders most want to skip, and the one that most often saves them, because writing "skipping breakfast" or "Piet and the laminated map" next to your offer keeps you humble about what you are really up against.

The last line, the riskiest assumption, is the one-pager's fuse. Every strategy is a bet, and every bet rests on one belief more load-bearing than the rest. Naming it does two things: it tells you what to test first and cheapest, and it tells you, in advance and in writing, what evidence would mean the strategy is wrong. How you track that assumption over time, and what a kill criterion looks like when you honour it, is chapter seven's machinery, and this one-pager is designed to slot straight into the living plan described there. For now it is enough that the fuse is visible. Jonas's was visible from day one, which is why he tested the €1.45 price in week two instead of wondering about it for a year.

And keep the page dated. Strategy is a living choice, and you will revise this sheet, ideally on purpose. A drawer full of dated one-pagers becomes, over the years, the most honest history of your company anyone will ever write.

Pricing Without Apologizing

We have arrived at the part of strategy that makes grown founders sweat through their shirts. I have watched people who negotiate office leases without blinking turn pale at the question "so what does it cost?" Pricing feels personal in a way the other choices do not, because a price is the one line of your strategy the customer gets to argue with, out loud, to your face. Everything else on the one-pager they simply believe or they don't. The price, they negotiate. So let us take it slowly and take the shame out of it.

First, the reframe that helped me most: your price is information before it is income. A price tells the customer what kind of thing they are buying and what kind of company is selling it. Jonas's €1.45 croissant is not just margin arithmetic, it is a message: this is a small daily luxury, made by people who take it seriously, and it will be exactly this good every day. Price the same croissant at sixty cents and the message becomes: this is fuel, expect fuel. Price it at four euros and the message becomes: this is an occasion, do not come daily. None of those messages is wrong. But only one of them matches the commuter on the one-pager, which is the point: price is not a number you pick at the end. It is a strategic choice that has to sit at the same table as all the others, agreeing with them.

Second, the mechanics. There are really only three places a price can come from, and every method you will ever read about is one of them wearing a costume. You can price from your costs, from the market around you, or from the value you create. Cost-plus asks "what does it cost me, plus a margin to live on." Market-based asks "what do the alternatives charge, and where do I sit relative to them." Value-based asks "what is the problem worth to this customer, and what share of that can I claim." They are not equally good, but they are all useful, because they answer different questions: cost-plus sets your floor, the market sets the customer's expectations, and value sets the ceiling. A sane price is set with all three in view, which is rarer than it sounds. Most founders compute the floor, glance nervously at the market, never look up at the ceiling, and then price slightly below the floor of their own confidence.

Before you pick the number, pick the unit, because what you charge per is a quieter decision than what you charge, and it shapes the business more. We charged per vehicle per month, and that single choice did half our selling for us: a fleet owner could hold it in his head, it grew automatically as his fleet grew, and it made the value comparison trivial, forty euros against one vehicle's monthly diesel waste. Had we charged per user, the same software would have punished the customer for letting more staff use it, which is the opposite of what we wanted. Had we charged per route calculated, dispatchers would have rationed the recalculations, hoarding exactly the behaviour that made the product valuable. The wrong unit installs a small perverse incentive at the heart of the relationship and then compounds it monthly. The test for a good unit is that it scales with the value the customer receives, is effortless to measure without arguments, and never punishes them for using the product more. Agencies, note that "per hour" fails this test spectacularly: it prices your inputs instead of the client's outcome, caps your income at the size of your calendar, and puts you and the client on opposite sides of every efficiency you ever gain. Every year you get faster at the work, an hourly price quietly cuts your pay for improving. Per project, per month, or per named outcome all beat it, and the productized service from the next section is largely a machine for escaping the hourly trap.

Story

The routing company's first real customer taught me pricing in one sentence, and he did not know he was doing it. We had agonized for weeks over our price, built a cost model, added a margin we considered ambitious, and arrived at 40 euros per vehicle per month. I drove to his office rehearsing defences for a number nobody had attacked yet. When the moment came, I said the price with the steadiness of a man reading his own hostage statement. He signed the same afternoon. No questions. And in the car park afterwards, instead of celebrating, I sat with a strange flat feeling I could not name until weeks later, when his dispatcher mentioned, cheerfully, that the software had paid for itself within the first month on diesel savings alone. Within the month. We were charging a twentieth of the value we created, and our customer, a fair and decent man, had simply accepted the gift. Nobody will ever interrupt your pitch to tell you that you are too cheap. The silence where the wince should be is the only signal you get. We tripled the price for new customers the following quarter and our close rate did not move. I have retold that car park feeling to every founder who has ever asked me about pricing, and I can see in their faces which ones have already felt it.

The courage part, handled practically

Everyone's first price is too low. I say "everyone" because in twenty years I have met two exceptions, and one of them went bankrupt from arrogance, so the rule holds well enough. The reasons are emotional, not analytical: you know everything wrong with your product, you desperately need the yes, and the price feels like a judgement of you personally. All three feelings are real. None of them is information about what the customer should pay. The customer is not buying your self-esteem, they are buying a solved problem, and the problem does not get smaller because you feel like a beginner.

So handle courage the way you handle any weak muscle: with small structured exercise rather than willpower. Raise the price for the next new customer only, and watch what actually happens instead of what you feared. Practise saying the number out loud, alone, until it stops sounding like a question; customers hear the wobble long before they evaluate the digits. And decide your walk-away in advance, in writing, at a calm desk, because you will not decide it well in the meeting. If you struggle to hold the line live, in the room, that is a conversation skill, and chapter nine covers the pricing conversation itself, discounts, objections and all. Here I only need you to arrive at that conversation carrying a number you believe.

The discount ratchet

A discount given once, to close one deal in one hard December, has a way of becoming your actual price. The customer renews at it, mentions it at the industry day, and their neighbour opens negotiations there. Ratchets turn one way. If you must flex, trade instead of discounting: a lower price for a longer commitment, a case study, a reference call, payment up front. Price cuts that buy nothing are not sales tactics, they are strategy leaks, and the leak is coming out of the only line on the one-pager that pays for the other eight.

One more pricing habit that separates the durable companies from the tired ones: revisit prices on a schedule, not in a crisis. Once a year, put the number on the table and re-run the three questions: floor, market, ceiling. Costs drift up. Value grows as the product improves. A price that was brave in 2021 is an apology by 2026. Jonas reprices every January, announces it on a small card by the register with no euphemisms, "prices go up on the 15th, thank you for a good year," and loses, by his count, almost nobody. Customers forgive a fair price stated plainly far more easily than founders believe. What they do not forgive, and what your good customers quietly resent on your behalf, is watching a business they like slowly starve itself out of politeness.

The Painful Art of Saying No to Features

Now the confession I promised, and the section this whole chapter has been walking toward. Everything so far, the problem, the customer, the overlap, the corner, the price, can be undone by a single small word said too often, and the word is yes.

Here is how it happens, and I am describing the routing company because I was holding the pen. A good customer, a real one, paying and friendly, asks for a small thing. A report, an export, a checkbox. The thing is genuinely useful to them, only mildly related to your strategy, and refusing feels absurd: it is two days of work and this customer pays you thousands. So you say yes, and you are right to, arguably, that one time. But the yes does not stay one time. The next customer asks for their small thing, and you have precedent now. Eighteen months later you have seventeen report types, a settings page like an airplane cockpit, and a product that takes three sessions to demo. No single yes was wrong. The sum was a catastrophe of kindness. Your product no longer says anything, because it is too busy saying everything.

And the cost is so much larger than the build time, which is the part nobody prices honestly. Every feature is a promise plus a chore, to borrow the pink-slip wisdom: it must be maintained forever, tested forever, documented forever, explained to every new employee and every new customer forever. It widens the surface where things break. It makes the next feature slower to build. Worst of all, it blurs. A product defined by one sharp overlap becomes a product defined by its longest-tenured customers' accumulated preferences, and to every new prospect it looks like what it is: a hallway of other people's coats.

Anyone can add. Addition is enthusiasm. A strategy is the list of things you refuse to build, written down while you still mean it.
Story

Jonas's version of this lesson has a wedding dress in it. In year three, a bride-to-be begged him to make her wedding cake, and because the margin on a wedding cake makes a croissant look like charity, he said yes. The cake was a triumph. Word got around, as word does, and within a season he had a sideline: eleven wedding cakes a summer, each one profitable on paper, each one requiring consultations on Sunday afternoons, a delivery van with special suspension in his brother's borrowed trailer, and, crucially, four hours of his best baker's week, the same baker who kept the croissant line running. Then came the Saturday when a cake collapsed in July heat on a farm road outside town, and Jonas spent the morning of his busiest day rebuilding tiers of sponge in a barn while, back at the shop, the queue at 7:40 stretched past the point where commuters risk it, and forty of them peeled off, and some meaningful number of them, he still maintains, never came back. He sat down that Sunday with the numbers and found that his most profitable product per unit was quietly bleeding his most profitable system. The wedding cakes were a better product. The croissant line was a better strategy. He killed the sideline that week, and "no wedding cakes" has been written on the one-pager of all nine bakeries since, where it functions less as a rule about cakes and more as a vaccine: the standing reminder that revenue which fights your strategy is just expensive noise. Gerrit, for the record, thought the wedding cakes were a great idea.

A sieve, so no does not depend on your mood

The problem with relying on discipline is that requests arrive one at a time, each reasonable, each attached to a human you like. You need a sieve that works when you are tired, and mine has three gates, applied in order, taped where the pink slips would be.

Gate one filters by customer: is this for the customer on the one-pager, or for "a customer," which is a different thing wearing the same face? The florist with three vans had real needs. They were not our needs to serve, and every hour spent on them was taken, invisibly, from the fleet owner we had chosen. Gate two filters by overlap: does this kill a pain from the left side of the fit diagram, one the customer actually ranked, or does it merely make the product larger? Gate three, the one everyone skips, prices the forever cost: not the build, the decade. A feature that takes two days to build and four hours a year to maintain, test, explain and apologize for is a forty-hour feature by year ten, and you are buying it with your future company's time.

And notice what the diagram insists on at the bottom: surviving the sieve earns a request a place in the queue, not a promise. The queue is ranked, the queue is short, and things fall off the end of it without ceremony. That is not rudeness. That is the strategy, breathing.

How to say no without losing the customer

The fear underneath every bad yes is that no ends the relationship, so let me report from the field: it almost never does, if the no is done well. A good no has three parts. First, gratitude that is real, because a customer who asks for features is a customer who imagines a future with you, and that is worth genuine thanks. Second, the reason, stated in terms of your strategy, not your capacity: "we're keeping the product ruthlessly focused on getting routes planned fast" lands entirely differently from "we don't have time," because the first says you have a spine and the second says they should ask again in March. Third, honesty about the future: log the request where they can believe it exists, and if the answer is never, say never, kindly. "That's a genuinely good idea for a different product than the one we make" is a sentence that has earned me respect, referrals, and exactly one storming exit in a decade of using it.

What you will discover, and I need you to be ready for it, is that customers respect a clean no far more than a mushy yes. The mushy yes, the "it's on the roadmap" said to end a meeting, is the real relationship killer, because it converts a moment of mild disappointment into a slow leak of trust, drip by quarterly drip. Customers talk to each other, and the thing you want them saying is the thing they said about Jonas: he won't do it, he'll tell you straight, and the croissants are always, always right.

Try this

Tonight, write your no-list: five things your business will not do, specific enough to sting. Not "we won't compromise on quality," which refuses nothing, but "no custom features for single customers," "no clients below X," "no wedding cakes." For each one, write the name of the person most likely to make you break it. If you cannot fill the second column, your list is too safe to be load-bearing. Then put the list where the requests arrive: by the phone, in the inbox template, taped inside the cabinet door.

Selling a Service Is Still Selling a Product

A short section now for half of my readers, who have been translating on the fly this whole chapter: the consultants, agencies, trainers, accountants, installers, and everyone else who sells hours and expertise rather than croissants and software. You may suspect this chapter applies to you only by analogy. It applies to you directly, and in one respect it applies to you more.

Service businesses drift into strategy-lessness more easily than product businesses, because a service can become anything the client asks for, one engagement at a time. There is no oven schedule to force the reckoning. Each bespoke project is a small yes, and after a few years the firm is eleven people doing forty things for anyone with a budget, competing on availability and price against everyone, positioned as "the ones who are nice and pick up the phone." I have watched agencies reach a million in revenue without ever once deciding what they sell. Revenue hides the problem. Exhaustion reveals it.

The remedy is the same one-pager with the same lines, plus one discipline particular to services: productize something. Take the engagement you do best, give it a name, a fixed scope, a fixed price, and a described outcome, and put it on the menu like a dish. "The two-week routing audit, €7,500, ends with a written plan" is a product in every way that matters: it can be positioned, priced on value, refused when out of scope, improved each time it runs, and sold by someone other than the founder, that last property being the difference between owning a firm and owning a job. You do not have to productize everything, and the bespoke work can stay, at bespoke prices. But a service firm with no named products has no croissant line, nothing that gets better every single day it is made, and it will feel the difference every January when the pipeline resets to zero and the year must be invented again from a standing start.

The no-list, meanwhile, matters more for services, not less, because your inventory is your people's weeks, and it is perishable. A product company that says a bad yes loses some build time. A service firm that says a bad yes has sold the actual finite substance the firm is made of, at a discount, to the wrong buyer. Scope, in a service business, is not an administrative detail. Scope is the product strategy, enforced one engagement at a time.

When the Strategy Should Change

Everything in this chapter has argued for choosing firmly, so I owe you the other half of the truth before we close: a product strategy is a living choice, and there are moments when changing it is the strategic act and holding it is the cowardice. The skill is telling those moments apart from ordinary weather, because the two feel identical at 2 a.m.

Here is the distinction that has served me best. Noise is a lost deal, a loud customer, a competitor's launch, a slow month, a conference that made you feel behind. Signal is a pattern with a date on it: the riskiest assumption on your one-pager failing its test in the real world. The recipe app's assumption, had we ever written it down, was "a meaningful group of people wants to cook more adventurously and will change apps to do it," and the evidence against it arrived within weeks and kept arriving for eighteen months while we called it a marketing problem. Meanwhile the routing company changed products three times on signal, each time because something written on the page had been disproven by paying customers, and each change felt terrifying and was, in hindsight, barely a risk at all, because the problem and the customer held still while the answer moved. Hold the problem tightly, the product loosely: the same rule from the start of this chapter, now doing its second job as your pivot compass.

Practically: reopen the one-pager on a schedule, twice a year, at a calm desk, and additionally whenever the riskiest assumption takes a documented hit. Change it deliberately, out loud, in writing, with a new date, so the drawer of old one-pagers stays honest. What you must not do is let the strategy be renegotiated ambiently, one exception at a time, in the space between decisions, which is how most strategies actually die: not killed, but nibbled. The mechanics of tracking assumptions, kill criteria, and the living plan get their full treatment in chapter seven, and I will see you there. For now, the whole discipline fits in one sentence Jonas gave me for free, at 4:30 in the morning, holding a fistful of pink slips: decide what you sell, decide it on purpose, and make the exceptions ask permission.

Field note

If reading this chapter has given you the queasy feeling that your current product list is Theo's, a hundred and forty items accumulated rather than chosen, do not schedule a strategy offsite. Spend six weeks with pink slips first: units, price, direct cost, hand minutes, per product or per service line. The kill list writes itself once the slips exist. The offsite without the slips produces a poster.

The Short Version

  • A product strategy is six choices that fit together: what you sell, to whom, why they care, why you, at what price, and what you refuse to do. If nothing was refused, it is an appetite, not a strategy.
  • Start from a problem, not an idea you love. A problem worth building on is frequent, painful, budgeted, and reachable. Hold the problem tightly and the product loosely.
  • Choose one customer whose version of the problem you serve first, narrow enough that you can name ten real prospects tonight. The beachhead is not a compromise, it is where customers gossip about you to each other.
  • Your value proposition is why people cross the street for you. Map their world against your offer and remember: they pay for the overlap and only the overlap. Feelings count as value. Price against both layers.
  • Position in the corner you can defend: finish "the only ___ for ___ that ___" without lying, and let chapter eight worry about dressing it up.
  • Keep the whole strategy on one dated page, taped where the work happens. Every line you cannot fill is a decision you are dodging, now labelled.
  • Your first price is too low. Nobody will ever tell you that; the missing wince is the only signal. Set the floor from costs, expectations from the market, the ceiling from value, and reprice yearly on purpose, never in a crisis.
  • Discounts ratchet one way. Trade instead: longer commitment, reference, payment up front. Never cut price and get nothing.
  • Every feature and every engagement is a promise plus a chore, priced at its forever cost. Run requests through the sieve, keep the queue short, and deliver the clean no: customers trust it more than the mushy yes.
  • Change strategy on signal, never on noise: reopen the page on a schedule, pivot when the riskiest assumption fails in writing, and make the exceptions ask permission.
