Chapter 5

Getting the Founding Team Together

Choosing a cofounder is the biggest decision you will make before you have any way of knowing it is a decision, and this chapter is about making it with your eyes open anyway.

The last full sentence Sam said to me before the silence started was "You can keep the domain."

We were standing in the parking lot of the coworking space we could no longer afford, on a Tuesday in November, next to his mother's car, which he had borrowed because his own had a broken starter motor that neither of us could pay to fix. Eighteen months earlier we had stood in almost exactly the same spot, having just signed the desk contract, and taken a photo of ourselves pointing at the building like it was a stadium we were about to fill. Now the recipe app was dead, the bank account held 212 euros, and my best friend since we were fifteen was handing me a cardboard box with a router in it and telling me I could keep the domain.

I did keep the domain, out of some combination of grief and administrative laziness. It renewed automatically for six years. Every March an invoice for eleven euros arrived like a small annual funeral notice, and every March I paid it without letting myself think about why.

Here is the thing I want you to sit with, because the whole chapter grows out of it: nothing that killed the recipe app was in the app. The code was fine. The idea was defensible. The market existed and, annoyingly, other people later served it profitably. What killed the company was that Sam and I had spent roughly four minutes, total, across eighteen months, discussing how the two of us would actually work together. We had discussed the product for hundreds of hours. We had discussed the logo for, I am ashamed to report, eleven days. The partnership itself, the machine that all the other decisions would come out of, we never discussed at all. We just assumed it, the way you assume a bridge will hold because you have walked across it before, without checking what it was built to carry.

A friendship is built to carry dinners, holidays, bad breakups, and helping each other move apartments. It is not automatically built to carry payroll. This chapter is about doing the engineering check before you drive the truck onto the bridge.

The Recipe App Postmortem, Told Properly for the First Time

I have mentioned the recipe app before, always at a safe distance, the way you mention a scar through a shirt. Time to take the shirt off, because you will get more out of the anatomy than out of the summary.

Sam and I met at school when we were fifteen, bonded over the fact that we were both terrible at football in complementary ways, and stayed close through everything after. At twenty-two, I had a few years of building websites for local shops behind me and a conviction, common at that age, that I had therefore learned business. Sam had a marketing degree, real charisma, and an ability to make any plan sound inevitable by the second beer. One night he described an app where home cooks would share recipes and, crucially, the shopping lists would generate themselves and connect to local supermarkets. I still think it was a good idea. I said so. He said "so let's build it." I said yes before he finished the sentence.

Notice what happened there. The biggest professional commitment of my life to that point, a commitment that would consume eighteen months, all of my savings and a chunk of his, was made in the time it takes to nod. We spent more diligence that same month choosing a laptop.

The first weeks were glorious, and I want to be honest about that, because the glory is exactly what makes this phase dangerous. Working with your best friend on your own idea is one of the best feelings available to a human being. Every session felt like the montage part of a film. We whiteboarded, we named features, we argued about typography with the intensity of men defusing a bomb. We paid a freelancer three thousand euros, a fortune to us, for a logo and a brand book, and the brand book genuinely was beautiful. We had, as I have admitted before, a logo before we had a user and a growth model before we had a recipe. The growth model was a spreadsheet with a compounding function in it, and if you have never watched two twenty-two-year-olds discover the compounding function, it is like watching kittens discover a mirror.

What we did not have, ever, at any point, was an answer to any of the following questions. Who is the CEO? Who decides when we disagree and the argument has gone on for a week? How many hours a week are we each putting in, and what happens when one of us has a girlfriend and the other does not? Whose money is this we are spending, in what proportion, and what does the person who put in more get for it? What happens if one of us wants out? What happens if one of us wants to pivot and the other does not? What is this company for, in each of our actual private lives, the ones we did not say out loud?

Every single one of those questions eventually got answered. That is the sinister part. Unasked questions do not stay unanswered, they get answered by events, at the worst possible moment, with interest. The hours question got answered in month seven, when I noticed I was committing code at midnight while Sam was at a festival, and instead of raising it I started keeping score. The money question got answered in month ten, when my savings ran out before his and the imbalance sat at the table with us every day, invisible and enormous. The decision question got answered in month fourteen, when Sam wanted to pivot the whole thing toward meal planning for gyms, because a gym-owner friend of his was enthusiastic, and I wanted to stay consumer, and we discovered that our company had no mechanism for resolving a disagreement between two people who each owned exactly half of it. We argued for six weeks. Users we did not have were not acquired at a tremendous rate while we did.

The end, when it came, was almost comically small. We had about two thousand euros left. I wanted to spend it on a stand at a food fair in Utrecht. Sam wanted to spend it on Instagram ads for the gym concept. The fight we had about that decision lasted four hours and was not about the two thousand euros, it was about all eighteen months at once, every kept score, every swallowed resentment, every midnight commit and every festival, arriving together like a delegation. Somebody said the sentence you cannot unsay. It might have been me. Honestly, it was probably me. Two weeks later we were in the parking lot with the router and the cardboard box.

We did not speak for a while after that. I have called it "a few tense weeks" in polite company, and it was weeks, but they were the kind of weeks that have a taste. What brought us back, and what eventually turned into this chapter, I will save for the end, because it belongs there. For now I want to hold the corpse up to the light, because the recipe app died of the five specific diseases this chapter exists to vaccinate you against.

First, we chose each other by friendship instead of by evidence. Second, we never had the prenup conversation, the explicit talk about money, power, hours and endings, while we still liked each other enough to have it safely. Third, we split the equity 50/50 in four minutes because it felt polite, and built ourselves a deadlock machine. Fourth, we had no defined roles, so we both did the fun parts twice and the boring parts never. Fifth, we put nothing on paper, so when it ended there was no process, only feelings, and feelings are a terrible liquidator.

Five diseases. Five vaccines. That is the chapter. Let us go get the injections, starting with a question that comes even before "who": whether.

Do You Need a Cofounder at All?

The startup world has a dogma about this. Accelerators famously prefer teams. The folklore says solo founders fail more, burn out more, and get funded less, and there is some truth in each claim. But I have watched the dogma push people into cofounder marriages of convenience, and I can report that the only thing worse than being a solo founder is being co-founders with someone you recruited because a blog post told you to. So let me try to give you the honest version instead of the folklore.

Here is what a cofounder is actually for. Not the pitch-deck answer, the real one.

A cofounder halves the loneliness. This sounds soft until you have lived the alternative. Building a company involves making decisions all day that you cannot discuss with employees, because they work for you, or with your spouse, because they have heard enough, or with your friends, because they stopped understanding your life around month six. A cofounder is the one other person on earth inside the same weather.

A cofounder covers your blind side. Every founder is lopsided. I am a builder who finds selling vaguely embarrassing. Others are sellers who think the product is basically done once the demo works. A real cofounder is strong precisely where you are weak, which we will map properly a few sections from now.

A cofounder keeps you honest. When you are alone, your bad ideas go unchallenged and your good ideas go uncelebrated, and over time you lose the ability to tell them apart. The daily friction of another committed brain is quality control you cannot hire.

And a cofounder shares the load-bearing risk. An employee, even a brilliant early one, can leave with two months' notice and a card signed by the office. A cofounder is roped to you on the mountain. That changes what they will carry.

Now here is what a cofounder costs, and the folklore mumbles this part. You will give away a large piece of the company, which is fine, and a large piece of the decision-making, which is only fine if the decisions get better. You acquire a relationship that needs active maintenance, forever, on top of the company that needs active maintenance forever. And you take on the single most common catastrophic risk in early companies: cofounder conflict kills more young ventures than competition does. Nobody's market destroyed them as efficiently as Sam and I destroyed each other's motivation.

Story

Priya went the other way, and I want you to hear her version, because she is the most capable solo founder I know and she would still not recommend it unreservedly. She started her B2B software company alone, partly by temperament and partly by accident: the colleague who was going to join her got a counteroffer he could not refuse, and rather than search for a stranger she just started. "Every article said find a cofounder," she told me once, over a dinner where I was supposed to be interviewing her about fundraising and we never got to it. "But they all described the cofounder like a feature list. Technical skills, complementary network, shared vision. Nobody wrote the real job description."

I asked her what the real job description was.

"Witness," she said. "I did not miss the help. Help you can hire. I hired brilliant help by year two. What I missed was a witness. Somebody who was there when we almost died in March and actually felt it. When we landed the first big client I stood in my kitchen with a glass of wine and told the news to a houseplant. My team celebrated, but they celebrated a good day at work. For me it was the day the previous four years stopped being a gamble, and there was no one on the planet for whom that sentence meant what it meant to me." She topped up my glass, which is what she does when she is about to close a topic. "I built a good company alone, so I am proof it can be done. But I made every hard decision at a table for one, and I am telling you, the table for one is where the doubt sits down across from you."

So, should you go alone? My honest scorecard, having done it both ways and watched dozens of others: go alone if you have a strong professional network you can lean on, a temperament that tolerates solitary doubt, enough breadth to cover selling and building at survival level yourself, and, ideally, a business that revenue can feed early so you can hire the help Priya hired. A bakery, an agency, a consultancy, these are honest solo businesses every day of the week. Go together if the business needs two disciplines at full strength from day one, if you know yourself well enough to know the loneliness will corrode you, and, this is the strict part, if you have a specific person about whom you can honestly say the things the rest of this chapter will demand. Do not go together because a blog post said teams get funded. A cofounder is not a checkbox on someone else's application form. And if what you actually need is skills rather than a partner, that is a hiring question, which is chapter ten's territory, not this one's.

Field note

If you are already solo and intend to stay that way, do not skip this chapter. Read the prenup questions with your future self as the counterparty, and read the equity section for the day you bring in a late cofounder or a key employee with shares, which is how most solo founders eventually meet this material, usually in a hurry.
