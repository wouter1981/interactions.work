Chapter 5

Getting the Founding Team Together

Choosing a cofounder is the biggest decision you will make before you have any way of knowing it is a decision, and this chapter is about making it with your eyes open anyway.

The last full sentence Sam said to me before the silence started was "You can keep the domain."

We were standing in the parking lot of the coworking space we could no longer afford, on a Tuesday in November, next to his mother's car, which he had borrowed because his own had a broken starter motor that neither of us could pay to fix. Eighteen months earlier we had stood in almost exactly the same spot, having just signed the desk contract, and taken a photo of ourselves pointing at the building like it was a stadium we were about to fill. Now the recipe app was dead, the bank account held 212 euros, and my best friend since we were fifteen was handing me a cardboard box with a router in it and telling me I could keep the domain.

I did keep the domain, out of some combination of grief and administrative laziness. It renewed automatically for six years. Every March an invoice for eleven euros arrived like a small annual funeral notice, and every March I paid it without letting myself think about why.

Here is the thing I want you to sit with, because the whole chapter grows out of it: nothing that killed the recipe app was in the app. The code was fine. The idea was defensible. The market existed; other people later made money serving it, which still stings. What killed the company was that Sam and I had spent roughly four minutes, total, across eighteen months, discussing how the two of us would work together. We had discussed the product for hundreds of hours. We had discussed the logo for, I am ashamed to report, eleven days. The partnership itself, the machine that all the other decisions would come out of, we never discussed at all. We just assumed it, the way you assume a bridge will hold because you have walked across it before, without checking what it was built to carry.

A friendship is built to carry dinners, holidays, bad breakups, and helping each other move apartments. Payroll is a different load class entirely. This chapter is about doing the engineering check before you drive the truck onto the bridge.

The Recipe App Postmortem, Told Properly for the First Time

I have mentioned the recipe app before, always at a safe distance, the way you mention a scar through a shirt. Time to take the shirt off, because you will get more out of the anatomy than out of the summary.

Sam and I met at school when we were fifteen, bonded over the fact that we were both terrible at football in complementary ways, and stayed close through everything after. At twenty-two, I had a few years of building websites for local shops behind me and a conviction, common at that age, that I had therefore learned business. Sam had a marketing degree, real charisma, and an ability to make any plan sound inevitable by the second beer. One night he described an app where home cooks would share recipes and the shopping lists would write themselves and connect to local supermarkets. I still think it was a good idea. I said so. He said "so let's build it." I said yes before he finished the sentence.

Notice what happened there. I made the biggest professional commitment of my life to that point, a commitment that would eat eighteen months, all of my savings and a chunk of his, in the time it takes to nod. That same month, we spent more diligence choosing a laptop.

The first weeks were glorious, and I want to be honest about that, because the glory is exactly what makes this phase dangerous. Working with your best friend on your own idea is one of the best feelings available to a human being. Every session felt like the montage part of a film. We whiteboarded, we named features, we argued about typography with the intensity of men defusing a bomb. We paid a freelancer three thousand euros, a fortune to us, for a logo and a brand book, and the brand book was beautiful, which somehow made everything worse. We had, as I have admitted before, a logo before we had a user and a growth model before we had a recipe. The growth model was a spreadsheet with a compounding function in it, and if you have never watched two twenty-two-year-olds discover the compounding function, it is like watching kittens discover a mirror.

What we did not have, ever, at any point, was an answer to any of the following questions. Who is the CEO? Who decides when we disagree and the argument has gone on for a week? How many hours a week are we each putting in, and what happens when one of us has a girlfriend and the other does not? Whose money is this we are spending, in what proportion, and what does the person who put in more get for it? What happens if one of us wants out? What happens if one of us wants to pivot and the other does not? What is this company for, in each of our actual private lives, the ones we did not say out loud?

Every single one of those questions eventually got answered. That is the sinister part. Ask nothing, and events will answer for you, at the worst possible moment, with interest. The hours question got answered in month seven, when I noticed I was committing code at midnight while Sam was at a festival, and instead of raising it I started keeping score. The money question got answered in month ten, when my savings ran out before his and the imbalance sat at the table with us every day, invisible and enormous. The decision question got answered in month fourteen, when Sam wanted to pivot the whole thing toward meal planning for gyms, because a gym-owner friend of his was enthusiastic, and I wanted to stay consumer, and we discovered that our company had no mechanism for resolving a disagreement between two people who each owned exactly half of it. We argued for six weeks. Our user count, meanwhile, held steady at zero.

The end, when it came, was almost comically small. We had about two thousand euros left. I wanted to spend it on a stand at a food fair in Utrecht. Sam wanted to spend it on Instagram ads for the gym concept. The fight lasted four hours, and underneath the two thousand euros it was about all eighteen months at once, every kept score, every swallowed resentment, every midnight commit and every festival, arriving together like a delegation. Somebody said the sentence you cannot unsay. It might have been me. Honestly, it was probably me. Two weeks later we were in the parking lot with the router and the cardboard box.

We did not speak for a while after that. I have called it "a few tense weeks" in polite company, and it was weeks, but they were the kind of weeks that have a taste. What brought us back, and what eventually turned into this chapter, I will save for the end, because it belongs there. For now I want to hold the corpse up to the light, because the recipe app died of the five specific diseases this chapter exists to vaccinate you against.

First, we chose each other by friendship instead of by evidence. Second, we skipped the prenup conversation, the explicit talk about money, power, hours and endings, while we still liked each other enough to have it safely. Third, we split the equity 50/50 in four minutes because it felt polite, and built ourselves a deadlock machine. Fourth, we had no defined roles, so we both did the fun parts twice and the boring parts never. Fifth, we put nothing on paper, so when it ended there was no process, only feelings, and feelings are a terrible liquidator.

Five diseases. Five vaccines. That is the chapter. Let us go get the injections, starting with a question that comes even before "who": whether.

Do You Need a Cofounder at All?

The startup world has a dogma about this. Accelerators prefer teams. The folklore says solo founders fail more, burn out more, and get funded less, and there is some truth in each claim. But I have watched the dogma push people into cofounder marriages of convenience, and I can report that the only thing worse than being a solo founder is being co-founders with someone you recruited because a blog post told you to. So let me give you the honest version instead of the folklore.

Here is what a cofounder is for. The real job description, the one that never makes it into a pitch deck.

A cofounder halves the loneliness. This sounds soft until you have lived the alternative. Building a company involves making decisions all day that you cannot discuss with employees, because they work for you, or with your spouse, because they have heard enough, or with your friends, because they stopped understanding your life around month six. A cofounder is the one other person on earth inside the same weather.

A cofounder covers your blind side. Every founder is lopsided. I am a builder who finds selling vaguely embarrassing. Others are sellers who think the product is basically done once the demo works. A real cofounder is strong precisely where you are weak, which we will map properly a few sections from now.

A cofounder keeps you honest. When you are alone, your bad ideas go unchallenged and your good ideas go uncelebrated, and over time you lose the ability to tell them apart. The daily friction of another committed brain is quality control you cannot hire.

And a cofounder shares the load-bearing risk. An employee, even a brilliant early one, can leave with two months' notice and a card signed by the office. A cofounder is roped to you on the mountain. That changes what they will carry.

Now here is what a cofounder costs, and the folklore mumbles this part. You will give away a large piece of the company, which is fine, and a large piece of the decision-making, which is only fine if the decisions get better. You acquire a relationship that needs active maintenance, forever, on top of the company that needs active maintenance forever. And you take on the single most common catastrophic risk in early companies: cofounder conflict kills more young ventures than competition does. No market ever destroyed a company as efficiently as Sam and I destroyed each other's motivation.

Story

Priya went the other way, and I want you to hear her version, because she is the most capable solo founder I know and even she recommends it with reservations. She started her B2B software company alone, partly by temperament and partly by accident: the colleague who was going to join her got a counteroffer he could not refuse, and rather than search for a stranger she just started. "Every article said find a cofounder," she told me once, over a dinner where I was supposed to be interviewing her about fundraising and we never got to it. "But they all described the cofounder like a feature list. Technical skills, complementary network, shared vision. Nobody wrote the real job description."

I asked her what the real job description was.

"Witness," she said. "The help I could hire, and by year two I had hired brilliant help. What I missed was a witness. Somebody who was there when we almost died in March and actually felt it. When we landed the first big client I stood in my kitchen with a glass of wine and told the news to a houseplant. My team celebrated, but they celebrated a good day at work. For me it was the day the previous four years stopped being a gamble, and there was no one on the planet for whom that sentence meant what it meant to me." She topped up my glass, which is what she does when she is about to close a topic. "I built a good company alone, so I am proof it can be done. But I made every hard decision at a table for one, and I am telling you, the table for one is where the doubt sits down across from you."

So, should you go alone? My honest scorecard, having done it both ways and watched dozens of others: go alone if you have a strong professional network you can lean on, a temperament that tolerates solitary doubt, enough breadth to cover selling and building at survival level yourself, and, ideally, a business that revenue can feed early so you can hire the help Priya hired. A bakery, an agency, a consultancy, these are honest solo businesses every day of the week. Go together if the business needs two disciplines at full strength from day one, if you know yourself well enough to know the loneliness will corrode you, and, this is the strict part, if you have a specific person about whom you can honestly say the things the rest of this chapter will demand. Never go together because a blog post said teams get funded. And if what you need is skills rather than a partner, that is a hiring question, which is chapter ten's territory, not this one's.

I took the solo road myself at the routing company, and Priya's dinner speech is the truest description of those years I have ever heard. What made them survivable was that I engineered the witness she was talking about. Three other founders and I kept a standing dinner, first Thursday of the month, no partners, no employees, phones in a pile by the bread. House rule: everyone brings one number they are proud of and one they are scared of. Over the years those dinners heard things my own management team never did. Add Henk, who reviewed my worst decisions with the enthusiasm of a man inspecting a used gearbox, and I had assembled maybe sixty percent of a cofounder. Sixty percent turned out to be enough. Build the bench before you need it, because you cannot recruit a witness in the middle of the thing they were supposed to witness.

Field note

If you are already solo and intend to stay that way, do not skip this chapter. Read the prenup questions with your future self as the counterparty, and read the equity section for the day you bring in a late cofounder or a key employee with shares, which is how most solo founders eventually meet this material, usually in a hurry.

Where Cofounders Come From, and Where Bad Ones Come From

Cofounders arrive from four places, and each place has a characteristic failure built into it, the way each breed of dog has a characteristic hip problem.

Friends. The most common source and the most dangerous, because you ran the selection with the wrong algorithm. You chose your friends for warmth, humor, loyalty, shared history. Not one of those attributes predicts how a person behaves when the bank balance is three weeks from zero. The trap is that friendship feels like evidence. It is evidence, but of the wrong proposition. Sam and I had fifteen years of proof that we could enjoy each other, and precisely zero hours of proof that we could disagree about money and both still show up the next morning. Those are different sports. Being excellent doubles partners at one tells you nothing about the other.

Colleagues. Statistically the best source, and it is not close. A former colleague comes with the exact evidence a friend lacks: you have seen them miss a deadline, absorb criticism, behave in a meeting that went wrong, handle a subordinate, handle being subordinate. You have seen their Tuesday. The characteristic failure here is subtler: former colleagues often reproduce the old company's hierarchy without discussing it. If she was your manager at the old firm, you will discover, around month four, that you have both quietly assumed she is the CEO, and if that assumption is wrong for the new company, unpicking it is delicate surgery.

Family. I will tread carefully, because family firms are the backbone of the European economy and plenty of them work beautifully. But a cofounder you cannot fire, cannot escape at Christmas, and cannot disappoint without disappointing your mother is a cofounder with extraordinary leverage over you, and you over them. If you do this, do it with more paperwork than you would use with a stranger. Everything this chapter tells you to write down, write down twice.

Strangers. Matchmaking events, founder platforms, a friend of a friend who "also wants to do something in logistics." This source produces the odd great pairing and a large number of polite disasters. The failure mode is speed: two people who both want a cofounder more than they want each other, contracting a marriage on the strength of three coffees. If you meet this way, you must earn everything that friends and colleagues get for free, which means the trial project I am about to describe is the whole courtship.

All this sourcing talk may sound like a software ritual. The pattern is older than software and shows up everywhere the stakes are shared. Two chefs who open a restaurant because they respected each other across a hot line for three years are colleagues-turned-founders, and their odds are good. The plumber and the electrician who form an installation firm because their fathers played cards together are friends-turned-founders, and their odds depend entirely on whether they do what this chapter says next. The pastry chef who partners with the investor cousin because the family pushed them together at a birthday, that is family sourcing with all its leverage, and I have watched that exact configuration produce both the best bakery in a town I will not name and, two towns over, a lawsuit that outlived the bakery. Same sources, same hip problems, whatever you sell.

The trial project, or dating before the wedding

Whatever the source, do not go from zero to incorporated in one conversation. Run a trial: a real project, with a deadline, a budget, stakes, and boredom in it. Four to eight weeks is enough. Build the prototype together. Run the market stall for a season. Deliver one client project end to end. You are running it for the evidence, so choose something that will force the interesting conditions: a moment of time pressure, a disagreement with actual consequences, a stretch of tedious work, ideally a small failure. You learn more from watching a person handle a botched delivery than from a year of coffees.

The best trial I ever witnessed was two women who wanted to start a catering firm and agreed to run a friend's wedding first, at cost. A wedding has everything you want in a trial: a hard deadline, a fixed budget, a client with feelings, and a long afternoon of peeling potatoes. Halfway through the evening the fridge van died. One of them rebuilt the dessert course from what the venue's kitchen held; the other kept the bride's mother laughing at the far end of the garden until it was solved. They incorporated a month later, and when I asked how they could be so sure so fast, the answer had nothing to do with food. "Now I know what she does when the fridge dies. And she knows what I do."

While it runs, watch for the signals that predict, not the ones that charm. Here is my field guide, assembled from my own scars and other people's dinners.

One more selection criterion that overrides all sourcing wisdom: commitment symmetry. A full-time founder paired with a keeping-my-job-for-now founder has recruited an employee with veto rights, whatever the shareholder register says. Asymmetric commitment can work briefly, with an explicit date by which it becomes symmetric, and with equity that reflects the asymmetry until then. Without those two clauses it is the second most common founding-team death I see, right after the fifty-fifty deadlock we will get to.

The Prenup Conversation

Now the heart of the chapter. Everything before this was choosing the person. This is the conversation you must have with the person, and I have named it deliberately. When two people marry with significant assets, sensible ones have an unromantic conversation and sign a document. They know the odds, and they know something sharper: if the marriage ever fails, the two people dividing the assets will no longer be the kind people currently sitting at the table. The company version of this conversation is more necessary, because business partnerships fail more often than marriages and dissolve with less social support. Nobody brings you lasagna when you lose your cofounder.

Yet almost nobody has the conversation. I have asked dozens of founding teams whether they discussed, before starting, what would happen if one of them wanted to leave. The honest answers cluster around 10 percent. The reason is always the same and always human: the conversation feels like an insult. You are sitting across from someone you like, at the most optimistic moment either of you will ever have about this venture, and raising the possibility of betrayal, laziness, divorce or death feels like spitting in the champagne. So both of you protect the mood. And in protecting the mood, you wire the bomb.

Let me reframe it, because the reframe is what finally got me through this material myself, years later, when the routing company's first key employees got shares and I sat down to have the talk from the other side of the table. The prenup conversation tests the partnership's conversational machinery. Every topic in it, money, power, effort, endings, will come up again, for real, under worse conditions. If the two of you cannot navigate the topic hypothetically, over coffee, at maximum goodwill, you have learned the most important fact available to you: you will certainly not navigate it in month fourteen with two thousand euros left. A person who gets defensive when asked "what if one of us wants out" over coffee does not become more graceful when it is real. The conversation is the cheapest look you will ever get at the expensive version.

A cofounder is the person who shares your Tuesday. Dreams are cheap and everyone's are lovely; the prenup conversation shows you what their Tuesday looks like before you buy it.

Here is the question list. It is long on purpose. Do not do it in one sitting, do it across two or three, and, critically, write your answers separately first, then compare. The gaps between the written answers are the entire value of the exercise. Two people who answer out loud will converge politely in real time and learn nothing.

A note on staging, because the setting does real work. Neutral ground beats either of your kitchens, and a long walk beats a table, since hard answers come easier side by side than face to face. Give the evening a shape that says construction rather than tribunal: rows, then food, then whatever you drink when something has gone well. And write the answers down as you go, dated, both signatures at the bottom, even if it is a page torn from a notebook. That page is the first draft of the founders' agreement you will sign later, and the teams that keep it grow oddly fond of it, the way you keep a photo of the foundation trench.

A few of these deserve unpacking, because they carry more freight than one table row can hold.

The ambition question is the sleeper

Money and power feel like the dangerous topics, but in my observation, the divergence that actually ends mature founding teams is ambition. Two founders can agree on everything operational and still be building different companies in their heads. One pictures selling in five years for enough to buy a house and freedom. The other pictures their grandchildren refusing to sell. Both picture it silently, because the topic never forces itself into any weekly meeting, and the company drifts along fine until an acquirer emails, or until a growth opportunity demands risk that only makes sense for one of the two futures. Then, overnight, you discover you have been rowing one boat toward two harbors.

I watched this take apart two men who had run a printing business together for eleven good years. An acquirer offered a fair price. The older founder was fifty-eight and heard the offer as a door opening; the younger was forty-four and heard an insult. Eleven years of agreeing about paper stock and delivery vans, and they had never once asked each other the question, so their first ambition conversation happened with a deadline on it and lawyers in the room. They sold in the end, at a worse price, mostly to be done with each other. Ask the question now: what is your number, what is your year, what does "done" look like? The answers may differ, and that is fine and normal. Undiscussed is the only fatal version.

The spouse at the table

Every founding team of two is a team of at least three or four, because partners and spouses hold invisible seats. They absorb the missing income, the cancelled holidays, the founder who is home but not present. A spouse who feels the company was inflicted rather than chosen becomes, entirely reasonably, a nightly voice for quitting, and no cofounder can outvote a kitchen table. I now believe the founding dinner, all founders and all partners, once before starting and once a year after, is as important as the shareholders' agreement. The dinner makes the invisible seats visible, so everyone making a sacrifice gets to choose it.

Try this

Book two hours with your cofounder or candidate this week. Before you meet, each of you writes answers to the Endings row and the Ambition row of the prenup table, alone, on paper, no peeking. At the meeting, swap papers and read in silence before anyone speaks. Then discuss only the gaps. If the meeting goes well, schedule the remaining rows. If it goes badly, it cost you two hours to learn something that otherwise costs eighteen months. If you are solo, write both sets of answers yourself, one year apart in imagination: what you want now, and what the exhausted year-three version of you will want. The gaps are still the point.

Skills and Temperament: The Two Axes That Matter

When people evaluate a potential cofounder, they instinctively evaluate skills, and they instinctively evaluate them wrong, by looking for someone impressive rather than someone complementary. An impressive person who is impressive at the same things as you is a rival with shares. The recipe app is the specimen in the jar here: Sam and I overlapped almost perfectly on the fun skills, product opinions and grand plans, and left a whole hemisphere of the company, selling to actual supermarkets, talking to actual users, bookkeeping, uncovered by either of us. Instead of dividing the work, we duplicated half of it and orphaned the rest.

So the first axis is skills, and the rule is simple: you want distance. If you are the builder, you want a seller. If you are the seller, you want a builder. In a bakery, if you are the baker, you want the one who loves the counter, the suppliers and the numbers. The company needs someone who makes the thing and someone who makes people pay for the thing, from the first week, at founder-level intensity, and one person is rarely both.

The second axis is temperament, and here the rule inverts, which is why so many teams get it wrong. On temperament you want proximity, or at most a short, respectful distance. Temperament is your default setting under uncertainty: how much risk feels like air and how much feels like drowning, whether your instinct in fog is to accelerate or to brake. A team of two accelerators is exhilarating and dies of a cliff. A team of two brakes is safe and dies of a standstill. A slight offset is the sweet spot, one founder whose first question is "why not faster" and one whose first question is "what would kill us," close enough to trust each other's readings, different enough that the company gets both questions asked. But a large offset on this axis is torture. If every risk that feels obvious to you feels reckless to them, every single decision becomes a negotiation between two nervous systems, and a nervous system's idea of compromise is taking turns being resentful.

Put the two axes together and you get the map I now draw, on an actual napkin, for anyone who tells me they have found a cofounder.

Two honest caveats about the map. First, "builder" and "seller" are stand-ins for whatever the two load-bearing disciplines of your specific business are. In an agency it might be craft and client. In a bakery, oven and counter. In deep tech, lab and grant. Name your own poles before you plot anyone. Second, people lie to themselves about their position on both axes, and founders are professional self-deceivers. Plot where the trial project showed the candidate sits, never where they say they sit. Sam described himself as the commercial one, and he had the talent, but eighteen months produced no evidence of him selling anything to anyone except me. The map only works when you feed it evidence.

What about three or four founders?

Everything in this chapter extends to larger founding teams, but the physics change: the number of relationships grows faster than the number of people. Two founders have one relationship to maintain. Three have three. Four have six, plus the coalitions, and coalitions are the real hazard, the recurring two-against-one that turns a founding team into a parliament. Three can work beautifully when the skills are genuinely three-cornered and there is one unambiguous CEO. Beyond three, ask hard whether founders two through five are truly founders or actually early employees who deserve good option packages instead, a distinction chapter ten will help you draw. And whatever the number, the prenup conversation happens with everyone in the room at once, because the private bilateral versions create exactly the coalitions you are trying to prevent.

Roles: Who Does What, and Who Decides What

With the person chosen and the prenup underway, divide the actual work. This sounds administrative. It was disease number four in the recipe app autopsy, and I want to distinguish the two failures hiding inside it, because they need different cures.

The first failure is duplicated fun. Every company has glamour work, product direction, big-client conversations, anything involving a whiteboard, and drudge work, bookkeeping, contracts, testing, chasing invoices, replying to the fourteenth support email. Left undivided, both founders gravitate to the glamour and orbit each other there, while the drudgery composts in a shared inbox. The cure is embarrassingly simple: write the boring functions down as a list, and assign every single one to exactly one name. One name. "We both do support" counts as no names. The routing company's version of this list, written after a week in which my sales lead and I had each promised a different discount to the same customer, hung by the coffee machine for years. It had two columns and no third. Invoices, me. The support inbox, him. The tax calendar, me. Supplier renewals, him. Every line on it was boring, and every line was the company.

The second failure is undecided decisions, and it is the one that kills. Two founders who both do everything have not divided the labor, but two founders who both decide everything have divided nothing at all. You need a decision protocol, agreed in peacetime. Mine has three tiers, and I have lent it to enough founding teams to know it holds. Domain decisions: each founder owns named domains, product and technology his, sales and money hers, whatever your split is, and within a domain the owner decides after consulting, not after convincing. The consultation is mandatory, the agreement is not. Both-founders decisions: a short, written list of what genuinely needs both signatures, taking debt, hiring, firing, changing anyone's pay, anything above an agreed spending limit, anything that touches equity. Deadlock decisions: for the rare disagreement that is both-founders and truly stuck, decide now, in peacetime, whose call it finally is. The cleanest answer is the CEO's, which is why one of you must be CEO.

Yes, one of you. I know the appeal of co-CEOs, I felt it too, it feels like the org-chart expression of friendship. But a title split evenly is a decision split evenly, and a decision split evenly is a deadlock stored for later. Give your cofounder the final call and you remain every bit a founder, the way a first officer remains every bit a pilot. Naming a captain is what makes the aircraft certifiable. The one who is not CEO gets domains with real sovereignty, gets consulted on everything big, and gets something the CEO never gets: the standing right to say "as your cofounder, not your employee, you are wrong," which is worth more than the title. How this pairing evolves once there are managers and departments between you is an org-design question, and that is chapter eleven's territory.

Make the spending limit a real number, by the way. At the routing company I set it at five hundred euros between me and anyone who ran a budget: below the line, spend and move on; above it, one message first. The number matters less than its existence, because the alternative is that every purchase carries a tiny social calculation, should I check, will he mind, and a thousand tiny calculations a year is how people get tired of each other. We raised the limit as the company grew, and the raising itself became a pleasant little ritual, a measurable form of "we are doing well." I know a two-woman agency whose limit is two hundred euros and a three-founder wholesaler whose limit is ten thousand, and all of them describe the same relief: never having to wonder what needs asking.

One warning about role boundaries: they blur silently. Companies change shape faster than titles do, and eighteen months in, the actual work has migrated while the agreement stays frozen in the founding document. The fix is a scheduled redraw, every six months, thirty minutes, same three questions: what do you actually spend your time on now, what do I, and does the map still match the terrain? It is the cheapest meeting on your calendar and almost nobody holds it.

Equity: Dividing a Pie That Does Not Exist Yet

Now the money part, and let me start by defusing the most common mistake, the one Sam and I made in four minutes over pizza: defaulting to 50/50 because discussing anything else feels rude.

Understand what the four minutes are actually avoiding. An equity split is a valuation of each founder's total expected contribution over roughly a decade, and valuing your friend's contribution against your own, out loud, is one of the most socially uncomfortable acts imaginable. So the brain reaches for the number that requires no valuation at all, the split that means "let's not discuss it." Half the time, 50/50 is also roughly correct, and those teams get lucky. The other half, it is a polite lie that one founder starts secretly amortizing from month one, and secret amortization is exactly what it sounds like: interest accrues, silently, until the balance gets called in during some unrelated fight about a food fair in Utrecht.

Warning

Whatever your split, a two-founder company at exactly 50/50 with no deadlock mechanism is a machine for converting disagreements into paralysis. If you choose equal shares, and there are honest reasons to, then you must also choose, in writing, how a stalemate breaks: a casting vote on defined topics, a trusted third party with a golden share worth 1 percent, a mediation-then-buyout ladder, anything. Sam and I had the 50/50 and no mechanism. The mechanism we eventually used was a parking lot.

How do you reason about the split, then? Walk through the factors that truly differentiate founders' contributions, assign honest weight to each, and then, this is the part everyone skips, say the resulting argument out loud to each other before converting it to a number. Online calculators exist, and they are useful the way horoscopes are useful, as prompts for conversation.

Walk the factors, argue honestly, land on a number. Some teams land on 50/50 with a deadlock breaker, having actually earned the number rather than defaulted to it, and those are fine numbers now. Others land on 60/40 or 55/45, and here is the counterintuitive news from the far side: the unequal splits, when they are the argued-out kind, tend to be the stable ones, because both founders can recite the reasons. Resentment grows from unspoken arithmetic, and it will grow there at any ratio, including 50/50. Say the arithmetic.

Two boundaries on the whole exercise. A founder below roughly 20 percent is, in their own head, an employee with a complicated tax situation, and their behavior will eventually match their stake, so if the factors genuinely produce 88/12, believe the factors and hire the person instead. And keep the split conversation separate from the investment conversation. What outside money does to everyone's percentages, and whether to take any at all, is chapter six, and mixing the two negotiations lets each hide inside the other.

One last thing about timing, and it deserves a picture, because founders keep making the same scheduling mistake: postponing the equity conversation until things are "clearer." Things do get clearer. The conversation also gets harder, faster, because every month adds contribution history to argue about, sunk feelings to protect, and company value to fight over. The cheapest day to divide the pie honestly was yesterday. The second cheapest is today.

Vesting, Paper, and the Notary

Everything agreed so far lives in conversation, and conversation has a shelf life. Memory, as established in the introduction, is a helpful colleague who tidies away your mistakes; it is just as happy to tidy away your agreements. So the last construction step is writing it down properly, and the two load-bearing pieces of paper are the vesting schedule and the founders' agreement.

Vesting: the most unromantic act of love available to founders

Vesting means the shares become yours over time rather than at once. A standard arrangement runs four years with a one-year cliff: leave before the first anniversary and you keep nothing, stay four years and your full stake is yours, leave in between and you keep the earned fraction. Founders resist vesting their own shares with real indignation. It is my company, why should I earn my own shares? Here is why, in the form of the only scenario that matters: your cofounder, not you, of course, never you, quits in month ten. Without vesting, they walk away holding 50 percent of everything you will build over the next ten years, in exchange for ten months of shared enthusiasm. Every drop of sweat you put in afterwards is half theirs. You will not raise money, you will not bring in a replacement, and you will not forgive them. With vesting, they leave with a sliver that roughly matches their contribution, and both of you can still attend each other's birthdays.

Vesting is the two of you protecting the company from every future version of yourselves, including the tired ones, the divorced ones, and the ones with a better offer. I have never once heard a founder regret vesting. I keep a small collection of founders who regret its absence, and they all tell the story in the same flat voice. One of them, a man I will call Jeroen, watched his cofounder leave in month nine for a corporate salary, holding 40 percent. Jeroen built the company anyway, for six more years, and the arithmetic never changed: two of every five days he worked belonged, in effect, to a man on a beach. When the company finally sold, the man on the beach made more from the sale than Jeroen had drawn in salary across the whole six years. "The worst part," Jeroen told me, "is that the clause would have cost us one page and an hour."

The founders' agreement: the prenup, notarized

The founders' or shareholders' agreement is where the prenup conversation stops being a conversation. In most of Europe you will pass through a notary to incorporate anyway, so the marginal effort of doing this properly is one more meeting and a fee that is a rounding error against what it insures. Bring your own answers, though. A notary or lawyer given no instructions will hand you a template, and a template does not know which of you is CEO or what you decided about the gym pivot scenario. The document should at minimum settle the following, and you should be able to tick each row against a prenup answer you already gave each other.

Read the third column again, slowly. Every question in it is one I have heard asked, in those words or close to them, by a real founder in a real mess, and in every case the honest answer was "whatever the two of you can still agree on," which by that point was nothing. The agreement converts the mess from a war into paperwork, and paperwork, whatever else you say about it, has never once made anyone sell the company out of spite.

Story

Years after the recipe app, long after we were friends again, Sam started his second company, the kitchen software business, the one that keeps a restaurant's stock synced with its menu, with a cofounder he had known for less than a year. A stranger, by our old standards. One evening he sent me a photo with no caption. It was a signed shareholders' agreement on a notary's desk, and next to it, held in place by his thumb, a paper napkin covered in our handwriting, both of ours, from the one dinner I will tell you about shortly. He had kept the napkin for six years.

I called him and asked how the negotiation with the new cofounder had gone, all those clauses, the vesting, the bad-leaver terms, with someone he barely knew.

"Easiest conversations we've had," he said. "That's the joke, Wout. With you it would have felt like an insult. With him it just felt like business. We did the whole list in two evenings, and I know him better after those two evenings than I knew you after fifteen years." He paused, and I could hear him grinning in the way that used to mean a plan was coming. "Turns out you can skip the parking lot. There was a form for it the whole time."

Try this

Before any lawyer is involved, take a pen and write the leaver clause yourself, from the leaver's side. Imagine it is you who walks away in month eighteen, because of burnout, a sick parent, a lost belief in the plan. Write down what you would honestly consider fair to keep, and what treatment would let you stay friends with the person remaining. Have your cofounder do the same, separately. Then swap. In my experience people write stricter terms for themselves than any lawyer would dare propose, and the swap produces the fastest, warmest agreement of the whole founding process. Keep the napkin.

When It Breaks Anyway

Now the section I would rather skip, which is usually the sign it is the one worth writing. Some founding teams break. Skipping this chapter worsens the odds, but teams that did everything right break too, because people change, companies change shape, and a partnership priced at twenty-two can be genuinely wrong at twenty-nine without anyone having sinned. So you should know what breaking looks like from the inside, how to steer it if it comes, and what waits on the other side.

It rarely announces itself. There is no morning when your cofounder walks in and declares the partnership over. Instead there is drift, and the drift has symptoms you can learn to read early. The meetings between you get shorter and more polite; politeness is the first fever of a founder relationship, because it means the friction has gone private. You start making decisions around each other, small ones first, each technically within someone's domain, each chosen partly because asking felt heavy. One of you starts saying "your part of the business." Jokes stop landing. One founder I know diagnosed his own drift the day he caught himself feeling relieved that his cofounder was on holiday. And at some point you notice you are rehearsing conversations with them in the shower, which is the moment to act, because a conversation you are rehearsing is a conversation you are not having.

What acting looks like: name the drift while it is still drift. A scheduled talk, out of the office, with the explicit agenda of "us, not the company." An ambush just produces more drift with adrenaline in it. Most drifts, caught early, turn out to be one founder carrying something unsaid, an imbalance, a fear, a home situation, and saying it aloud recovers the partnership at the cost of one difficult walk. This is exactly the muscle the prenup conversation built, and this is why you built it in peacetime. Teams that never learned to discuss the partnership hypothetically cannot suddenly discuss it for real, which is roughly the whole reason Sam and I ended in a parking lot rather than in a conversation.

Sometimes the walk fails, and the honest outcome is separation. Then the goal changes to saving three other things: the company, the money, and, if you are lucky and careful, the friendship. The mechanics come out of the paperwork you signed in the good times, vesting fixes what leaves, the valuation clause fixes the price, the non-compete fixes the aftermath. What the paperwork cannot fix is conducted in tone. Do it fast rather than slow, a founder separation dragged over a year poisons every employee who watches it. Do it generously rather than precisely, the few points of equity you win in a hard negotiation cost more in reputation and grief than they will ever be worth. And do it with a script for the outside world, agreed together, told identically, because customers and staff need to know the company is calm; the truth of the marriage stays yours.

And if you are reading this section not as a founder but as the friend, spouse or mentor of someone inside a founder breakup, the useful thing you can do is the thing a man named Henk once did for me, which was to refuse to agree that the other person was a villain. The villain story is always available and always cheap. What I needed, and what he gave me by simply not nodding, was the more expensive story, the one where two people built a machine with no steering and were both surprised where it went.

Story

The silence between Sam and me ended because of his mother, which neither of us knew for years. She ran into me at the market, asked how I was, ignored my answer, and said, "He listens to the voicemail you left about the servers. I have heard it through the door. Call him about nothing." So I called him about nothing. Football, at first. It took three calls about nothing before one of us, him, said, "We should probably do the funeral properly," and that became a dinner.

The dinner was the postmortem we should have had as a kickoff. We ordered too much food, out of nerves, and then took the app apart for five hours, not the product, the partnership. Somewhere past midnight Sam flattened a napkin and said, "Rules for next time, whoever the next time is with." We wrote them together. Who is CEO, decided before anything else. Hours on the table monthly, so the score is public and therefore not a score. Money separate from pride. Every "what if" answered in writing while you still like each other. And at the bottom, in Sam's handwriting, underlined twice: the friendship is not collateral.

I told him that night that I thought the app had cost me my best friend and eighteen months. He thought about it, in that way he has, like the question had to clear customs. "Wrong on both," he said. "The app cost eighteen months. The friend you got back is a different one. Better model. This one has crashed with you." We split the bill exactly in half, which by then was funny, and it remains the best money I have spent on my education, including the eleven euros a year for the domain, which, yes, I still pay. Some invoices you keep on purpose.

That napkin became, years later and with better handwriting, the chapter you have almost finished. Which brings me to the last thing I want to say before the summary: a founding team failure, survived honestly, is tuition. Sam spent his on the second company and on a cofounder he vetted like a notary. I spent mine the other way. I started the routing company alone, partly because I am built that way and partly because the parking lot was still fresh, and I ran it alone to the end. The napkin rules earned their dinner even so: every key employee who ever held shares in that company got the prenup conversation, the vesting, the written what-ifs, and when the company sold, all of those people were still speaking to me. But Priya's table for one was my table too, for more than a decade, and when she talks about telling the news to a houseplant, I know exactly which kitchen she means. One parking lot, two educations. Sam and I split the bill.

Choose the person by evidence. Have the conversation while it is cheap. Split by argument, and never by politeness. Put it on paper with vesting. Redraw the roles before they blur. And if it breaks anyway, break it the way you built it, on purpose, out loud, together. The company is the thing you are making, but the founding team is the thing that makes the thing, and it deserves at least as much engineering as the product. More, actually. You can pivot a product. Parking lots are forever.

The Short Version

  • Friendship proves you can enjoy someone. Proof you can build together comes only from building together: run a four-to-eight-week trial project, with a deadline, a budget and some boredom in it, before you incorporate anything.
  • Solo founding is a legitimate choice, but budget for the missing witness: build a bench of peers and advisors who can sit at the table where the doubt sits down. If what you need is skills, hire them (chapter ten) rather than marrying them.
  • Have the prenup conversation now, in writing first, answers compared: money in, money out, hours, power, ambition, family, endings. The conversation gets more expensive every month you delay it, and the topics never change.
  • Map skills and temperament separately: maximum distance on skills, short distance on temperament. Two of the same founder is a rival with shares; opposite nervous systems is a permanent negotiation.
  • One CEO. Domains with real ownership, a short both-signatures list, and a written answer to "who decides when we are stuck." Redraw the role map every six months before it blurs on its own.
  • Split equity by argued factors, and only accept 50/50 when the argument earned it and a deadlock breaker sits beside it. Resentment grows from unspoken arithmetic, at any ratio, so say the arithmetic.
  • Vest your own shares, four years, one-year cliff: insurance against every future version of both of you, including the tired ones and the ones with a better offer.
  • Put it all in a founders' agreement with leaver terms, buyout valuation, and IP assignment, while you still like each other. Paperwork converts the mess from war into administration.
  • Watch for drift: politeness, shrinking meetings, decisions made around each other, conversations rehearsed in the shower. Name it while it is still drift.
  • If it breaks anyway, separate fast, generously, and with one agreed story. Protect the company, the money, and the friendship, in whatever order you can manage, and take the tuition with you to the next one.
