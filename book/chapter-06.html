Chapter 6

Managing Money

Companies die of one thing: an empty bank account. Bad products, bad hires, and bad luck only set the date. The empty account announces itself weeks in advance, if you know where to look.

The best quarter Jonas ever had nearly killed his company.

Jonas is my brother-in-law. He turned one bakery into nine over the better part of two decades, and at the time of this story he had three: the original shop, a second one two towns over, and a third that had been open for six weeks and smelled of fresh paint and ambition. He had also just landed the account he had been chasing for a year, supplying bread and pastries to a small hotel chain. Sales were the best they had ever been. His accountant had told him, in writing, that he was on track for his most profitable year since opening.

On a Tuesday in October, the owner of his flour supplier called him. The owner, past the sales rep, on his own phone: that was a message before the man said a word.

The message itself was short. Two invoices were overdue, a third was about to be, the total was a little over eleven thousand euros, and unless meaningful money arrived by Thursday, Friday's delivery would stay on the truck. The man kept his voice kind. He had his own chapter six to worry about.

Jonas called me that evening, from the new shop, after closing. I could hear him wiping down a counter that did not need it. I remember the exact sentence, because it is the sentence this whole chapter exists to explain. He said, "Wouter, I don't understand. We are having the best quarter in the history of the company, and there is no money."

Both halves of that sentence were true. That is the terrifying part. The hotel chain paid on sixty-day terms, so the best sales in company history were sitting in a folder marked "invoices sent," worth exactly nothing at the flour counter. The new bakery had eaten a deposit on ovens, a fit-out bill, and two months of wages before selling its first loaf. The quarterly VAT payment had left the account the week before, taking with it a pile of money that had looked reassuring and had never been his. Jonas was profitable, growing, praised by his accountant, and about forty-eight hours from being a baker who could not buy flour.

He survived, and I will tell you how later in the chapter, because the way he survived is more useful than the fact that he did. But I want you to hold onto the image: a man standing in a successful, profitable, growing business, staring at a bank balance that says the business is dying. If you have ever stood there, you know the specific temperature of that feeling. If you have not stood there yet, you probably will, and my whole ambition for the next few thousand words is that when it happens, you recognize it, you know it has a name, and you know what to do on Wednesday morning.

One practical note before we start, because I know how this chapter gets read. In the introduction I said some chapters get raided at midnight, and this is the most raided chapter in the book. If it is midnight for you right now, if your version of the flour call is tomorrow, skip directly to the sections called "The 13-Week Cashflow Forecast" and "Runway Math." They are the first aid. Then come back here, because the earlier sections explain why you ended up needing first aid, and that is the part that stops it happening twice.

Profit Is an Opinion, Cash Is a Fact

Everything in this chapter grows from one distinction, so we will take it slowly and get it right. Profit and cash answer two different questions, on two different clocks, and confusing them is the most common way founders get hurt by money.

Profit answers the question: over some period, did the value of what we sold exceed the cost of selling it? Cash answers the question: can I pay the bills that are due this week? The first question is about performance. The second is about survival. A business needs good answers to both, but only one of them can kill you by Friday.

The two numbers drift apart because of a bookkeeping convention called accrual accounting, which underlies every set of accounts you will ever read, so it is worth understanding. Accrual accounting records a sale on the day you earn it; payment arrives whenever it arrives. When Jonas delivered four hundred croissants to a hotel, his books recorded the revenue that morning, while the actual money was scheduled to arrive two months later, if the hotel's accounts payable department was in a good mood. The books also spread big costs over time: his new ovens hit the bank account as one brutal payment, but appeared in the profit calculation as a modest depreciation charge, sliced into pieces over ten years.

Accountants built accrual accounting for a fair reason: it measures performance better. If you paid for the ovens in January, it would be silly to declare January a catastrophe and February a triumph when the underlying business was identical in both months. Smoothing things out gives you a fairer picture of how the machine is running. But notice what has happened: in exchange for a fairer picture of performance, you have accepted a picture that says nothing reliable about the bank account. The books show the movie. The bank account is the single frame you are actually living in.

Story

In the second year of the routing company, I got my first properly prepared annual accounts back from the accountant, and they said we had made a profit. A small one, but real, five figures, with my name under it. I printed them. I want to be honest about how proud I was: I printed them twice, in case something happened to the first copy.

I took them to Henk. He was retired by then in the way a kettle is retired when you unplug it, which is to say not at all, and he read annual accounts for fun the way other people do crosswords. He put on his glasses, went through my proud pages in about four minutes, and then asked a question that had nothing to do with anything printed on them.

"What was in the bank account this morning?"

I knew the number, because I checked it every morning with one eye closed. It was small. Embarrassingly small, given what the papers in his hand said. I told him, and he nodded like a doctor hearing the symptom he expected.

"And who owes you money, and who do you owe money?" I knew those numbers too, roughly. Customers owed us a lot. We owed the tax office a chunk and two suppliers a bit. He took off his glasses and tapped my beautiful accounts with them.

"Remember this, because it will save your life one day. Profit is an opinion. Cash is a fact. This document," and here he handed my accounts back with the respect you would give a decent restaurant menu, "is a well-argued opinion. The bank account this morning is a fact. Opinions do not pay wages."

I have since watched Henk's line hold up in every business I have run, invested in, or sat next to at dinner. It deserves unpacking, because under the grumpy slogan sits a precise technical claim.

Profit is an opinion because a surprising number of the ingredients in it are choices. Someone chose how many years to depreciate the ovens over, and choosing eight instead of ten would change the profit. Someone chose when a long project counts as "earned," which for a six-month software implementation can move tens of thousands of euros between this year and next year with one decision. Someone chose how much to set aside for customers who probably will not pay, for the holiday days your team has built up, for the lawsuit that has a thirty percent chance of happening. Reasonable, honest professionals can make different choices on every one of those and produce meaningfully different profit numbers from identical businesses. That is what an opinion is.

Cash has no ingredients. It is a number your bank reports, to the cent, with no assumptions in it. You cannot restate it, smooth it, or recognize it early. This is why it is both cruder and more trustworthy than profit. When the two disagree, and in a young company they disagree constantly, cash is telling you about this week and profit is telling you about this era. You need both, but you check them on very different schedules: profit monthly, with coffee and some distance, and cash weekly, or in the hard seasons, daily.

Here is the sequence I want to burn into you, because it is the mechanism behind almost every founder money crisis I have ever seen, including mine and including Jonas's. A euro travels through your company in stages. First you spend it: on stock, on salaries, on the work itself. Then, later, you earn it: the sale happens and your books record revenue. Then, later still, you receive it: the customer actually pays. Spend, earn, receive, in that order, with gaps between each stage. Profit lives at stage two. Survival lives at stage three. The gaps between the stages are where companies die, and those gaps have a name, and a section of their own coming shortly.

Before we get there, though, we need to make peace with the document that made me so proud and Henk so unimpressed. You are going to receive a profit and loss statement every month or quarter for the rest of your working life. It is time to learn to read it without crying.

Reading a P&L Without Crying

The profit and loss statement, the P&L, is the document your bookkeeper or accountant produces to answer the performance question: over this month, this quarter, this year, did the business create more value than it consumed? Founders avoid it for two reasons. The first is that it arrives formatted like a tax form and written in a dialect nobody speaks at home. The second is fear: the suspicion that if you actually understood it, it would say something you do not want to hear. Both problems have the same cure, which is realizing that underneath the formatting, every P&L in the world is the same five-line story.

The story goes like this. We sold some things (revenue). The things cost us something to make or deliver (direct costs). What is left over is the gross profit, which is the money the actual product generates before the company around it starts eating. Then the company eats: salaries, rent, software, marketing, insurance, the coffee machine (overhead). Whatever survives that is your operating profit. After a few final bites for depreciation, interest, and tax, the number at the bottom is the net profit, the famous bottom line, which as we established is a well-argued opinion.

Let me make it concrete. Here is a full year for an invented but very typical small company, a twelve-person studio selling a mix of services and a small software product. If you run a bakery, swap the labels and nothing else changes: flour and butter and counter staff are direct costs, the shop lease is overhead, the mixer is depreciation. The rightmost column is the part no accountant will print for you, which is what each line is actually telling you and where it can mislead you.

Two honest reactions people have to a table like that. The first: "only twenty thousand euros left from four hundred and twenty thousand? Why bother?" Welcome to small business margins. Most healthy small companies keep between three and fifteen cents of every euro of revenue, which is precisely why the pricing decisions from chapter four are worth more than almost anything else you do; a two percent price increase in this company, costs unchanged, would add €8,400 straight to the bottom line and increase net profit by forty percent. The second reaction: "where in this document is the eleven thousand euros for the flour supplier?" Nowhere. That is the point. This whole useful document contains no answer to the question "can I pay Thursday's bill?" Nobody ever asked it that question.

The five questions to ask every P&L

A ritual will serve you better here than an accounting course. Once a month, when the P&L arrives, ask it these five questions, in order, and write the answers somewhere you will see them again next month.

One: is revenue where I expected, and if not, do I know why? Prediction matters more than size here. The gap between expectation and reality is the most informative number in your company, and chapter seven is built around tracking exactly those gaps in your plan.

Two: what is my gross margin percentage, and which direction has it moved over three months? Direction beats level. A bakery at 65 percent falling to 61 has a bigger problem than a bakery stable at 58.

Three: which overhead line grew fastest, and did I decide that on purpose? Costs that grow by decision are strategy. Costs that grow by themselves are rot.

Four: is operating profit positive, and if not, when exactly do I believe it will be, and what has to happen first? Demand a real answer from yourself. "Positive in March, if the two signed contracts start on time" is an answer. "Soon" is a prayer.

Five: if revenue stopped growing today and simply stayed flat, would this company be fine? If yes, you are what an investor would call default alive, and every choice you make from here is made from strength. If no, you are on a clock, and you should know the exact date on the clock, which is what the runway section later in this chapter is for.

Twenty minutes, once a month. People run forty-person companies on exactly this ritual and nothing more, right up until the complexity genuinely demands a finance function, which is a scaling problem I will point at in chapter fourteen and otherwise leave alone.

Liquidity, or Where the Money Hides

So the P&L says you are doing fine, and the bank account disagrees. Where did the money actually go? In a small company it is nearly always hiding in one of five places, and I want to walk through them one at a time, because the day you can recite this list from memory is the day bank account surprises mostly stop happening to you.

Hiding place one: your customers' bank accounts. Every unpaid invoice is your money, sitting in someone else's account, earning them interest. Accountants call the total "debtors" or "accounts receivable." Jonas's record quarter was real, and nearly all of it sat in this hiding place, locked behind a sixty-day payment term. The bigger and more corporate your customers, the longer they hold your money, because their finance department reads the same advice in reverse: pay late, collect early. Somebody's working capital has to finance the gap, and both sides would prefer it to be yours.

Hiding place two: your shelves. Stock is money that has been turned into objects. Flour, packaging, components, finished goods waiting to sell: every euro of it is a euro that cannot pay wages until someone buys it and then actually pays for it. Service and software businesses smile at this one, then discover their version of it, which is work in progress: weeks of salary poured into a project you have not yet been allowed to invoice.

Hiding place three: things you paid for early. Deposits, annual insurance paid in January, the year of rent guarantee, the conference sponsorship for an event nine months away. All real spending, all invisible on this month's P&L, all missing from the account.

Hiding place four: equipment. Jonas's ovens. Your laptops, vans, fit-outs. Cash out on day one, cost spread over years. The P&L will spend a decade calmly mentioning the ovens in €8,000 annual slices, long after the actual €80,000 left the building in a single Tuesday.

Hiding place five, and the cruellest: money that was never yours. This one deserves its own warning box.

The VAT trap

When a customer pays a €10,000 invoice, roughly €2,100 of it is VAT you collected on behalf of the tax office. It sits in your account looking exactly like your money, and every cent of it belongs to the state. You are babysitting that cash, unpaid, until the quarterly VAT deadline sweeps it away in one lump. The same story applies to payroll taxes and, in a slower way, to the holiday pay your team is accruing. Most founders learn this the way Jonas learned it: the account looks fine, the VAT payment lands, the account looks very different. The cure is simple and I will nag you about it at the end of the chapter: a second bank account, and a fixed percentage of every customer payment moved into it on the day the payment arrives. Money you never see is money you never spend by accident.

Add the five hiding places together and you get what accountants call working capital: the money that is permanently stuck in the machine just to keep it turning. Here is the sentence about working capital that surprises everyone the first time: growth makes it worse. Every new customer means more money hiding in unpaid invoices. Every new bakery means more flour on shelves and more deposits paid. Grow fast enough and the machine swallows cash faster than the profits refill it, which is how a company can be growing, profitable, and going bankrupt all at once, and why the fastest-growing companies are so often the ones that die of it. Bankers, who see it weekly, have a dry name for it: overtrading. I prefer the plainer description, which is growing yourself to death.

Field note

A note on European payment reality, since the books mostly quote American norms. The law in the EU says business customers should pay within 30 days, or 60 by explicit agreement. The practice, as every founder discovers, is that large companies treat your payment terms as an opening offer and their own payment run calendar as the actual law. Assume big customers pay 10 to 20 days later than agreed, and build that assumption into the forecast we are about to make rather than into your feelings. The law also entitles you to interest and collection costs on late payments. Knowing this is useful roughly once a year, in a firm email, and the rest of the time the real lever is boring discipline: invoice the same day you deliver, and chase on the first day overdue, with the same friendly note, every single time.

The Cash Gap

Let us give the killer its proper name. The cash gap is the stretch of time between the day money leaves your account to make a sale possible and the day money from that sale finally arrives. Every business has one. Most founders have never measured theirs, which is a shame, because it is the single number that explains why your bank balance behaves the way it does.

Walk through Jonas's version. He pays for flour on delivery, near enough day zero. He bakes and delivers to the hotel through the month. He invoices at the end of the month, day 30. The hotel's terms say 60 days, and their accounts department adds a little artistic license, so money lands around day 100. From the day his money left to the day their money arrived: a gap of just over three months. For one hundred days, every euro of that sale was financed by Jonas. Multiply by every sale happening at once, and that is the pile of his own cash Jonas must keep permanently inside the machine, before he earns anything at all.

Once you see the gap, you can start doing something about it, because every technique in the working capital playbook is just a way of dragging those markers toward each other. Drag the green marker left: invoice the day you deliver instead of at month end, offer a small discount for payment within seven days, take deposits, bill half up front, chase overdue invoices on day one with a friendly note instead of on day thirty with a sigh. Drag the red marker right: negotiate longer terms with suppliers once you have a payment history with them, lease the ovens instead of buying them, keep less stock. None of these is glamorous. Together, at the routing company, moves like these once freed up more cash in a quarter than our profit for the whole year, which taught me that working capital is where profit goes to hide.

You can, and should, put a number on your own gap, and the arithmetic is kitchen-table stuff. Take the average number of days your customers actually take to pay you, your bookkeeper can pull it from the ledger in minutes, add the number of days your money sits in stock or in work you have not yet invoiced, and subtract the days your suppliers give you before you must pay them. Jonas's arithmetic ran roughly seventy debtor days plus forty days of baking and month-end invoicing, minus ten days of supplier terms: a gap of about a hundred days. Our invented studio does better, around forty-five days, but watch what even that costs. The studio spends roughly €1,100 a day keeping itself alive, so a forty-five day gap means about €50,000 of its own cash is permanently locked inside the machine, doing nothing but holding the door open between paying and being paid. Shorten the gap by ten days, through faster invoicing and firmer chasing alone, and €11,000 walks back into the bank account without a single new sale. When people say there is money hidden in your business, this is the money they mean, and it is the first place to look before you ever consider the funding section coming later in this chapter.

Software founders, before you feel smug about having no flour: your gap is made of salaries. You pay developers monthly for work that becomes sellable much later, and if you sell subscriptions, each small monthly payment recovers the cost of a customer over a year or more. Your ovens are payroll. The one genuinely enviable trick in software is annual prepayment, where the customer pays in January for the whole year: that runs the timeline backwards, cash in before cost out, a negative cash gap. If you can get it, price it generously. A customer who pays a year up front at a fifteen percent discount is often financing your company more cheaply than any bank would, and the discount buys you something no bank sells, which is calm.

Here is the part I most need you to internalize. The cash gap scales with growth. Double your sales and you roughly double the money trapped in the gap, and the new money has to be found now, while the new revenue arrives months later. This is the machinery behind the phrase I used earlier, growing yourself to death, and it means a very counterintuitive thing: the moment of fastest growth is the moment of highest danger. The hotel deal and the third bakery, the two proudest wins of Jonas's year, caused the flour call.

You go bankrupt from running out of cash, and you can run out in a profitable year, in the best quarter in your history.

The 13-Week Cashflow Forecast

Now the tool. Everything so far has been diagnosis; this is the treatment, and I will make you a promise about it. If you build and maintain the simple spreadsheet described in this section, you will never again be surprised by your bank account. Worried, sometimes. Surprised, never. In fifteen years I have not found another twenty minutes a week with a better return.

A 13-week cashflow forecast is a table with one row per week, running a quarter into the future. For each week you estimate the cash that will arrive, the cash that will leave, and the bank balance that results. Money crossing the border of your bank account, and only that, week by week, thirteen weeks out. Revenue, costs, accruals, and the rest of the opinion stay in the P&L where they belong.

Why weeks? Because months hide the truth. A month can look fine in total while containing a Tuesday where payroll leaves before the big customer pays, and your bank does not evaluate you monthly. Why thirteen? Because a quarter is far enough ahead to see trouble while there is still time to act, and near enough that your estimates are real numbers from real invoices rather than hopeful fiction. Beyond thirteen weeks you are planning; inside thirteen weeks you are steering.

Here is what one looks like, with realistic numbers for the same invented twelve-person studio from the P&L table. Read the closing balance column first, top to bottom, and notice what it does in weeks 7 and 10.

The forecast says that in seven weeks it will be unable to pay its VAT, and in ten weeks it will be unable to pay its people. A predicted shortfall seven weeks out is a to-do list with a generous deadline. Read that sentence again, because it holds the whole point of the tool. With seven weeks of warning, the founder of this studio can chase the Groot & Zn invoice into week 6 instead of week 8, ask two retainer clients to move to payment on the 20th, request a VAT payment plan (tax offices across Europe grant short deferrals as a matter of course if you ask before the deadline rather than after), delay a laptop purchase, or arrange a small overdraft while the bank still sees a calm customer asking early. The same shortfall discovered on the Tuesday it happens offers a very different list: which of my people do I not pay, and in Jonas's version, which of my bakeries does not get flour.

That is the whole value of the tool: it converts emergencies into chores.

Story

Jonas survived flour week with an ugly, effective scramble: a partial payment to the supplier that afternoon from his personal savings, a humiliating phone call to the hotel chain's finance department that got half the outstanding invoice paid early, and a Saturday spent learning, at speed, most of what is in this chapter. It cost him a month of proper sleep, which his wife will tell you about before he does. But the part of the story I want you to keep is what he did afterwards.

He built the ugliest 13-week forecast I have ever seen. It lived in a spreadsheet with the filename geld.xlsx, money.xlsx, and the formatting of a ransom note. Every Friday at two, after the lunch rush, he sat in the back office of the first bakery with a coffee and updated it: fifteen minutes, sometimes twenty. Actual balance versus predicted, new invoices in, next deliveries out, VAT dates marked in red like saints' days.

Two years later, over Christmas, I watched him glance at his phone and tell his wife, "we're fine until March, but I'm not buying the van until the February VAT is out." Nine bakeries now, and the finance function is still, at its heart, Friday at two with a coffee. When his accountant once suggested proper forecasting software, Jonas said the sentence that belongs on the cover of this chapter: "Friday is the tool. The spreadsheet is just where Friday writes things down."

Try this

Build week one of your own forecast right now, with a pen, before the motivation fades. Draw six columns on a sheet of paper: week, cash in, cash out, net, closing balance, notes. Fill in just the next four weeks from memory: payroll dates, rent, VAT if due, and the invoices you honestly expect to get paid rather than the ones you deserve to have paid. Then open your banking app and check your memory against reality. The gap between what you wrote and what the bank says is a precise measurement of how much this chapter was written for you. Tomorrow, move the paper into a spreadsheet and extend it to thirteen weeks. Then put a repeating twenty-minute appointment in your calendar, weekly, same day, same time, forever.

Runway Math

The 13-week forecast answers "will I survive this quarter?" Runway answers the bigger, colder question: "if nothing changes, on what date does this company die?" Every founder should be able to answer that question to within a month, at any moment, including at a wedding, including at two in the morning. Every important decision you make, hiring, pricing, raising money, taking that risky big contract, means something different at two years of runway than at four months.

The arithmetic is deliberately primitive. First, two definitions. Your gross burn is everything that leaves the account in a typical month: salaries, rent, suppliers, software, the lot. Your net burn is gross burn minus the cash that typically comes in. If more comes in than goes out, congratulations, your net burn is negative, your runway is infinite, and your remaining job in this section is to build a buffer and stay that way. Otherwise:

Runway in months = cash in the bank ÷ average monthly net burn.

A worked example. The studio from our tables holds €18,400, which we already know is too thin, so let us give it the year-end position instead: €62,000 in the bank after a good December. Gross burn is €33,000 a month. Typical collections are €27,000 a month. Net burn: €6,000. Runway: about ten months. That is the whole calculation. A twelve-year-old can do it, and yet I have sat across the table from founders of real companies, with employees and lawyers and opinions about espresso, who could not tell me their number. The hard part is looking.

Now for the three refinements that turn the primitive number into an honest one.

Refinement one: your runway is a sawtooth

The formula uses an average, and averages are smooth. Your actual bank balance is a sawtooth: it climbs when the big invoices land and plunges on payroll day and VAT day. The average tells you the trend. The teeth tell you the truth: the first single tooth to touch zero kills you, whatever the average was doing that day. This is why runway math and the 13-week forecast come as a pair: runway is the long lens, the forecast is the short one, and companies die in the blind spot of founders who keep only one of them.

Refinement two: measure to your cash floor

Zero is the wrong finish line, for two reasons. Practically, a company stops working well before the account is empty: suppliers want prepayment the moment they smell trouble, and you must never gamble a payroll. Legally, in most of Europe, letting a company trade toward a wall you can see coming can land the mess on you personally. So set a cash floor and treat it as zero. My rule of thumb: one full payroll plus one VAT quarter, untouchable. For the studio, that is about €26,000, which shortens the honest runway from ten months to six. Yes, that stings. It is meant to.

Refinement three: run three movies, believe the sad one

Your net burn is built on assumptions about money coming in, and money coming in is the part you do not control. So calculate runway three times. The expected version: current customers keep paying, current pipeline converts at your historical rate. The pessimistic version: your biggest customer leaves or pays ninety days late, and nothing new closes for a quarter. The optimistic version: things go the way the pitch deck says. You may enjoy the optimistic movie for exactly one evening. You make plans, hiring plans, spending plans, raising plans, against the pessimistic one, because it is the only version where being wrong is pleasant. In the studio's pessimistic movie, losing its largest client takes collections from €27,000 to €19,000, net burn jumps to €14,000, and the runway to the floor is under three months. Ten months, six months, three months: same company, same bank balance, same morning. The spread between those numbers is your real exposure, and most founders have never once calculated it.

What do you do with the number? You act on thresholds, agreed with yourself in advance, while you are calm. Above twelve months of pessimistic runway: build, take risks, think in years. At six months: the yellow light. Choose, this month, between the two available roads, get to breakeven on the cash you have or secure new money, and start walking, because both roads take longer than people think. Cutting costs is slower than it looks: notice periods, cancellation terms, and severance mean a cut decided today often reaches the bank account three months later. Raising money is slower still, six to nine months door to door. At three months: the red light. Everything is on the table, and your calendar should contain almost nothing but revenue, collections, and cost decisions. The founders who die badly hit the yellow light and drive through it hoping, because hope arrives dressed as a promising sales call.

Try this

Right now, with a pen: write down your bank balance. Under it, write your average monthly net burn over the last three months, from real bank statements rather than memory or the budget. Divide. That is your naive runway. Now subtract your cash floor, one payroll plus one VAT payment, from the bank balance and divide again. Then write the pessimistic version: biggest customer gone, nothing new signed for a quarter. Three numbers, five minutes. Put them somewhere you will see them, and update them on the first Friday of every month. If the pessimistic number is under six, you have not finished this exercise until you have also written one sentence beginning "so this month I will."

Self-Funding, or Other People's Money

Sooner or later, usually at the six-month yellow light or in the glow of a growth opportunity, every founder faces the question: should we put more money into this machine, and whose money should it be? The startup press has flattened this question into a single ritual, raising venture capital, to the point where founders apologize to me for not having raised, as if bootstrapping were a diet they keep failing. Let me widen the menu back out, because there are at least eight kinds of money, they cost wildly different things, and the most expensive one is the one everyone writes articles about.

Before the menu, though, the question before the question: do you actually need money, or do you need a smaller cash gap? Go back to the timeline diagram. A business that invoices faster, collects deposits, holds less stock, and pays itself a VAT-safe buffer often discovers that the funding round it was planning was really a working capital problem wearing a fleece vest. Customers are the cheapest financiers you will ever have, they conduct no due diligence, take no equity, and attend no board meetings, and asking them to pay sooner is the most underrated fundraising strategy in Europe. Only once you have squeezed the gap and still genuinely need fuel, for equipment, for hiring ahead of revenue, for surviving to a milestone, does the menu below apply.

One line in that table deserves a paragraph of its own, because it is the one that reaches into your house: the personal guarantee. Banks lending to young companies will often ask you, the founder, to guarantee the loan yourself, which turns your limited company inside out; if the business cannot pay, your savings, and in the worst constructions your home, stand behind it. Sometimes a guarantee is the honest price of admission and worth paying, especially for a modest, well-understood amount. But treat it as its own negotiation rather than a formality on page nine. Ask whether a state guarantee scheme can replace part of it; most European countries run one for exactly this. Ask for a cap, a fixed maximum rather than "everything." Ask for it to expire once the company has a trading history. And whatever you agree, agree it in daylight, with your partner at home fully informed, because a guarantee signed in a hungry week is the most common way founders convert a business problem into a family one.

The self-funding path, living from the revenue column plus modest debt, has a compounding advantage that rarely gets written about because its beneficiaries are too busy running companies: every constraint it imposes is a discipline you need anyway. When customer money is the only money, you cannot afford to build things nobody wants, so you don't. You cannot afford vanity hires, so you don't make them. Jonas built nine bakeries on retained profit and two bank loans, and every bakery had to prove itself before the next one existed. The price is speed, and the price is real: self-funded companies grow at the pace of their own profits, and there are markets where that pace loses.

Which brings us to investment, and to Priya.

Story

Priya raised one round for her B2B software company, €2.2 million, and to understand what happened next you have to know that the raise itself went well. Fair terms, a lead investor she still respects, champagne, a press mention her mother framed. What follows is something more useful than a horror story. It is a receipt.

Years later, over dinner, I asked her what the money had bought. She gave the question real thought, the way she gives everything real thought, and then said: "Two years of speed, one competitor we outran, and three hires we couldn't have made. That part was real." Then she held up her other hand. "And I sold a quarter of the company at the moment it would ever be cheapest, I acquired a definition of success I hadn't chosen, and I spent, honestly, a third of my next two years managing the money instead of the business. Board decks, updates, the next round conversation that starts the morning after the wire lands."

She can live with the dilution, though she notes that the quarter she sold would today be worth about forty times what she got for it. Her regret sits somewhere subtler. "Before the round, the company had one boss: customers. After it, there were two, and they wanted different things. Customers wanted the product deeper. The money wanted the line steeper. Every argument in the company for two years was secretly those two bosses arguing." She built a good company anyway. But when younger founders ask her advice, she asks them one question first, and it is the right question: "What exactly would you do with the money? If the answer has the word 'faster' in it, fine, tell me faster at what. If the answer is 'runway,' what you have is a business problem, and money will only make it quieter for a while."

Here is the mechanical truth under Priya's receipt. An investor buys a permanent share of every future outcome, plus, usually, a say in how you pursue them. That is a plain description, and good investors give it to you themselves. The say is the part founders underweight. The money arrives with a metabolism: a fund needs its winners to be very large within roughly a decade, so the moment you take it, "a wonderful €3 million company that feeds twelve families forever" stops counting as success. If that sentence describes the company you want, and for most readers of this book it honestly might, then venture money is the wrong money for you, the way a wedding cake is the wrong daily bread. Match the money to the shape of the business: VC for the few businesses with genuine winner-take-most physics, bank debt and customer money for businesses with steady cash physics, and no shame anywhere on that spectrum.

And if you do raise, from angels or funds, three rules from Priya's receipt and my own observation deck. Raise before you need it, because the hungriest founders sign the worst terms. Raise for a named purpose with a named milestone, "this money takes us from 40 to 200 customers in 24 months," never for oxygen. And keep enough of the steering wheel that the company still goes where you point it; what a normal round costs is negotiable, what control costs is usually permanent. The details of valuations and exits belong to chapter fifteen, where we sell a company together, and I will not spoil that here.

A word about pitch decks, and only a word

If you go the investment route you will be asked for a deck, so here is the shortest useful briefing I can give. A pitch deck is ten to fifteen slides answering, in roughly this order: what problem, for whom, how you solve it, proof people pay, the size of the pond, how you win it, who you are, the numbers so far, what you are raising, and what the money makes true. That is the whole genre. Two warnings and we move on. First, evidence raises money; the deck is just the tray it arrives on, and a mediocre deck with revenue beats a beautiful deck with adjectives every single time. Second, the deck is a postcard of your actual plan, and if the plan underneath is stale or imaginary, investors can smell it through the slides, so do chapter seven's work first; that chapter covers the living business plan the deck should be a photograph of.

Making the Money Boring

We have covered a lot of machinery, so let me tell you what it is all for. The goal of managing money is to make money the most boring topic in your company. Boring means predicted. Boring means no surprises, no flour calls, no heroics. Companies where money is exciting are companies in trouble. The founders who seem calm about cash installed the small set of habits this section describes, early, and then never stopped.

The weekly money hour

One hour, same day every week, non-negotiable, phone face down. Jonas does Friday at two; I did Monday at eight, because I wanted the week to start from facts. The agenda never changes. Update the 13-week forecast against the real bank balance. Send every invoice that can be sent, today, because an invoice not yet sent is a payment term you are extending for free. Chase everything overdue the same morning, in writing, with a friendly note; you will be astonished how much of collections is just being the supplier who notices. Glance at the runway number. Done. On a normal week it takes forty minutes and you spend the spare twenty feeling smug, which is also part of the system.

The three accounts

Open two bank accounts next to your operating account, this week, it takes minutes. The first is the tax account: on the day any customer payment arrives, move a fixed percentage across, your bookkeeper will give you the right rate for your mix of VAT and profit tax, and for most small companies it lands between 20 and 30 percent. That money was never yours, so make the bank statement agree. The second is the buffer account, where you park your cash floor and, over time, a real reserve. My rule at the routing company, adopted after our own flour-call equivalent, was three months of gross burn in the buffer before we allowed ourselves any new recurring cost. It felt monastic. It also meant that when a top-three customer went bankrupt on us in a January, owing us five figures, the story was an annoyance instead of a chapter.

The people

Get a bookkeeper in your first month, long before it feels justified; a few hundred euros a month buys clean books, filed VAT, and the monthly P&L your five-question ritual needs. Add an accountant for the annual accounts and for tax decisions above your pay grade. And notice what stays yours: judgment. The bookkeeper records the past and the accountant referees it, but the 13-week forecast, the runway number, and the yellow-light decisions are the steering wheel, and the steering wheel stays in your hands, because the founder who cannot feel the cash cannot feel the company. The day will come, somewhere well past twenty people, when you need a real finance function with a controller and dashboards and someone who says "accruals" with a straight face, and that day belongs to chapter fourteen. Today, a bookkeeper and your own Friday hour are plenty.

The posture

Last habit, and it is a posture rather than a procedure: talk about money earlier than is comfortable, with everyone. With the supplier, before the invoice is overdue rather than after; the flour merchant who called Jonas had, it turned out, been ignored twice, and suppliers almost never pull delivery on a customer who calls first. With the bank, while you still have a year of runway rather than three months. With the tax office, before the deadline; every finance authority in Europe has a payment-plan procedure, and all of them work better when you arrive early. With your cofounder, monthly, numbers on the table, because money silence between founders compounds like debt, and chapter five already gave you the meeting to put it in. An account running low is Tuesday; it happens to everyone. Weakness is knowing and staying silent.

Years after the flour call, I told Henk the whole Jonas story over coffee, expecting him to enjoy the vindication, profit as opinion, cash as fact, his greatest hit performed by a baker. He listened, nodded, and then, being Henk, refused the applause and improved the song instead.

"You are all so dramatic about it," he said. "Money in a company is weather. You cannot choose it, but you can read the sky, you can keep a coat by the door, and you can stop acting surprised by rain in November." He drank some coffee. "You think your brother-in-law got good with money? He checks the sky on Fridays. That is the whole trick."

That is the entire chapter, compressed to a Henk. All it asks of you is a Friday.

The Short Version

  • Profit is an opinion, cash is a fact. Profit measures performance over an era; cash decides survival this week. You can be profitable, growing, and forty-eight hours from failure, all at once.
  • A euro moves through your company in three stages, spend, earn, receive, with gaps between them. The gaps are where companies die.
  • Read the P&L monthly with the five questions, and watch gross margin direction hardest. The P&L contains no answer to "can I pay Thursday's bill," and it never will.
  • Know the five hiding places: unpaid invoices, stock, prepayments, equipment, and money that was never yours. The VAT in your account belongs to the tax office; it only rests there between deadlines.
  • Growth eats cash before it feeds you. The fastest-growing quarter is the most dangerous one, and "overtrading" is the official name for growing yourself to death.
  • Build the 13-week cashflow forecast and update it every week at the same hour, forever. It converts emergencies into chores. Friday is the tool; the spreadsheet is where Friday writes things down.
  • Know your runway to the month, measured to a floor of one payroll plus one VAT payment, in the pessimistic movie. Act at six months. Everything takes longer than you think.
  • Customers are the cheapest financiers on earth. Squeeze the cash gap before you sell equity or rent money, and match any money you do take to the actual shape of your business.
  • Investment buys speed and sells the steering wheel. Raise before you need it, for a named milestone, or not at all. Evidence raises money; decks just carry it.
  • Three accounts, a bookkeeper from day one, and honest money conversations before they are comfortable. The goal is for money to be boring.
