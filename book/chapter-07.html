Chapter 7

Evolving the Business Plan

A business plan is a list of guesses with your name on it, and the only unforgivable thing is refusing to check which guesses came true.

The recipe app had a business plan. I want to be precise about this, because when people hear that Sam and I ran our company into the ground at twenty-two, they assume we were the kind of founders who never planned anything, two kids with a logo and a dream. The truth is more embarrassing. We planned beautifully. Once.

The plan was thirty-eight pages long. We wrote it over three consecutive weekends at Sam's kitchen table, fueled by supermarket energy drinks and the specific confidence of people who have never been billed for anything. It had a market analysis with a pie chart. It had a competitive landscape with our competitors arranged in a two-by-two grid where, by a remarkable coincidence, the top-right quadrant was empty except for us. It had financial projections that went out five years, month by month, in a spreadsheet so detailed it calculated our office coffee budget for a month in which we would no longer exist.

Page nineteen said we would have ten thousand users by Christmas. Page twenty-two said revenue would start in month six, from premium subscriptions at three euros a month, converting five percent of our users. Page thirty-one described our partnership with a national supermarket chain, written in a tense that suggested it had already happened. It had not happened. We had not contacted them. But the plan needed a growth engine, and the supermarket was a beautiful growth engine, so in it went.

We printed two copies at a copy shop and had them spiral bound, one for Sam, one for me. I remember the weight of it in my hand. It felt like the company, done, real, already achieved, and everything after that would just be typing it into existence.

Here is the part I need you to sit with. Over the following eighteen months, while the actual company lived and struggled and died, neither of us opened that document once.

Not once. I know this because of the dust, and because of what happened at the end. When we were packing up, selling the desks, doing the paperwork of a small honest failure, I found my copy in a drawer under a box of ethernet cables. And because I am the kind of person who picks at scabs, I sat on the floor and read it, cover to cover, while Sam carried boxes past me and pretended not to notice.

It described a company I had never worked at. That company had users who planned their meals a week in advance. Ours had users who opened the app twice and vanished. That company had six months of runway from subscriptions. Ours had made, in its entire life, three hundred and twelve euros. That company had a supermarket partnership. Ours had two unanswered emails. And the worst part, the part that still gets me: we had known all of it for over a year. Every number in that plan had already failed, one by one, and at no point did either of us say the sentence that would have saved us: the plan is wrong, so what do we know now, and what do we do about it?

The plan never even got the chance to fail. We wrote a prophecy, filed it, and ran the company on vibes and adrenaline, which are real forces but poor instruments.

This chapter is about the document Sam and I should have written instead, and, more importantly, about what you do with it after you write it. Because the writing is the cheap part. Everyone can write a plan. The rare and valuable act is rereading one.

What a Plan Is Actually For

There is an old soldier's line, usually pinned on Eisenhower, that plans are worthless but planning is everything. Like most quotes that survive, it survives because it is half right in a useful way. Here is the founder's version: the plan is a decision-making instrument, and it has exactly three jobs.

Job one: alignment. A written plan forces the people running the business to discover whether they are running the same business. This sounds absurd until you test it. Ask two cofounders to separately write down who the customer is, what the price will be in a year, and what gets built next quarter, then compare pages. I have watched this exercise end friendships, or rather, reveal that the friendship and the company had been diverging for months. Sam and I agreed on everything, right up until we didn't, and we had no document that would have surfaced the disagreement while it was still small. Chapter five was about getting the founding team together. The plan is one of the tools that keeps them together, because it converts assumptions from private beliefs into public statements that can be argued with over coffee instead of screamed about in a crisis.

Alignment gets more valuable, not less, as the company grows past the founders. The first employee who joins you is joining a plan she has mostly inferred from job interviews and lunch conversations. By employee eight, the company contains eight privately inferred plans, and they diverge exactly where it costs the most, on questions like which customers we say no to and what we protect when a quarter goes badly. You cannot fix that with speeches. You fix it with a page anyone can read in four minutes, because a plan that lives only in the founder's head has a staff of one, no matter what the payroll says.

Job two: memory. A plan records what you believed and why, at a moment in time. This matters because your memory betrays you, and it smiles while it does it. Six months from now, when the numbers are in, you will believe you always knew it would go this way. The plan is the witness that says otherwise. It lets you ask the only question that makes a founder smarter: what did I believe, what happened instead, and what does the gap teach me? Without the written record there is no gap, because you will edit your remembered beliefs to match the outcome. Everyone does this. It comes factory-installed. The plan is the workaround.

Job three: tripwires. A good plan tells you, in advance, what evidence would mean you are wrong. This is the job almost every business plan skips, because it is the uncomfortable one. Nobody wants to write the paragraph that begins "we will stop doing this if." But that paragraph is worth more than every pie chart you will ever produce, and it gets its own section later in this chapter.

Notice what is missing from that list. Impressing anyone. Completeness. Being right, which is fortunate, because the plan won't be. A plan is wrong the way a weather forecast is wrong, inevitably and forgivably, and like a forecast it stays useful as long as you keep issuing new ones. A forecast from last March is a historical document. So is your plan from last March, and most founders are running on one.

One boundary before we go on. The plan sits below the mission and the vision. If you skipped Part I, the short version: the mission is the job your company does in the world, the vision is the postcard from the future, and both live in chapters two and three, untangled once so we never have to do it again. Mission and vision change rarely, on the timescale of years. The plan changes constantly, on the timescale of months, and that difference in speed is the whole point. The vision is where you are going. The plan is your current best guess about the next stretch of road, and the road has opinions.

Likewise, if you did the work in chapter four, you already own a strategy one-pager: what you sell, to whom, and why they care. The plan we are about to build swallows that page whole and adds the parts strategy leaves out, namely the numbers, the guesses, and the tripwires. Strategy is the choice. The plan is the choice plus the bet plus the schedule for checking the bet.

How Plans Die

Before I show you the plan I want you to write, it helps to know what kills the ones people write today. I have seen four deaths, and I have administered at least two of them myself.

Death one: the drawer

This is the recipe app death, and it is by far the most common. You write the plan in a burst of energy, usually for an external occasion: a loan application, a subsidy, a course, a cofounder's nervous parents. The writing feels productive, and briefly it is. Then the occasion passes, the plan goes into a drawer, and the business happens to you in real time while the document that was supposed to steer it composts under the ethernet cables. The drawer death has one cause: a plan written with no next appointment. A plan without a review date is a diary entry about the future.

Death two: the shrine

Rarer, and more expensive. This founder rereads the plan, every week, with devotion, and treats every divergence between plan and reality as reality's mistake. The numbers are behind? We must push harder. The customers want something different? They misunderstand the product. The shrine death is sunk cost wearing a suit. It usually strikes people who spent so long writing the plan, or paid a consultant so much for it, that abandoning any part of it feels like burning money, and so they burn the company instead, which costs more but arrives on a later invoice. If you have ever heard someone in a doomed project say "we just need to execute," lower your voice, you are in a shrine.

Death three: the novel

Forty pages. Sixty pages. A man who wanted my admiration, and received my condolences, once showed me a business plan of a hundred and forty pages, with a table of contents that had its own table of contents. The novel death is subtle because the document is often good. Well researched, well written, internally consistent. It dies anyway, because nobody, including its author, can hold it in their head, and a plan that cannot be held in a head cannot steer a decision made at speed. A young company makes its decisions in hallways, in vans, on the phone at 17:40 with a supplier waiting. The plan that governs those moments is whatever fits in working memory. If the written plan is a novel, the real plan is the half-remembered summary of it, which means the real plan is unwritten, which means you are back in the drawer, just with better production values.

Death four: the costume

Some documents that look like plans are costumes, dressed up to be seen. The pitch deck you build when you raise money is the classic case, and I touched on it in chapter six, so I will say only this here: a document written to persuade outsiders obeys different physics than a document written to inform you. The costume shows the best plausible version. The plan shows the current honest version. You need both, at different moments, and the fatal move is letting the costume replace the plan, because then the company steers by its own advertising. I have watched founders raise money on a deck and then, having no other written artifact, start believing the deck. The deck says the market is enormous and the growth is inevitable, and it says so in the founder's own voice, and month by month the founder forgets he wrote it to be believed rather than because it was true.

Story

Years after the recipe app, over the second beer of a long evening, Sam told me something I had never known. Around month eight, he had started keeping a private spreadsheet. Actual signups against page nineteen. Actual revenue against page twenty-two. He updated it every Sunday night, alone, and watched the gap widen week after week, and never once showed it to me.

I asked him why not. He turned his glass around for a while. "Because the plan felt finished," he said. "It was bound. It had a cover. Bringing you the spreadsheet felt like telling you our house had subsidence. I kept thinking, one good month and the numbers come back to the plan, and then I never have to have the conversation."

Here is what floors me about this, still. We were best friends. We had no investors to disappoint, no board, no employees. There has never been a cheaper or safer setting in which to say "the plan is wrong," and the plan's own finished-ness defeated him anyway. That spiral binding cost us four euros and functioned as a padlock. Whatever format you choose for your plan, choose one that looks easy to change, because people, including you, take emotional instructions from documents. A laminated plan says do not touch. A page with crossings-out says argue with me.

Four deaths, one autopsy finding. In every case the document and the company stopped speaking to each other. Nobody consults the drawer plan, nobody corrects the shrine plan, nobody remembers the novel, and the costume never tells the truth. The cure is the same in all four cases: make the plan small enough to reread, cheap enough to change, and scheduled enough that rereading happens. Which brings us to the page.

The One-Page Plan

Here is my entire position on business plan length, formed over three companies and defensible at any dinner table: if it does not fit on one page, you are hiding from something.

That sounds like a productivity slogan, so let me earn it. The forty-page plan feels safer because every additional page feels like additional thinking. Usually the opposite is true. Length is where vagueness goes to hide. On page thirty-four you can write "we will pursue a multi-channel acquisition strategy" and it reads fine, because by page thirty-four the reader's standards have died of exhaustion. On a single page, surrounded by white space, that sentence stands there naked, and everyone can see it decides nothing. The discipline of one page works because a page has no room for sentences that do no work. Every hollow sentence gets evicted, and what remains is the actual plan, which for most young companies is about nine decisions and five guesses.

One page also fixes the working memory problem from the novel death. You can reread a page in four minutes, which means you will, which means it can be present in the Tuesday decisions where the company actually gets built. And you can rewrite a page in an evening, so updating it never feels like a project, so it might stay alive.

Here is the page. Ten blocks. I have filled in the example column for a bakery adding a lunch counter, because if this framework only worked for software it would not be worth your money, and because I want you to see how concrete the entries have to be. Yours should be this concrete or more so.

Read the example column again and notice its temperature. Nothing in it is inspiring. There is no mention of disrupting lunch, no addressable market for midday bread products in the Benelux, no journey. It is a page a sceptical brother-in-law could interrogate line by line, and that is the standard: every entry either survives the brother-in-law or gets rewritten until it does. I use a bakery on purpose, but I have watched the same ten blocks hold a software company planning its move upmarket, a design agency deciding whether to productize, and a one-person consultancy deciding whether to hire. The blocks do not care what you sell. They care whether you have decided.

Rules for filling it in

A few notes on the blocks, because the table is compact and the traps are not. And before the blocks, three writing rules for the whole page. Every claim gets a number or a date or both, because "soon" and "significant" are moods. No adjectives that a competitor would not also use, so "high-quality" and "innovative" are banned on grounds of meaning nothing. And write in full sentences, because bullet fragments let you feel finished without having decided anything. "Pricing: competitive" is a fragment hiding a decision. "Three lunches at 8.50 euros" is a decision, and someone can now disagree with it, which is the point of writing things down.

The bet comes first for a reason. Most plans open with the market, because market sections are easy and flattering. Yours opens with a falsifiable sentence, and everything below it exists to serve that sentence. If you cannot write block one, no amount of work on blocks two through ten will save you, and the missing raw material usually lives upstream: you have not really decided what job the company does or where it is going, and that is chapter two and three territory, go and come back, we will keep your seat.

Blocks two through five should feel familiar. If you built the product strategy in chapter four, they are imports from that work, compressed. The plan straps numbers and dates to the strategy you already chose.

Block seven is three numbers, not thirteen. The instinct to track everything is really the instinct to avoid deciding what matters. Pick the number that proves customers want it, the number that proves the unit economics work, and the number that tells you whether you will still be alive in six months. That last one is almost always cash, and chapter six already made you build the thirteen-week cashflow, so point to it, do not duplicate it.

Blocks eight and nine are the soul of the document. They are also the blocks that appear in almost no business plan written for outsiders, because they are lists of ways you might be wrong, and costumes do not advertise their seams. They each get their own section of this chapter, because they are each worth one.

And a note on format, learned from a four-euro spiral binding: keep it ugly. Mine, at the routing company, was a single page in a shared document with a visible edit history and, for two glorious years, a coffee stain that survived seventeen revisions. The ugliness was load-bearing. A document that looks provisional invites correction, and correction is the entire point. The moment someone offers to make your one-page plan look nice, thank them and refuse.

Try this

Tonight, no research allowed, write your one-page plan from memory with a pen. All ten blocks, forty-five minutes, honesty over polish. Where you cannot fill in a block, do not invent, write UNKNOWN in big letters. Then look at the pattern of unknowns. A blank block three means you do not know what problem you solve. A blank block six means chapter six is your next stop. A blank block nine means you have never said out loud what would make you quit. The unknowns are the most accurate map of your company you have ever drawn. If you have a cofounder, have them do it separately, and compare pages before you read any further. Comparing will sting. It stings less tonight than it will in a year.

The Load-Bearing Guesses

Every plan is a stack of assumptions, and the stack is doing more work than you think. That bakery plan above looks like a modest, sensible little scheme, and it stands on at least five guesses about the world, any one of which can drop it. Office workers will change a habit. Four minutes is achievable. The ovens have slack. The staff can switch modes. The landlord will play along. If assumption one is false, nothing else matters, and note the horrible detail: assumption one is the one the baker can least verify from inside the bakery, because from inside the bakery, everyone loves the bakery.

The recipe app stood on a stack too, and I can name it now with the merciless clarity of hindsight. Home cooks plan their meals in advance. People will type their own recipes into a phone. Five percent will pay three euros a month. A supermarket will partner with two unknown twenty-two-year-olds. The kitchen, mid-cooking, is a moment when people want to interact with an app. Five load-bearing assumptions. The plan treated every one as a fact. Not one survived contact with an actual human being, and here is the sentence I most need you to take from this chapter: we could have tested each of them in under two weeks for under a hundred euros, and instead we spent eighteen months and everything we had testing all five the expensive way, by building the entire company on top of them and standing back.

It helps to sort the stack into three grades, because they hide among each other and only one grade is dangerous. Facts are things you have verified with your own eyes or your own bank statements: the rent is 1,900 euros, the oven fits four trays, last month we invoiced 41,000. Estimates are numbers derived from facts with stated reasoning: about 1,200 office workers pass the window between noon and two, we counted for three lunchtimes and multiplied carefully. And hopes are statements about the future behavior of other people: they will change their habits, they will pay, they will renew, they will tell their friends. Every plan worth writing contains hopes. The danger is grade inflation, the process by which a hope, repeated in enough meetings, becomes an estimate, then a fact. Our supermarket partnership climbed through all three grades while we sent nobody a single email. When you fill in block eight, mark the grade of each entry, and watch the hopes hardest, because a hope is the one grade you can test but never simply look up.

Your plan contains guesses. It should; that is what a plan is. The practical question is which guesses to test first, and the answer has two dimensions: how hard the plan leans on the guess, and how sure you really are. Ignore the little assumptions and the near-certainties. Hunt the ones that are both load-bearing and shaky. Every plan has a danger corner, and most founders can name what lives in theirs in one sentence, the moment a friend asks them at a kitchen table. The trouble is that the plan never asks.

Once you know which corner a guess lives in, the follow-up discipline is the tracker. This is the second table of the chapter and the one I most wish someone had handed me at twenty-two, so I have filled it in as the recipe app's tracker, reconstructed the way it should have looked in our first month. Reading it still stings, which is how I know it is the right example.

Look at the totals at the bottom of that imaginary page. Five assumptions, all testable inside six weeks, for a combined cost of maybe two hundred euros and some dignity. The information was lying around, in kitchens and inboxes, free for the asking. We did not ask, and I want to be honest about why, because the why is the actual disease and the tracker is only the medicine. We did not test the assumptions because some part of us knew. Testing threatened the dream, and the dream was eighteen months from running out of money, so there was no urgency, and the plan, our beautiful bound plan, contained no mechanism to force the confrontation. A tracker with a date column is that mechanism. It converts "someday we should validate this" into "the supermarket test is overdue," and overdue is a feeling founders respond to.

Three rules for filling in your own tracker, then we move to the part where it gets uncomfortable.

Write the assumption as a sentence that can lose. "There is demand for premium features" cannot lose, it can only be debated. "Five percent of active users convert at three euros within sixty days of seeing the offer" can lose, on a date, in plain sight. If you cannot write the losable version, you have found something better than an assumption. You have found a place where the plan is made of fog.

The cheapest honest test, with the emphasis on honest. Cheap is easy, honest is the hard part. Asking your mother if she would use the app is cheap and dishonest. Watching a stranger try to use it costs a little more and cannot flatter you. The test must be able to come back negative, and you must decide, before running it, what negative looks like. A test that cannot fail is a ritual.

The last column is the whole point. A tracker where the "what we learned" column stays empty is a drawer document with better formatting. Fill it in even when the lesson is embarrassing, especially when it is embarrassing. That column, twelve months of it, is the most valuable education you will ever get at this price. It is also the raw material for every future plan you will write, because your next company's plan will lean on guesses too, and you will be a founder who knows what her guesses feel like from inside just before they collapse.

Kill Criteria, or Deciding While You Can Still Think

Now the uncomfortable part, block nine, the tripwires. A kill criterion is a sentence, written in advance, that names the evidence and the date on which you will stop doing something. Not slow down, not revisit, not take another look at the numbers. Stop.

You write it in advance because you are, right now, the smartest you will ever be about this decision. That sounds wrong, so let me defend it. Today, before the project launches, you have no sunk costs, no wounded pride, no team whose jobs depend on the answer, no years of your identity welded to the outcome. Today you can ask "what would failure look like?" as a neutral engineering question. Two years from now, the same question arrives as an accusation: "are you saying all that work was wasted?" and no useful answer survives that framing. So you make the decision now, while it is cheap, and you seal it in writing, and future-you, the compromised one, only has to check whether the tripwire fired. Judging evidence against a written line is a hundred times easier than drawing the line while the evidence is coming at you and everyone in the room has a salary that depends on where it lands.

Professional poker players do this. So do mountaineers, who call it a turnaround time: the summit is right there, but it is 14:00, and 14:00 was the line, and the mountain does not care that you are close. The graveyards on the big peaks are full of people who were forty-five minutes from the top. Businesses have the same graveyard, and the recipe app is buried in it. We were always forty-five minutes from the top. One more feature, one more month, one partnership away. Nobody had written down a 14:00, so every evening we decided, with total sincerity, to keep climbing.

Story

The routing company nearly grew its own recipe app, and a written line is what saved us. Around year five, flush with the confidence of a business that finally worked, we built a side product: a warehouse module, inventory and picking, sold to the same customers. It was my idea, which matters for what follows. Two developers, six months to build, and the sales team, who knew where their bread was buttered, agreed it was promising.

The only unusual thing we did, and I would love to claim it was wisdom rather than the residue of an old bruise, was write one sentence in the plan before the first line of code: if the module is not generating 8,000 euros in monthly recurring revenue by the end of the second quarter after launch, we stop, and the two developers go back to routing. I signed it. That detail turned out to matter more than the number.

The second quarter ended at 3,100 euros, most of it from two customers who I suspect bought it to be nice. And here is the thing: in the review meeting, I could feel the machinery of self-deception spinning up, right on schedule. The pipeline is building. Enterprise sales cycles are long. One big logistics client changes everything. Every sentence was technically true, and I was the founder and it was my idea, so no one was going to fight me hard. What saved us was that the argument ran between me and a sentence I had written before the first line of code, back when I could still think. The meeting took forty minutes. We killed it, moved the developers back, and wrote down what we had learned, which was that our customers trusted us with routes but bought warehouses from warehouse people, a lesson that later saved us from two similar ideas at zero cost.

A year afterwards, one of those two developers told me it was the moment she decided to stay long-term. I had expected the kill to look like weakness. From below deck, apparently, it looked like the captain reads the instruments.

I want to dwell on why "one more month" is so hard to refuse without a written line, because understanding the trap is half of avoiding it. Each individual month really is cheap. One more month of the warehouse module cost us perhaps fifteen thousand euros, and the possible payoff, in the story we told ourselves, was enormous, so as a single decision it always passed. The trap: "one more month" is a subscription. It renews on its own, and each renewal makes the next one easier to sign, because now there is more invested, more to justify, more embarrassment waiting on the far side of stopping. Nobody ever decides to waste two years on a zombie project. They decide to spend one more month, twenty-four times. A kill criterion cancels a subscription your future self will keep renewing, and it works because you only have to be wise once, in advance, instead of twenty-four times under pressure.

A kill criterion is a fire extinguisher, and writing one summons failure exactly as much as buying an extinguisher summons fire. I have heard founders refuse to write them because "we're not planning to fail," which is exactly as sensible as refusing the extinguisher because you are not planning to have a fire. The criterion leaves the odds alone. It changes what happens to you when the odds land badly: whether you lose one project or, one plausible Tuesday at a time, the whole company.

Keep kill criteria apart from change criteria; the distinction pays for itself. A kill criterion ends a bet. A change criterion adjusts a bet that is broadly working: a lunch counter clearing 300 euros a day at terrible margin needs a cheaper menu, not a funeral. Write both kinds into block nine. Most reviews trip change criteria, and that is healthy, that is the plan breathing. The kill criterion is the rare, load-bearing one, and its whole value is that it was never up for negotiation in the moment.

The failure mode when writing them is vagueness, because vagueness is painless now and pays out later, when the room can lawyer a vague criterion into meaning whatever the room needs. Here is what the difference looks like in practice.

Three craft notes. First, sign them. A criterion with your signature and a date on it has a completely different gravity in the later meeting than an anonymous bullet point, because the argument stops being between colleagues and becomes between you and your own earlier, clearer head. Second, set the review before the tripwire can fire, not after. A tripwire nobody checks is a drawer document with a fuse. Third, when one fires, the meeting has exactly three legal outcomes: stop, change, or, rarely and loudly, an explicit written decision to double down with a new tripwire behind it. What is never legal is the fourth outcome, the quiet one, where everyone agrees the number was "always a bit arbitrary" and the meeting moves to other business. Do that once and every tripwire you ever write afterwards is decoration.

Watch out

Kill criteria written after launch are alibis. Once the project is live and the money is spent, you will pick every number to be passable, and you will know it even if you never admit it. The entire value of the tripwire comes from placing it before you were in love. If you are reading this with a project already running and no lines written, you can still salvage something honest: ask someone with no stake in the project to draft the criterion, and agree in advance to accept their number. It stings. It is supposed to. It stings much less than year three of a zombie product.

The Loop: Plan, Act, Learn, Adjust

Everything so far, the page, the tracker, the tripwires, are parts. Here is the machine they belong to, and it is humbling how simple it is. You plan, you act, you learn, you adjust the plan, and around again. That is the entire technology. A living plan is this loop, running. The document is just the loop's memory between turns.

The diagram earns its place because of the stall points, so let me walk them, clockwise, with their symptoms, since you will meet them all eventually and it helps to recognize the room you are standing in.

Plan without act: the research hobby. This founder is on version eleven of the plan. The spreadsheet has scenario tabs. The market analysis is genuinely excellent. Revenue is zero, because planning has become a way of experiencing the pleasures of the business without the risks, like reading cookbooks in bed. The tell is that each new round of planning generates questions answerable only by more planning. The cure is brutal and cheerful: the next version of the plan may not be written until something in the current one has been tested against a stranger with money. If this is you, and you know if this is you, block ten of your one-pager should contain nothing but acts.

Act without learn: thrash. The opposite pole, and much better camouflaged, because it looks like virtue. Launches, campaigns, features, pivots, a new pricing page every month, the calendar is full and the sensation of progress is constant. But nothing is measured before the next thing starts, so nothing is ever known, and in twelve months the company has taken twelve initiatives and accumulated zero lessons. I once counted, with a founder who had asked me to look at his stalled agency, fourteen distinct marketing experiments in one year. When I asked which had worked, he said, and I am quoting, "it's hard to say, we were pretty busy." Fourteen experiments, zero findings. That is expensive fidgeting. The cure is the tracker: no act ships without a sentence saying what it is supposed to prove and when we will look.

Learn without adjust: the recipe app. I have nothing to add to the cold open except the diagnostic, which is worth having in clinical form. The information was in the building. Sam's Sunday spreadsheet existed. The knowledge was present in at least one human skull, and the plan never changed, because no mechanism forced the knowledge and the plan into the same room. If your company has someone in it who privately knows the plan is wrong, and most companies over five people do, the loop has stalled here. The fix is a standing meeting where you read the page aloud with the actuals beside it. Smarter people won't unstick it; the meeting will. Boring, unglamorous, company-saving.

Adjust without plan: drift. The subtlest stall. The company responds to everything, adjusts constantly, and after two years of nimble little corrections nobody can say what the current bet actually is. Ask three people in a drifting company what would make this year a success and you get three warm, incompatible answers. Drift feels like flexibility from the inside, which is what makes it dangerous. The difference between agile and lost is whether there is a written page you are deviating from on purpose.

How fast should the loop turn? As fast as your feedback arrives, and no faster. A bakery's lunch counter produces verdicts daily, sixty lunches sold or not sold, so its loop can turn weekly with a clear conscience. Enterprise software with four-month sales cycles cannot learn weekly no matter how many meetings it holds, and pretending otherwise just means adjusting to noise, which is thrash with paperwork. I have sat in a Monday meeting where a founder changed the pricing page because of one bad week, in a business where a customer's buying decision took a quarter. He was checking the oven every ninety seconds and wondering why the bread kept falling. Match the loop to the physics of your business: fast loops for fast feedback, patient loops for slow feedback, and for most founder-led companies a monthly glance and a quarterly turn is honest. And when the loop feels too slow, design cheaper, faster tests. Reviewing more often just measures the same silence twice; a faster test shortens the distance between acting and knowing. What the rhythm looks like in the calendar is the second-to-last section of this chapter.

Pivots Without Whiplash

Sooner or later the loop hands you a big one. Not a change criterion, not a menu tweak, but evidence against a load-bearing assumption, the kind the whole page stands on. The word for what happens next is pivot, and because the word has been fashionable for two decades it has gone soft, so let me restore its edges.

A pivot is a deliberate change to a load-bearing assumption, made because of evidence, keeping what you have learned and built. Every part of that sentence is doing work. Deliberate: you decide it in a meeting, write it down, and announce it. Load-bearing: changing your logo is a rebrand, and changing your prices is a price change, unless pricing was the bet. Because of evidence: a pivot responds to what the loop learned, which is what separates it from its two impostors. And keeping what you have learned: a pivot is the moment your expensive education gets cashed in. The customer relationships, the technical machinery, the hard-won knowledge of what people do at 17:40 in a supermarket, all of it carries forward into the new bet. If nothing carries forward, you have started a second company and given it the first one's name.

The two impostors deserve names, because from inside they both feel like pivoting. The first is the panic swerve: the numbers dip, a competitor launches, an important customer says something wounding at a trade fair, and by Friday the company has a new direction. No evidence beyond fright, nothing learned, nothing kept. The second is the boredom pivot, which is more dangerous because it afflicts good founders. Two years in, the original bet is grinding forward the way real bets do, and the founder is no longer learning anything new, and a shinier idea appears, and suddenly the plan has a bold new chapter. The tell is that the boredom pivot is always toward something more exciting, while evidence-driven pivots are usually toward something more boring: the narrower segment, the unglamorous use case, the customers who actually pay instead of the ones who applaud. If your pivot makes a better story at parties than your current business, audit it twice.

How do you know a real one is due? The honest answer is that the tracker tells you. When a danger-corner assumption comes back falsified, and you cannot find a cheaper adjustment that routes around it, the bet itself has to move. The discipline is to pivot on the assumption, not on the whole identity of the company. Sam and I, in the last desperate months, briefly discussed turning the recipe app into, in sequence, a food blog network, a meal-kit delivery service, and, for one long whiteboard evening I would pay money to un-remember, a dating app for people who cook. Notice the pattern: each idea abandoned everything, kept nothing, and responded to no specific piece of evidence, because we had never gathered any. We had courage to spare. We lacked the bookkeeping. A pivot is a precision instrument, and it needs to know exactly which assumption died, and our plan was a thirty-eight-page monolith in which every assumption was structural and none was labelled. When any part of it failed, the only sizes available were "ignore it" and "abandon everything," and we alternated between those two settings until the money ran out.

A plan you never reread is a souvenir of a company you once imagined.

When you decide a real pivot, one job remains, and skipping it undoes the rest: say it out loud, in plain words, to everyone the old bet touched. The team first, in one meeting, with the evidence on the wall, in the grammar of the tracker: here is what we believed, here is what we tested, here is what came back, here is the new bet and what carries over. People can follow a company that changes its mind for reasons. What corrodes them is the unannounced pivot, where nobody cancels the old plan, they just starve it, and the team has to deduce the new direction from which projects still get budget. Customers get a shorter version, and earlier than feels comfortable, especially the ones who bought into the old bet. At the routing company we called the two customers who had actually paid for the warehouse module before we told our own investors we were killing it, offered them a year of the module maintained for free while they migrated, and both were still customers of the core product five years later. The pivot itself cost us almost nothing in reputation. Being caught drifting would have cost plenty, because a pivot announced is a decision, and a pivot discovered is a secret.

There is one more thing about pivots that the frameworks never mention, and I will mention it because this book promised you the weather. A real pivot involves grief. The load-bearing assumption you are giving up was the reason you started, the thing you told your partner and your parents and yourself. Killing it hurts in a way that has nothing to do with economics, and founders who do not expect the grief mistake it for a signal that the pivot is wrong, and reverse course halfway, which is the worst of every world. Expect it. Budget a bad week. Say the old bet out loud, thank it for the education, and let the door close. Companies rarely die from changing the plan. They die from refusing to.

Story

Sam and I have a ritual. Once a year, whichever city we are both near, we have a dinner that we have privately called the shareholders' meeting for a company that has not existed for decades. A few years ago, over the second glass, I slid a single sheet of paper across the table. I had rewritten the recipe app's plan as a one-pager, the version this chapter teaches, with the tracker and the tripwires, exactly as we could have written it in month one with no information we did not already have.

Sam read it for a long time. Longer than one page needs. Then he said, "This kills the company by June." He meant the first summer, month nine or so, when the tests would all have come back negative and the tripwires would have fired one after another like a string of fireworks.

"Right," I said. "It kills the app by June. We had eighteen months of runway. This plan leaves nine of them on the table, plus everything we knew by then." We sat with that. Nine funded months, two founders who by that point understood home cooks better than anyone in the country, free to place a new bet with the education already paid for. There was a real business somewhere in what we had learned, and we never got to look for it, because the plan that could have killed the app in June did not exist, so instead the app killed the company in month eighteen, and very nearly the friendship in month nineteen.

Sam folded the page and put it in his jacket, which is the closest either of us gets to sentiment. "Cheap document," he said. "Expensive absence."

A Rhythm You Will Actually Keep

Everything in this chapter now comes down to whether it happens on a schedule, because none of it, not the page, not the tracker, not the tripwires, survives being left to inspiration. Nobody rereads a plan on impulse. It loses on any day it competes with anything, and in a company every day contains something, so the review must be an appointment, recurring, defended like a customer meeting. Here is the rhythm I settled on at the routing company after several years of doing it wrong, sized for a founder-led company that does not yet have a management layer.

The plan's calendar: four rituals, none of them optional
RitualHow oftenHow longThe only questions on the agenda
The glanceMonthly, same day each month30 minutesRead the page aloud with actuals beside it. Which of the three numbers moved? Which assumption looks weaker than it did last month? Is any tripwire close?
The rewriteQuarterlyHalf a dayRewrite the page from scratch, without looking at the old one. Then compare. Every difference is either learning or drift, and you must say which, out loud, for each.
The bonfireYearlyOne day, away from the buildingIf we started today, knowing everything we now know, is this the plan we would write? If not, why are we running it?
The tripwire reviewWithin a week of any tripwire firingOne hourStop, change, or explicitly double down with a new tripwire behind it. Plus one written sentence: what did this cost and what did it teach?

The quarterly rewrite deserves a word of defense, because rewriting from scratch sounds wasteful when you could just edit. The waste is the point. Editing a document inherits its structure, its emphasis, and its blind spots, and you will nudge numbers while preserving a shape that may no longer match the company. Writing the page blank forces this quarter's honest picture out of your head, and the comparison against the old page is where the real conversation lives. The blocks that come out nearly identical are your keel. The block that comes out unrecognizable is where the company changed while you were busy, and it is worth knowing whether it changed on purpose.

You will be tempted to skip the glance in a good month and in a terrible one, and both temptations deserve a word. In the good month, rereading the plan feels like bureaucracy, everything is working, why interrupt it. But good months are exactly when drift begins, because success changes what the company does before anyone updates what it says, and six good months of unexamined success is how you wake up one day running a business you never chose. In the terrible month, the glance feels unbearable, one more room where the bad numbers get to shout at you. Hold the meeting anyway, and shrink it if you must, ten minutes, one question: which assumption is the bad news actually attacking? A crisis examined through the plan becomes information. A crisis handled without the plan becomes mood, and companies steered by mood in bad months make their worst decisions at the worst prices. The months you most want to cancel the glance are the two kinds of month it exists for.

Two boundary markers so this chapter stays in its lane. First, this rhythm is deliberately minimal, a founder's metronome, four appointments and a page. As the team grows, it will graduate into a full operating cadence with scorecards and quarterly sessions and all the machinery, and that is chapter thirteen's territory, where we will also discuss when such machinery saves a company and when it curdles into theater. Second, none of the documents in this chapter is the one you show investors or the bank. That artifact, the pitch deck and its supporting cast, came up in chapter six where the money conversation lives. The costume gets tailored from the plan, never the reverse, and the tailoring is honest work, but this chapter's documents have an audience of exactly the people who must act on them: you, your cofounders, and later your leadership team. Write them accordingly, which is to say, with no adjectives you would not say to your own face.

Field note

Solo founders: the rituals work, but you will need to import the argument, because a plan reviewed alone drifts toward being marked by its own author. Find one outside person, a fellow founder, a retired operator, your own Henk, and send them the page quarterly with one request: ask me the three questions I least want to answer. Buy the coffee, take notes, do not defend yourself in the first ten minutes. This costs two hours a quarter and replaces roughly eighty percent of what people hope to get from an advisory board.

Try this

Open your calendar right now, mid-chapter, and create the monthly glance: thirty minutes, recurring, first Tuesday of the month or whatever survives contact with your life, titled "Is the plan still true?" Invite your cofounder if you have one. Then, and this is the part that makes it real, attach the one-page plan from the earlier exercise to the invitation itself, unknowns and all, so the document and the appointment can never lose each other. Total elapsed time, four minutes. You have just built the mechanism whose absence killed the recipe app, and it cost you less than the spiral binding.

The Plan Is a Conversation, Not a Monument

My copy of the recipe app plan is on the shelf behind my desk, spiral binding and all. I have moved offices five times since, and it always makes the cut, which tells you something about what it turned out to be worth, just not as a plan. Visitors sometimes pull it down, and it has become a small test of character: some people flip to the financial projections and laugh, kindly, at the five-year coffee budget, and some people go quiet at the same pages, because they recognize the handwriting. Not mine specifically. The species of handwriting. The plan written once, believed absolutely, and never spoken to again.

Because that is the reframe I want to leave you with. A plan is not a monument you build and then live beside. It is one half of a conversation, and the other party is reality, and reality, whatever else you can say about it, always shows up to the meeting. It replies to every plan, on schedule, in the form of numbers and customers and quiet Sundays where a cofounder updates a spreadsheet he cannot bring himself to show you. The only question, the entire question of this chapter, is whether you show up too. The founders who get called visionary later are, in my experience, mostly the ones who kept the appointment: who wrote down their guesses, checked them against the replies, and changed their minds in writing, over and over, for years, while their competitors filed prophecies in drawers.

Write the page. Name the guesses. Set the tripwires. Keep the appointment. And when reality tells you something you did not want to hear, remember that it is not grading you, it is negotiating with you, and it only negotiates with people who read their mail.

The Short Version

  • A business plan is a list of guesses with your name on it, not a prophecy. Its three jobs are alignment, memory, and tripwires.
  • Plans die four ways: the drawer, the shrine, the novel, and the costume. In every case, the document and the company stopped speaking.
  • Write it on one page, ten blocks, ugly on purpose. If it needs forty pages, length is where your vagueness is hiding.
  • Every plan stands on five to seven load-bearing assumptions. Write each as a sentence that can lose, and test the danger corner first: leans hard, barely sure.
  • The cheapest honest test wins. Most fatal assumptions can be tested in two weeks for a hundred euros. Building the whole company is also a test, just the most expensive one available.
  • Write kill criteria before you are in love, with a number, a date, and your signature. Afterwards, every number you pick will be secretly chosen to be passable.
  • The loop is plan, act, learn, adjust. Every stall has a name: research hobby, thrash, the recipe app, drift. Close the loop at the speed your feedback actually arrives.
  • A pivot changes a load-bearing assumption because of evidence, and keeps the learning. If nothing carries forward, or the new idea just sounds better at parties, it is not a pivot.
  • Put the review in the calendar: monthly glance, quarterly rewrite from scratch, yearly bonfire. A plan without a standing appointment is a diary entry about the future.
  • Reality does not read your plan. It just replies to it. Keep the appointment and read the replies.
