Chapter 9

Sales Approach

Selling is finding the people who already have the problem, then making yourself easy to buy from. Everything else in this chapter is how.

Priya called me from a parking garage in Stuttgart, and before I could say hello she said, "They loved it."

Some background, for those who skipped ahead. Priya is a former colleague of mine who left a comfortable job to found a B2B software company. Her product handles the paper trail that manufacturers drown in: supplier certificates, audit reports, quality documents, the folder called FINAL_v7 that decides whether a factory keeps its biggest customer. By the time of the parking garage call she had real customers, mid-sized firms paying her mid-sized amounts, deals closed in three weeks over two video calls and one nervous silence. She knew how to sell to them. She did not yet know that she knew, which is a different chapter, but she did.

And then a large industrial group, I will call them the gearbox people, invited her in. Fourteen factories, six countries, a headquarters with oak furniture and a visitor badge printer that took four minutes per badge. She gave the demo of her life to six people in a meeting room named after a mountain.

"They loved it," she said again, over the sound of the garage. "The head of quality said, and I quote, this would save us hundreds of hours."

I was happy for her, I said so, and then, because she had called me and not her mother, I asked three questions.

"Which of the six people can sign?"

A pause. "I think the head of quality? He did most of the talking."

"What did you agree happens next?"

"They're going to discuss it internally and get back to me."

"And what happens to them if they buy nothing at all?"

A longer pause. The garage hummed. Somewhere a barrier arm went up for somebody with fewer problems.

"You are ruining this for me," she said, which was fair, and also the point.

The gearbox deal took nine months to close and died three times on the way. There was a security questionnaire with 214 questions, one of which asked about her data center's flood defenses, interesting because her data center was a cloud provider and her office was on a hill. There was a procurement round in which a man named Falk asked for a discount with the calm of someone ordering coffee. There was a week in which the head of quality, her one true believer inside the building, updated his LinkedIn profile, and Priya slept badly. The deal closed. We will get to how, what it cost, and the one sentence Priya now says to every founder who asks her about enterprise sales.

But the chapter starts in that parking garage, because the call contains the whole disease. "They loved it" feels like a result. It is applause, and applause is free. No pipeline I have ever seen has a stage called love, and nobody has ever paid an invoice with enthusiasm. In the months after that call, Priya learned, and I re-learned alongside her, because teaching someone your scars is the cheapest way to check they have healed, that selling is a craft with parts. Finding the real problem. Finding the real buyer. Building a pipeline that tells you the truth. Saying a price out loud without flinching. And losing, sometimes, in a way you can be proud of. This chapter is those parts, in order.

Nobody Else Can Sell This Yet

Let me deal immediately with the sentence half of you are already muttering, because I muttered it too, for years: "I'm not a salesperson."

Correct. You are something worse: a founder, which means a salesperson with no script, no manager, no product marketing department, and a personal stake so large that a stranger's indifference can wreck your Tuesday. You are also, for the foreseeable future, the best salesperson your company will ever have. Possibly the only one it can afford.

The muttering comes from a picture: a man with too-white teeth who talks fast, calls you "my friend," and cares nothing for whether the thing he sells you works. That man exists, I have bought a car stereo from him, but he sells with pressure because pressure is all he has. Selling done well means helping a person with a problem decide whether your thing solves it, at a pace and in a language that works for them. That is the whole job. It sits closer to doctor than performer. The best salespeople I hired at the routing company asked questions like diagnosticians and took notes like journalists, and customers thanked them, sometimes in writing, for the experience of being sold to. Nobody thanks the car stereo man.

So the identity objection is a misunderstanding. A second objection, more practical, deserves a straight answer: "Can't I just hire someone who likes doing this?" No. Not yet, and the reasons matter.

First, in the early days your sales process is a series of adventures. A hired salesperson executes a process. Given adventures, they improvise, and they improvise toward whatever closed deals at their last company, which sold something else to someone else. When it fails, and it usually fails, you cannot tell whether the problem is the person, the pitch, the price, or the product, because you never learned to sell the thing yourself and have no baseline. You will have paid a year of salary to remain confused.

Second, early selling produces intelligence first and revenue second. Every conversation with a prospect tells you what the market actually hears when you describe your product, which objections repeat, which features nobody cares about, what people compare you to, and what they say when the price lands. Outsource the conversations and you outsource the learning, and the learning is the part your company cannot survive without. Revenue buys months. Learning buys direction.

Third, and I say this gently: a salesperson hired to spare you from selling is a hiding place with a salary. A real moment to build a sales team will come, with onboarding and territories and a commission plan that avoids rewarding chaos; that machinery lives in chapter fourteen. You earn that chapter by doing this one yourself until the process is boring. Boring means repeatable. Repeatable means teachable. Teachable means, finally, hireable.

A fair question at this point: sell to whom, exactly? If the previous chapter's reputation work is doing its job, some conversations arrive on their own, but early on, most will not, and the founder's first prospecting ground is nearly always the network, used honestly. Honestly means you skip the ambush: no pitching former colleagues over what was advertised as a coffee. You tell them the truth: "I've started a company that does X for people like Y, and I'm trying to learn whether the problem is as bad as I think. Can I ask how it works at your place?" Then you do exactly that, and only that. Some of those conversations turn into deals on their own, because if the person has the problem and you visibly understand it, they will ask what you charge before you have worked out how to raise the subject. The ones that stay conversations get the second most useful sentence in early sales: "Who do you know who struggles with this?" A specific ask for a specific kind of person, which people can answer, unlike "let me know if you hear of anything," a sentence shaped like a request but functioning as a goodbye. Ten honest conversations, each ending with that question, is a prospecting engine that costs nothing and lies far less than a landing page does.

One more thing before we move on, for the readers who sell croissants or kitchen renovations or design retainers rather than software, and who suspect this chapter was written over their heads. I wrote it for you too. If you run a bakery, you have a sales motion so smooth you have stopped seeing it: the window display is your marketing, the counter conversation is your discovery, and the moment a restaurant owner asks "could you do forty of these every morning?" is an enterprise deal walking in wearing an apron. If you run an agency, every proposal you write is a sales document and every "we'll think about it" is a pipeline stage. I draw the vocabulary from B2B because B2B makes the moving parts visible, the way a glass engine shows you combustion. The parts are in your business too.

The Founder's Unfair Advantages, and How to Waste Them

Founder-led selling, done right, is the strongest sales motion your company will ever run, because you walk into every conversation with advantages no hired rep will ever have. It also happens to be the cheapest.

You built the thing, so you can answer any question without checking. You own the roadmap, so you can say "we could build that by March" and mean it. You carry authority: when the founder shows up, the customer reads it as respect, and mid-sized European buyers will forgive a rough demo from a founder faster than a polished one from an account executive. You can decide on the spot, price, terms, exceptions, with no "let me check with my manager" theater. And you have the one thing that cannot be faked or trained: you care whether this works for them, and it shows, because your rent depends on it and your name is on it.

That is the good news. The bad news: founders waste these advantages in patterns so consistent I can list them like a menu.

Happy ears

The founder's ear is tuned to hope. A prospect says "this is really interesting" and the founder hears "we will buy this." A prospect says "send me some information" and the founder hears "we are close." A brutal translation service, free of charge: in most of Europe, "very interesting" means "I am being polite," "send me something" means "I would like this conversation to end pleasantly," and "we'll discuss it internally" means nothing whatsoever, it is verbal packaging foam. The only sounds that mean a deal is moving are specific ones: a date, a name, a number, an introduction to someone new, a question about implementation. Everything else is weather. Priya's parking garage call was one hundred percent weather, and it took three follow-up emails into silence before she accepted that.

Pitching too early

Because you love the product, you present the product, and because you present well, the meeting feels great. The feeling is the trap. A demo given before you understand the customer's problem is a lottery ticket: maybe you happened to show the four minutes they cared about, probably you did not, and either way you spent your one first impression teaching them about you instead of learning about them. An entire section on this is coming, because it is the most fixable expensive mistake in early selling.

Taking it personally

When you sell what you built, every no has your name in it. Founders respond in two bad ways: some stop selling, rearranging the calendar so that outreach never quite happens, and some start discounting, shaving the price at the first frown because the frown hurts. Both are the same wound. The fix is volume and bookkeeping, because I have never met a founder who managed to stop caring. When you have twelve conversations running, a no is data. When you have two, a no is a verdict. Keep enough conversations alive that no single one carries your self-worth, and write down why each no happened, because you can analyze a list of reasons, while a feeling of rejection just sits there.

Confusing interest with demand

People are interested in new things the way they are interested in documentaries: sincerely, and without any intention of paying. Demand is interest plus a problem plus money plus a deadline. A founder who cannot tell these apart fills their pipeline with documentary watchers and then wonders why nothing closes. The discovery section of this chapter is, at heart, a machine for telling them apart.

Field note

If you are an introvert, you may have been waiting for permission to skip this chapter. Denied, but here is something better: the people who study sales performance agree, and my own hiring backs them, that listening beats charisma in every sales motion longer than one conversation. This chapter teaches asking, listening, writing things down, and following up reliably. Introverts are often excellent at all four. The car stereo man could not sell industrial software to save his life. Charm expires in about forty minutes. Trust keeps.

Pick a Motion That Fits Your Business

Before you can improve your selling, you have to know which game you are playing, because the games have different rules, and the most common structural mistake I see is a business running the wrong motion for its price point.

A sales motion is the shape of how deals happen: how many conversations, with how many people, over how long, driven by whom. Arithmetic dictates the shape. Every hour you or anyone else spends selling costs money, so the size of the deal determines how much selling it can carry. A 4,000-euro deal cannot absorb ten meetings and a site visit; you would lose money on every win. A 90,000-euro deal cannot close through a payment link and a cheerful automated email; nobody wires that amount to a website. When the motion and the deal size disagree, one of them has to move.

Three notes on using this table honestly.

First, most businesses run two motions at once, and that is fine as long as you know it. The bakery runs walk-in at the counter and consultative for wholesale accounts. Priya ran transactional for her mid-sized customers and, with the gearbox people, wandered into enterprise still playing by transactional rules, which explains most of her first three months of pain. She kept expecting the deal to behave like her three-week deals, only larger. An enterprise deal is a different animal that happens to eat the same food.

Second, the motion decides your economics as much as your price does. Count what a sale costs you in hours, multiply by what your time should be worth, and hold it against the deal size. If a 3,000-euro project takes fifteen hours of unpaid selling, you are paying a twenty percent commission to yourself and calling it hustle. Raise the price, shrink the motion, or aim at bigger deals. Which of the three is right is a positioning question, and positioning lives back in chapter four; here I only insist on the arithmetic.

Third, where do the conversations come from? Mostly from the reputation work of the previous chapter, and I will leave it there, because marketing is chapter eight's job and this chapter picks up at the moment a human being is actually talking to you. What happens in that moment is discovery, and discovery is where most founders lose deals they never knew they were losing.

Discovery That Actually Discovers

Discovery is the unglamorous name for the most important conversation in any deal: the one where you find out whether a real opportunity exists and what it is made of. Done well, it looks like almost nothing. Two people talking, one asking most of the questions, the other doing most of the talking and, ideally, most of the discovering, because the strange secret of good discovery is that the customer usually learns more from it than you do. Plenty of buyers have never once explained their problem out loud, in order, with the costs attached. Asking them to do it is a service. Sometimes it is the service they remember when three quotes are on the table and yours is not the cheapest.

Done badly, discovery is a demo with a two-minute warm-up act. The founder asks "so, tell me a bit about your situation," listens for ninety seconds while visibly loading their pitch, says "great, that's exactly what we solve," and shares their screen. I have watched recordings of my own early sales calls, an experience I recommend the way I recommend cold showers. In one of them I asked a fleet manager four questions in fifty minutes, and one of the four was whether he could see my screen.

What are you trying to discover? Four things. Salespeople have stacked acronyms on this since the 1960s, but every framework I have used boils down to the same four, so I will give them to you without capital letters. Misery: is there a real problem, does it hurt, what does it cost in money, hours, risk, or sleep? Money: does budget for solving it exist, or could it be found, and what have they spent on this problem before? Mandate: who can actually say yes, who can say no, and is your enthusiastic contact either of those people? Moment: why now, what happens if they do nothing, is there a date on any of this? A deal has all four, or it is a hope. A hope might be worth nurturing, but it stays out of your pipeline, and it certainly stays out of your revenue forecast and your optimistic Sunday-evening thoughts.

Questions that earn their airtime

Here are the questions I actually use, and what each one is for. Do not read them out like a customs officer. Space them through a natural conversation, follow the interesting answers, and let silence do some of the work. But make sure that by the end of the conversation, you could answer most of the middle column.

Notice what the list leaves out: any question about your product. Discovery is the phase where you decide, together, whether a problem worth solving exists at a size worth paying for. The feature conversation comes later, and if discovery did its work, presenting your product becomes almost embarrassingly easy, because you answer, point by point, a problem the customer just finished describing in their own words. You demo four things instead of nineteen, and each of the four lands on a sentence they said.

Disqualify like you mean it

Here is the reframe that makes discovery emotionally survivable: go in trying, sincerely, to qualify the deal out. Attack your own opportunity. Hunt for the missing piece, the absent budget, the mandate that never existed, the "honestly, we'd probably be fine" behind the polite enthusiasm. This feels backwards to a founder who needs revenue, and the founders who need revenue most can least afford to skip it, because the scarcest resource in your company is your weeks. Every week spent courting a deal that was never real is a week taken from a deal that was, or from finding one.

Founders avoid the hard qualifying questions because the questions might kill the deal. Sit with that logic for a second: if a question can kill the deal, the deal was already dead, and the question is just the coroner. Asking "is there actually money for this?" in week two costs you nothing but an illusion. Discovering the same answer in month five costs you the months. The worst case of asking early is losing a deal you were always going to lose, minus the suspense. And a paradox works in your favor: buyers trust sellers who are visibly willing to walk away. The founder who asks blunt questions reads as someone with other options and a real business, which, usefully, is also what the questions are helping you build. Desperation is the only thing I know of that customers can smell through a video call.

The hardest skill is the pause

The mechanics of asking are simple. The discipline is in what you do after asking, which is nothing. Ask, then stop. The silence after a hard question feels, to a nervous founder, like a smoke alarm, and the instinct is to smother it: suggest possible answers, soften the question, answer it yourself. Every time you do this you buy comfort with information. The customer was three seconds away from telling you the true thing, the thing after the official answer, and you paid them to stop. Count to five in your head if you must. The best answers in every discovery call I have ever run arrived in second four.

Story

Priya's second meeting with the gearbox people was a fifty-minute demo to nine attendees, three more than last time, which she took as momentum. It was free entertainment. She walked them through every module. Heads nodded. Someone asked about single sign-on. Afterwards she could not answer a single new question about their situation: not what the document chaos cost them, not who owned the budget, not why this year instead of last year. Two weeks of silence followed, and the deal was quietly dying of politeness.

What she did next she still calls the hardest sales advice she ever took: she asked for one more meeting and went in with no laptop. No demo, no slides, a notebook and a pen. She told the head of quality, "I realised I've shown you a lot and understood very little. Can I ask you to walk me through what actually happens when a supplier document is wrong?" He talked for forty minutes. He drew their process on the whiteboard, all fourteen factories of it, and somewhere in the middle of the drawing he stopped, tapped the marker on one box, and said, "This is where we failed the Küpper audit." A customer audit, eight months earlier. It had cost them a contract worth more than Priya's entire company. Nobody had mentioned it in either previous meeting, because nobody mentions the wound while the vendor is performing.

The deal changed shape in that minute. Saving hundreds of hours was the polite version, the parking garage version. The real purchase was never again standing in front of a customer's auditor with a missing certificate. Priya's proposal, when it finally went in, opened with audit risk and mentioned saved hours on page three. The head of quality forwarded it to his board sponsor within an hour, because she had finally written a document he could get in trouble for ignoring.

That is the whole argument for discovery, told as one meeting. The demo meeting produced enthusiasm. The notebook meeting produced the deal. Enthusiasm is what people give you instead of information. Information is what closes.

Try this

In your next sales conversation, whatever you sell, forbid yourself statements for the first fifteen minutes. Only questions. Bring a pen, and write down, verbatim, the exact words the customer uses to describe their problem, not your translation of them. Then use their words, and only their words, in every email, proposal, and conversation that follows. When a proposal says "eliminate certificate chaos before the next customer audit" instead of "streamline document workflows," the customer recognizes their own voice, and people do not argue with their own voice.

A Pipeline That Tells You the Truth

Once you have more than a handful of conversations running, you need a pipeline. I want to be precise about that word, because software vendors have decorated it beyond recognition. A pipeline is a list of your open deals, each tagged with how far along it honestly is, what happens next, and when. That is all. It can live in a CRM, a spreadsheet, or, below ten deals, on a whiteboard. The tool is a detail. The truth is the point.

A pipeline exists to answer three questions you cannot answer from memory. Where is each deal really, as opposed to where I feel it is? What is the next concrete step for each one, and whose move is it? And is there enough in here, at honest odds, to pay for the next few months, or do I need to find more conversations this week rather than polish the ones I have? That third question saves companies. Deals take time, which makes the pipeline a weather forecast for your cashflow two or three months out, and the runway mathematics of chapter six is exactly why you cannot afford to discover a drought the month it arrives.

The failure mode is universal, and I say that as someone who has audited his own pipelines and winced. Left alone, a pipeline becomes a list of people who were nice to you. The friendly conversation from February still sits at "proposal stage" in July, not because anything moved but because deleting it feels like defeat. You start defining stages by what you did, "demo given," "proposal sent," instead of by what the customer did, so the pipeline records your activity while saying nothing about your prospects' intentions. Then one evening you multiply the hopeful numbers by the invented percentages, get a figure that feels like a salary, and relax, which is the most expensive feeling in sales.

A pipeline is a list of people with a problem, a budget, and a date. Everyone else in it was just being nice to you.

The cure is exit criteria. Define each stage by evidence, something the customer did or said that you could prove to a skeptical stranger, and let no deal advance without its evidence, however warm the last call felt. Here is a set that fits founder-led selling in a consultative or small-enterprise motion. Rename freely, but keep the spine: every stage earned by customer action, never by yours.

Two warnings about numbers, because pipelines invite arithmetic and early-stage arithmetic lies. First, with eight deals in play, conversion percentages are astrology. Two wins out of eight is two wins; calling it a twenty-five percent win rate dresses luck up as statistics. Percentages start whispering truth somewhere past thirty or forty closed outcomes; before that, read the stories, not the stats. Second, weight nothing by feel. A qualified deal is in or it is out. The moment you record a deal as "80% likely," you have invented a number whose only function is to make Sunday evening feel better.

What should the funnel look like between stages? Leaky. Honestly, healthily leaky, and mostly near the top, where leaking is cheap. Here is the funnel nobody prints on the sales software marketing page.

Read that diagram the way a plumber reads a wall. A big leak after conversations is healthy: it means you disqualify politely and early, and spend your hours on real deals. A big leak after proposals is expensive: a proposal takes a day to write, and if half of them vanish into silence, the fault usually sits two stages back, where misery, money, mandate, or moment went unverified and you proposed to a documentary watcher. And the leak labelled "doing nothing" deserves respect, because in most founder pipelines the status quo beats every named competitor combined. You will lose more often to a shrug than to a rival, which is why "what happens if you do nothing?" appeared in the discovery table and will keep appearing in this book.

A pipeline only tells the truth if you visit it, so give it a standing appointment. Mine was Friday afternoon, thirty minutes, coffee, and the same three questions for every deal: what did the customer do since last week, what is the next step and its date, and do I still believe this one, truly, or am I keeping it for company? Deals with three consecutive Fridays of "no customer movement" got either the honest email or the honest exit. The ritual matters more than the tooling, and it has a second function beyond hygiene: it forces the go-find-more decision. A rough rule of thumb wants three times your revenue target sitting in qualified pipeline, because roughly two thirds of even honest deals will leak. Hold the rule loosely at founder scale, your numbers are too small for the ratio to be science, but hold the question tightly: if everything in this list closed at believable rates, would it be enough? If the answer is no, the most important sales work this week is starting conversations you do not have yet. That work vanishes first when the calendar gets loud, because it has no meeting invite and nobody is waiting for it. Friday is when you catch it escaping.

I mentioned in the introduction the year the routing company's sales stalled and we tried to fix it with pipeline stages and commission plans, when the rot lived three chapters back, in who we were. I will not retell it, but I will extract the one lesson that belongs here: a pipeline is a measuring instrument, not a motor. When sales are sick, the pipeline shows you where the deals die, and that is real value, but the cure is almost never "more stages." The cure lives wherever the deals are dying, and sometimes that is nowhere near the sales chapter at all.

The Pricing Conversation

To be clear about lanes: what your product should cost, how to package it, whether you charge per seat or per loaf or per project, all of that is strategy, and chapter four settled it, or at least made you face it. This section is about something smaller and somehow harder: the moment in a live conversation when a human being asks "so what does this cost?" and you have to answer with your voice.

I have watched confident, competent founders fall apart in that moment in ways they never would in a product discussion. The number comes out wrapped in apology: "so, the price, and obviously we're flexible on this, and it really depends, but roughly, and again this is negotiable, it would be somewhere around..." By the time the number lands, the founder has told the buyer four times to ignore it. Buyers listen. Every softening word is an invitation to negotiate, and worse, a signal that you doubt the price yourself. If you doubt it, why on earth would they believe it?

The mechanics of doing it well are almost insultingly simple. Say the number in a full sentence with context on either side, then stop talking. "For your fourteen factories, the platform is 38,000 euros a year, and that includes onboarding and the audit module. Most customers see it pay for itself within the first audit cycle." Then silence. The same five-count from the discovery section, redeployed. The pause after a price feels geological. Let it. The buyer is doing arithmetic, comparing, deciding what to ask next, and every word you add while they think is a word spent negotiating against yourself. The first person to speak after a price usually concedes something. Know, at minimum, that the game has started.

Practice this out loud. I mean it. Stand in your kitchen and say your biggest price into the empty room until it sounds like a fact instead of a confession. Founders practice demos for hours and never once rehearse the twelve seconds where the money happens. The number will feel enormous to you, because you know what the product cost to build and you privately suspect you would never pay it yourself. Both feelings are irrelevant. The customer is buying the shrinkage of a problem, and if discovery did its job, you know exactly what that problem costs them, and your price is standing next to that figure looking modest.

Story

Early in the routing company's life, a mid-sized parcel carrier, our biggest prospect by far at the time, reached the pricing moment with me. The operations director looked at my quote, let a silence happen that I now recognize as standard, and raised one eyebrow. One eyebrow. I would like to tell you he applied pressure, made threats, invoked competitors. He raised an eyebrow, and I said, "Of course, for a partner like you we could look at something closer to..." and gave away forty percent. Unprompted. He had never asked for a discount. He told me later, once we knew each other well, that he had budgeted for my full price and the eyebrow was about his coffee.

Here is what the forty percent actually cost. More than forty percent; that alone would have been survivable. The renewal anchored to the discounted price, for years. The two referrals he sent arrived expecting his pricing, because in a small industry your prices talk to each other at trade fairs. And inside my own company, the discount taught me, and later my first sales hire, that our list price was fiction, which meant every future negotiation started from fear. It took three years and one very uncomfortable price increase letter to climb out of a hole I dug in four seconds to make an eyebrow go away.

The rule I carved into the wall afterwards: every discount is a trade. Money moves only when something moves the other way, a longer commitment, payment up front, a case study with their logo on it, a smaller scope. Trading squeezes out more margin, true, but the deeper reason is trust: trading is the only way the buyer can believe your prices. A price that collapses when an eyebrow moves was an opening bid all along, and once you have shown one opening bid, everything you say afterwards is an opening bid.

Discount requests will come regardless, and from enterprise procurement they come professionally, on schedule, from people like Priya's Falk, who ask for twenty percent the way other people ask for the time. Take it calmly: it is a ritual, the asker's bonus often depends on the concessions they extract, and your job is to let them win something you priced in advance. What matters is decoding the request, because "too expensive" is five different sentences, and your move depends on which one you just heard.

Two more tools for the twelve seconds, both cheap. The first is options. Where the work allows it, quote three versions: a lean one, the one you recommend, and a fuller one, each with a plain sentence about who it is for. The psychologists will tell you this works as a manipulation trick, and it does, but the honest reasons are better. It changes the question in the buyer's head from "yes or no" to "which." It gives the flincher somewhere to land besides the exit. And it gives procurement a concession path that spares your margin: the discount becomes "take the lean version" instead of "take twenty percent off." The agency quoting a rebrand and the software founder quoting a rollout can both do this. The baker mostly cannot, and the baker's pricing life is simpler for it.

The second tool is payment terms, which founders forget are part of the price. Half up front for project work is a normal ask, and the customers who resent it are running a signal up a flagpole for you. Annual prepayment against a modest consideration turns a software deal into a cashflow event. Deposits on large orders separate the serious from the curious better than any qualifying question. I will not redo the runway arithmetic, chapter six already made you cry about it, but a slightly smaller number that arrives now is often worth more to a young company than a slightly larger one that arrives in ninety days, after a reminder, and a second reminder, and a phone call you dreaded making. Terms are tradeable. Trade them like they are money, because they are.

One more word on the smaller end of the market, because pricing conversations happen far from enterprise too. The agency founder quoting a rebrand across a café table, and the baker naming a price for two hundred wedding pastries, face the same twelve seconds, usually with the customer physically present, which is harder. The mechanics hold. Say the number in a sentence with its context, stop talking, and when the customer flinches, resist the urge to rescue them. "I understand, it's a real investment. Shall I walk you through what's in it?" is a complete response. The flinch is part of buying. It was never an emergency.

Keeping Deals Alive Without Becoming a Pest

Between the great conversation and the signature stretches the middle of the deal, and the middle is where founder pipelines go to die quietly. No drama, no rival swooping in, no angry email. The deal just goes still, like a pond. Understand why and you can prevent it: your deal is one of your three most important things, and one of your buyer's forty-seven. The buyer means no malice. You have drifted down their list under the pressure of their actual job, and unless something specific and dated pulls the deal upward, it settles to the bottom like sand.

The single habit that prevents most of this costs nothing: never end any interaction, call, meeting, coffee, corridor chat, without a specific next step, a date, and a named owner. Not "I'll follow up in a couple of weeks," which is sand. Instead: "I'll send the revised proposal Thursday. Can we book thirty minutes Tuesday the 14th to walk through it while it's fresh?" If the buyer will not put anything on a calendar, that is information, and your pipeline should hear about it.

The proposal itself deserves a paragraph, because founders reliably misuse it. A proposal is a confirmation, not an exploration. It should contain, in the customer's own recorded words, the problem, the cost of the problem, what you will do about it, what success looks like, and the price they have already heard. Already heard: that phrase carries the whole rule. No customer should ever meet your price for the first time in a PDF. In a conversation you can watch the number land and handle what it stirs up. In a document the number lands alone, gets forwarded to people who never met you, and gets compared against things you cannot see. Say the price out loud first, always. The proposal exists so that your champion can sell you internally when you are out of the room, which is what champions do with their time. Write it for the people you have not met.

Watch out

If every relationship in a deal runs through one person, you have a hostage situation with pleasant weekly calls. Champions get reorganized, overruled, poached, and sent on parental leave. Priya spent a bad week watching her one gearbox believer polish his LinkedIn profile, with no second contact to call if he vanished. He stayed, she got lucky, and she never ran a deal single-threaded again. The fix is unglamorous: in every meeting, ask "who else should see this?" and collect introductions like the deal depends on them. Two contacts is a minimum. Three or more people who would notice if the deal died is a deal with its own pulse.

For bigger deals, the enterprise motion and the upper end of consultative, the next-step habit graduates into something more formal that earns its overhead: a shared plan. After the verbal yes, or even after a serious proposal meeting, you write one page, jointly owned, listing everything that has to happen between here and the product being live, with dates and owners on both sides. Security review, legal, signature, kickoff, training, the first real milestone. Two things make this document powerful. First, it converts "we'll get back to you" into a schedule both sides can see slipping, which makes slippage a topic instead of a mystery. Second, and better, it moves the conversation past the purchase to the outcome, and buyers who spend a meeting planning the rollout with you have, in every way that matters, already bought. Priya credits the gearbox deal's survival through procurement to one artifact: a shared timeline with the next audit date sitting at the bottom like a lighthouse, making every internal delay visibly expensive to the people causing it.

Then there is ghosting, the proposal that vanishes into silence. Here is the etiquette that preserves both the deal and your dignity. Follow up two or three times, spaced over a couple of weeks, and make each follow-up carry something: a relevant article, an answer to an open question, a customer result. Never the naked "just checking in," which asks the buyer to do your work of having a reason. If silence persists, send the closing email, which some salespeople call the breakup email: "I don't want to keep cluttering your inbox. I'm assuming this isn't a priority right now, so I'll close the file on my side. If things change, you know where I am, and I'd be glad to pick it up again." Two things happen. Often, nothing, and you have your answer and your hours back. But surprisingly often the reply arrives within the day, sometimes with an apology and a signature, because permission to say no, sincerely given, is so rare that buyers reward it. Either outcome beats month four of hopeful silence. The deadliest thing in your pipeline is the deal that neither closes nor dies, eating your attention and propping up your forecast, a zombie with a logo.

Try this

Tonight, with a pen, run the pipeline scrub. List every open deal. Next to each one, write three things: the customer's problem in their words, the agreed next step, and its date. Any deal missing one of the three gets exactly one honest email tomorrow morning to establish it. If the email gets no answer within two weeks, the deal moves to "no decision" with a reason logged. The first scrub typically shortens a founder's pipeline by a third and improves their mood by more, because a short true list beats a long flattering one in every way that pays rent.

Losing Gracefully

You will lose deals. Let me say it more precisely: if you qualify honestly and price with a spine, you should be losing deals, regularly, because a founder who wins everything is either lying to their pipeline or charging half of what the work is worth. So this section is about what losing is for. A lost deal, handled well, produces two assets: information and reputation. Handled badly, it produces a small enemy and a lesson you paid for but never collected.

Start with the moment of the no. Whatever you feel, and you will feel it in your stomach, because it is your product and your name, the reply is short and warm: thank them for the time, congratulate the choice if a competitor won, and leave the door visibly open. Do not argue with the corpse. Rebutting a decision already made has never once reversed one, and it converts a neutral outcome into a story the buyer tells about you at dinner. The vendor they chose this year may stumble next year, and when it does they will remember exactly one losing vendor: the gracious one or the bitter one, whichever you decided to be in a five-line email you wrote in four minutes.

Then, once the sting settles, ask for the debrief. Wait a week or two, outside the rejection thread, and frame it as a favor between professionals rather than a plea: "We're a small company and we learn the hard way. Would you give me fifteen minutes to hear honestly why we didn't win? No selling, I promise." Perhaps half will say yes, and what they tell you is worth more than most consulting you could buy, with one warning: treat the first stated reason as a curtain. "It came down to price" is the polite exit, because price is a no that needs no further conversation. Ask one gentle question behind it, "if the prices had been identical, who would have won?", and watch the real reason step out: they never quite believed a six-person company could support them, the other side knew their industry's jargon, the IT director had a preference all along and the process was theater. Log every reason. Six months of logged loss reasons will tell you more about your product, positioning, and pitch than any strategy offsite, and in the customer's voice.

Remember, too, that your most common loss will have no winner at all. "No decision," the buyer keeping the spreadsheet and the sighing, deserves its own postmortem question, because the polite explanation, "the timing wasn't right," hides a diagnosis you need. A no-decision loss usually means the pain was real but you never made the cost of doing nothing concrete enough to beat the effort of changing. That is a discovery failure, months old by the time it surfaces. Logging it as "timing" teaches you nothing; logging it as "never quantified the do-nothing cost" changes how you run the next first meeting.

And sometimes, be the one who ends it. The graceful loss has a proactive cousin: the graceful withdrawal. When discovery reveals a poor fit, a problem your product only half solves, a fantasy timeline, the strongest move is to say so and step back, ideally pointing them somewhere useful. It costs you a deal you were going to lose anyway, slowly and expensively, and it buys you the rarest asset in any market: the reputation of a seller whose yes means yes. People remember the vendor who talked them out of a purchase. They come back years later, with better problems, and they bring their friends.

Story

The same spring the gearbox deal was crawling through procurement, Priya lost a different deal, a mid-sized automotive supplier, to a competitor four times her size. The buyer, a quality director named Anneke, picked up the phone to tell her, which buyers almost never do, and Priya, exhausted and briefly free of strategic ambition, answered with plain honesty: thanks for calling, good choice given your global footprint, and one piece of unsolicited advice about migrating certificates out of the old system, based on where she had seen it go wrong. Then a two-line note the next morning wishing them a smooth rollout. Total investment: one phone call she did not enjoy and an email.

Two winters later, Anneke changed jobs, as ambitious quality directors do. Her new employer had the same document chaos and no vendor. She ran no comparison this time. She called Priya directly, opened with "you were so decent when we didn't pick you," and closed in five weeks at full list price. Priya, who tracks everything now, tells founders that her single highest-converting pipeline source, measured over years, is people who told her no the first time. Buyers move. Their memory of how you lost moves with them.

Which brings us back, finally, to Stuttgart. The gearbox deal closed in month nine, and closing looked nothing like the movies. Three factories instead of fourteen, a pilot scoped around the audit calendar, with expansion criteria written into the contract in plain language. Falk got his discount, four percent, traded against a three-year term and a reference visit, and seemed as satisfied with the ritual as with the result. Priya survived the security questionnaire with a freelance consultant and two long evenings. And the head of quality, her champion, stood up in the final meeting and presented the business case himself, from her proposal, in his own words, which by then were also her words, because she had taken them down in a notebook in the meeting where she finally stopped demoing. Priya called me afterwards, from what I choose to believe was the same parking garage. "They signed," she said, and then, before I could ask anything: "Three factories, signature verified, first invoice sent, and I know exactly why they bought." That is the sentence. The whole chapter in one breath, and she got there in nine months, which beat my own pace by roughly four years.

You Will Not Be the Only Salesperson Forever

Everything in this chapter assumes it is you on the calls, you writing the proposals, you saying the number into the silence. That season ends, and it should. A point will come, usually once selling competes with everything else for your calendar and loses, when the company needs salespeople beyond you, and a machine around them: hiring profiles, ramp plans, territories, commission structures that reward the behavior you actually want. That is chapter fourteen's territory, and I will not poach it. The general craft of hiring anyone at all is chapter ten, which is conveniently next.

But there is one thing you must do now, during the founder-selling season, because no future chapter can do it retroactively: write it down. Every time you notice what works, capture it somewhere unglamorous. The discovery questions that open people up. The exact phrases customers use for their problems, in their dialects, by industry. The objections that recur and the answers that landed. The loss reasons, logged. The stage definitions and their evidence. The pricing lines you rehearsed in your kitchen. None of it needs to be a manual yet; a running document with a terrible title will do. I called mine "sales stuff.doc" for six years. When the day comes to hire your first salesperson, that document is the difference between handing over a craft and handing over a hope. The founders who skip it stand behind their first sales hire six months in, wondering why the magic failed to transfer, never realizing the magic lived nowhere but their own reflexes.

And here is the quiet gift of doing this phase properly, the one nobody mentions at networking events. Founder-led selling is the last time you will be this close to your market with this little in between. No reports, no dashboards summarizing summaries, no account manager's interpretation. Just you, a notebook, and customer after customer telling you, in their own words, what your company is actually for. Some of what they tell you belongs in this chapter's pipeline. Some belongs three chapters back, in what you sell and who you are. Take the notes either way. Out there, closing deals, you are also running the cheapest, most honest market research your company will ever get, at the only moment in its life when the person hearing the answers can act on all of them.

The Short Version

  • You are the sales department, and for now you should be: early selling produces learning first and revenue second, and both die if you outsource the conversations. Scaling a sales team is chapter fourteen's problem.
  • "They loved it" is weather. A deal moves on specifics: a date, a name, a number, a new stakeholder, a question about implementation. Listen for those and discount the rest.
  • Pick the sales motion your deal size can afford, and notice when a deal from a different row walks in. An enterprise deal is a different animal that happens to eat the same food.
  • Discovery before demo, always. Verify misery, money, mandate, and moment, in the customer's own words, and write the words down verbatim. Their words close deals; yours decorate them.
  • Ask the question, then shut up. The real answer arrives around second four of the silence you were about to smother.
  • A pipeline is a list of people with a problem, a budget, and a date, where every stage is earned by customer evidence rather than your activity. Your biggest competitor in it is doing nothing.
  • Say the price in a full sentence, then stop talking. Rehearse it out loud until it sounds like a fact. The first person to speak after the number usually concedes.
  • Discounts are traded, never gifted. A price that collapses at a raised eyebrow was an opening bid all along, and it anchors every renewal, referral, and negotiation that follows.
  • End every interaction with a next step, a date, and an owner, and kill zombie deals with a clean breakup email. A short true pipeline beats a long flattering one.
  • Lose on purpose and lose warmly: log the real reason behind the polite one, and treat every graceful loss as a deposit. Buyers change jobs, and their memory of your no travels with them.
