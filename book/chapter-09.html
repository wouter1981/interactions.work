Chapter 9

Sales Approach

Selling is not convincing strangers to want what you have. It is finding the people who already have the problem, and making yourself easy to buy from.

Priya called me from a parking garage in Stuttgart, and before I could say hello she said, "They loved it."

Some background, for those who skipped ahead. Priya is a former colleague of mine who left a comfortable job to found a B2B software company. Her product handles the paper trail that manufacturers drown in: supplier certificates, audit reports, quality documents, the folder called FINAL_v7 that decides whether a factory keeps its biggest customer. By the time of the parking garage call she had real customers, mid-sized firms paying her mid-sized amounts, deals she closed in three weeks over two video calls and one nervous silence. She knew how to sell to them. She did not yet know that she knew, which is a different chapter, but she did.

And then a genuinely large industrial group, I will call them the gearbox people, invited her in. Fourteen factories, six countries, a headquarters with oak furniture and a visitor badge printer that took four minutes per badge. She gave the demo of her life to six people in a meeting room named after a mountain.

"They loved it," she said again, over the sound of the garage. "The head of quality said, and I quote, this would save us hundreds of hours."

I was very happy for her, and I said so, and then, because she had called me and not her mother, I asked three questions.

"Which of the six people can sign?"

A pause. "I think the head of quality? He did most of the talking."

"What did you agree happens next?"

"They're going to discuss it internally and get back to me."

"And what happens to them if they buy nothing at all?"

A longer pause. The garage hummed. Somewhere a barrier arm went up for somebody with fewer problems.

"You are ruining this for me," she said, which was fair, and also the point.

The gearbox deal took nine months to close. In those nine months it died three separate times. There was a security questionnaire with 214 questions, one of which asked about her data center's flood defenses, which was interesting because her data center was a cloud provider and her office was on a hill. There was a procurement round in which a man named Falk asked for a discount with the calm of someone ordering coffee. There was a week in which the head of quality, her one true believer inside the building, updated his LinkedIn profile, and Priya did not sleep well. The deal closed. We will get to how, and to what it cost, and to the one sentence Priya says now to every founder who asks her about enterprise sales.

But the chapter starts in that parking garage, because the parking garage call contains the whole disease. "They loved it" feels like a result. It is not a result. Love is not a stage in any pipeline I have ever seen. Nobody has ever paid an invoice with enthusiasm. In the months after that call, Priya learned, and I re-learned alongside her, because teaching someone your scars is the cheapest way to check they have healed, that selling is a craft with parts. Finding the real problem. Finding the real buyer. Building a pipeline that tells you the truth. Saying a price out loud without flinching. And losing, sometimes, in a way you can be proud of. This chapter is those parts, in order.

Nobody Else Can Sell This Yet

Let me deal immediately with the sentence half of you are already muttering, because I muttered it too, for years: "I'm not a salesperson."

Of course you are not. You are worse than a salesperson. You are a founder, which means you are a salesperson with no script, no manager, no product marketing department, and a personal emotional investment in the outcome so large that a stranger's indifference can wreck your Tuesday. And you are still, for the foreseeable future, the best salesperson your company will ever have. Possibly the only one it can afford.

The muttering comes from a picture. The picture is a man with too-white teeth who talks fast, calls you "my friend," and does not care whether the thing he sells you works. That man exists, I have bought a car stereo from him, but he is not what selling is. He is what selling looks like when it is done badly by someone with no better product than pressure. What selling actually is, when it is done well, is this: helping a person with a problem decide whether your thing solves it, at a pace and in a language that works for them. That is the whole job. It is closer to being a good doctor than a good performer. The best salespeople I ever hired at the routing company asked questions like diagnosticians and took notes like journalists, and the customers thanked them, sometimes in writing, for the experience of being sold to. Nobody thanks the car stereo man.

So the identity objection is a misunderstanding. But there is a second objection, more practical, that deserves a straight answer: "Can't I just hire someone who likes doing this?" No. Not yet, and the reasons matter.

First, in the early days you do not have a sales process, you have a series of adventures. A hired salesperson executes a process. Given adventures, they improvise, and they improvise toward whatever closed deals at their last company, which sold something else to someone else. When it fails, and it usually fails, you will not be able to tell whether the problem is the person, the pitch, the price, or the product, because you never learned to sell the thing yourself and have no baseline. You will have paid a year of salary to remain confused.

Second, early selling is not primarily revenue. It is intelligence. Every conversation with a prospect tells you what the market actually hears when you describe your product, which objections repeat, which features nobody cares about, what people compare you to, and what they say when the price lands. If you outsource the conversations, you outsource the learning, and the learning is the part your company cannot survive without. Revenue buys months. Learning buys direction.

Third, and I say this gently: a salesperson hired to spare you from selling is not a hire, it is a hiding place. There will come a real moment to build a sales team, with onboarding and territories and a commission plan that does not accidentally reward chaos, and that moment has its own machinery, which lives in chapter fourteen. The way you earn that chapter is by doing this one yourself until the process is boring. Boring means repeatable. Repeatable means teachable. Teachable means, finally, hireable.

A fair question at this point: sell to whom, exactly? If the previous chapter's reputation work is doing its job, some conversations arrive on their own, but early on, most will not, and the founder's first prospecting ground is nearly always the network, used honestly. Honestly means you do not ambush your former colleagues with a pitch over what was advertised as a coffee. You tell them the truth: "I've started a company that does X for people like Y, and I'm trying to learn whether the problem is as bad as I think. Can I ask you about how it works at your place?" Then you do exactly that, and only that. Some of those conversations turn into deals on their own, without your help, because if the person has the problem and you visibly understand it, they will ask you what you charge before you have worked out how to raise the subject. The ones that do not turn into deals get the second most useful sentence in early sales: "Who do you know who struggles with this?" A specific ask for a specific kind of person, which people can actually answer, unlike "let me know if you hear of anything," which is a sentence shaped like a request but functioning as a goodbye. Ten honest conversations, each ending with that question, is a prospecting engine that costs nothing and lies to you far less than a landing page does.

One more thing before we move on, for the readers who sell croissants or kitchen renovations or design retainers rather than software, and who suspect this chapter was written over their heads. It was not. If you run a bakery, you have a sales motion, it is just so smooth you have stopped seeing it: the window display is your marketing, the counter conversation is your discovery, the moment a restaurant owner asks "could you do forty of these every morning?" is an enterprise deal walking in wearing an apron. If you run an agency, every proposal you write is a sales document and every "we'll think about it" is a pipeline stage. The vocabulary in this chapter is drawn from B2B because B2B makes the moving parts visible, the way a glass engine shows you combustion. The parts are in your business too.

The Founder's Unfair Advantages, and How to Waste Them

Founder-led selling is not just the cheapest option. Done right, it is the strongest sales motion your company will ever run, because you walk into every conversation with advantages no hired rep will ever have.

You built the thing, so you can answer any question without checking. You own the roadmap, so you can say "we could build that by March" and mean it. You carry authority, because when the founder shows up, the customer reads it as respect, and mid-sized European buyers in particular will forgive a rough demo from a founder faster than a polished one from an account executive. You can decide on the spot: price, terms, exceptions, all of it, no "let me check with my manager" theater. And you have the one thing that cannot be faked or trained, which is that you visibly, sincerely care whether this works for them, because your rent depends on it and your name is on it.

That is the good news. The bad news is that founders waste these advantages in patterns so consistent I can list them like a menu.

Happy ears

The founder's ear is tuned to hope. A prospect says "this is really interesting" and the founder hears "we will buy this." A prospect says "send me some information" and the founder hears "we are close." I want to offer you a brutal translation service, free of charge: in most of Europe, "very interesting" means "I am being polite," "send me something" means "I would like this conversation to end pleasantly," and "we'll discuss it internally" means nothing whatsoever, it is verbal packaging foam. The only sounds that mean a deal is progressing are specific ones: a date, a name, a number, an introduction to someone new, a question about implementation. Everything else is weather. Priya's parking garage call was one hundred percent weather, and it took her three follow-up emails into silence to accept that.

Pitching too early

Because you love the product, you present the product, and because you present well, the meeting feels great. Feeling great is the trap. A demo given before you understand the customer's problem is a lottery ticket: maybe you happened to show the four minutes they cared about, probably you did not, and either way you have spent your one first impression teaching them about you instead of learning about them. There is an entire section on this coming, because it is the most fixable expensive mistake in early selling.

Taking it personally

When you sell what you built, every no has your name in it. Founders respond to this in two bad ways: some stop selling, quietly, rearranging their calendar so that outreach never quite happens, and some start discounting, shaving the price at the first frown because the frown hurts. Both are the same wound. The fix is not a thicker skin, I have never met a founder who genuinely stopped caring, the fix is volume and bookkeeping. When you have twelve conversations running, a no is data. When you have two, a no is a verdict. Keep enough conversations alive that no single one carries your self-worth, and write down why each no happened, because a list of reasons is analyzable and a feeling of rejection is not.

Confusing interest with demand

People are interested in new things the way they are interested in documentaries: sincerely, and without any intention of paying. Demand is interest plus a problem plus money plus a deadline. A founder who cannot tell these apart fills their pipeline with documentary watchers and then wonders why nothing closes. The discovery section of this chapter is, at heart, a machine for telling them apart.

Field note

If you are an introvert, you may have been waiting for permission to skip this chapter. Denied, but here is something better: the modern consensus among people who study sales performance, and my own hiring experience, is that listening beats charisma in every sales motion longer than one conversation. The skills this chapter teaches are asking, listening, writing things down, and following up reliably. Introverts are frequently excellent at all four. The car stereo man, for what it is worth, could not sell industrial software to save his life. Charm expires in about forty minutes. Trust does not.

Pick a Motion That Fits Your Business

Before you can improve your selling, you have to know which game you are playing, because the games have different rules, and the most common structural mistake I see is a business running the wrong motion for its price point.

A sales motion is the shape of how deals happen: how many conversations, with how many people, over how long, driven by whom. The shape is not a matter of taste. It is dictated by arithmetic. Every hour you or anyone else spends selling costs money, so the size of the deal determines how much selling it can carry. A 4,000-euro deal cannot absorb ten meetings and a site visit; you would lose money on every win. A 90,000-euro deal cannot be closed with a payment link and a cheerful automated email; nobody wires that amount to a website. When the motion and the deal size disagree, one of them has to move.

Three notes on using this table honestly.

First, most businesses run two motions at once, and that is fine as long as you know it. The bakery runs walk-in at the counter and consultative for wholesale accounts. Priya ran transactional for her mid-sized customers and, with the gearbox people, wandered into enterprise wearing transactional shoes, which explains most of her first three months of pain. She kept expecting the deal to behave like her three-week deals, only larger. It is not a larger version of the same animal. It is a different animal that happens to eat the same food.

Second, the motion decides your economics as much as your price does. Count what a sale costs you in hours, multiply by what your time should be worth, and hold it against the deal size. If a 3,000-euro project takes fifteen hours of unpaid selling, you are paying a twenty percent commission to yourself and calling it hustle. Either raise the price, shrink the motion, or aim at bigger deals. Which of those three is right is a positioning question, and positioning lives back in chapter four; here I only insist that you do the arithmetic.

Third, where do the conversations come from in the first place? Mostly from the reputation and visibility work of the previous chapter, and I will leave it there, because marketing is chapter eight's job and this chapter picks up at the moment a human being is actually talking to you. What happens in that moment is discovery, and discovery is where most founders lose deals they never knew they were losing.

Discovery That Actually Discovers

Discovery is the unglamorous name for the most important conversation in any deal: the one where you find out whether a real opportunity exists and what it is made of. Done well, it looks like almost nothing. Two people talking, one of them asking most of the questions, the other one doing most of the talking and, ideally, most of the discovering, because the strange secret of good discovery is that the customer usually learns more from it than you do. Plenty of buyers have never once been asked to explain their problem out loud, in order, with the costs attached. Asking them to do it is a service. Sometimes it is the service they remember when three quotes are on the table and yours is not the cheapest.

Done badly, discovery is a demo with a two-minute warm-up act. The founder asks "so, tell me a bit about your situation," listens for ninety seconds while visibly loading their pitch, says "great, that's exactly what we solve," and shares their screen. I have watched recordings of my own early sales calls at the routing company, an experience I recommend the way I recommend cold showers, and in one of them I asked a fleet manager four questions in fifty minutes, and one of the four was whether he could see my screen.

What are you actually trying to discover? Four things. Salespeople have stacked up acronyms for this since the 1960s, and you can go read them, but every framework I have ever used boils down to the same four, and I will give them to you without capital letters. Misery: is there a real problem, does it hurt, what does it cost in money, hours, risk, or sleep? Money: does budget for solving it exist, or could it be created, and what has been spent on this problem before? Mandate: who can actually say yes, who can say no, and is your enthusiastic contact either of those people? Moment: why now, what happens if they do nothing, is there a date on any of this? A deal has all four or it is not a deal. It might become one, it might be worth nurturing, but it does not belong in your pipeline, and it absolutely does not belong in your revenue forecast or your optimistic Sunday-evening thoughts.

Questions that earn their airtime

Here are the questions I actually use, and what each one is for. Do not read them out like a customs officer. Space them through a natural conversation, follow the interesting answers, and let silence do some of the work. But make sure that by the end of the conversation, you could answer most of the middle column.

Notice what is not on the list: any question about your product. Discovery is not the phase where you check which features they want. It is the phase where you decide, together, whether a problem worth solving exists at a size worth paying for. If it does, presenting your product afterwards becomes almost embarrassingly easy, because you are no longer presenting features, you are answering, point by point, a problem the customer just finished describing in their own words. You demo four things instead of nineteen, and each of the four lands on a sentence they said.

The hardest skill is the pause

The mechanics of asking are simple. The discipline is in what you do after asking, which is nothing. Ask, then stop. The silence after a hard question feels, to a nervous founder, like a smoke alarm, and the instinct is to smother it: to suggest possible answers, to soften the question, to answer it yourself. Every time you do this you buy comfort with information. The customer was three seconds away from telling you the true thing, the thing after the official answer, and you paid them to stop. Count to five in your head if you must. The best answers in every discovery call I have ever run arrived in second four.

Story

Priya's second meeting with the gearbox people was a fifty-minute demo to nine attendees, three more than last time, which she took as momentum. It was not momentum, it was free entertainment. She walked them through every module. Heads nodded. Someone asked about single sign-on. Afterwards she could not answer a single new question about their situation: not what the document chaos cost them, not who owned the budget, not why this year and not last year. Two weeks of silence followed, and the deal was quietly dying of politeness.

What she did next she still describes as the hardest sales advice she ever took: she asked for one more meeting and went in with no laptop. No demo, no slides, a notebook and a pen. She told the head of quality, honestly, "I realised I've shown you a lot and understood very little. Can I ask you to walk me through what actually happens when a supplier document is wrong?" He talked for forty minutes. He drew their process on the whiteboard, all fourteen factories of it, and somewhere in the middle of the drawing he stopped, tapped the marker on one box, and said quietly, "This is where we failed the Küpper audit." A customer audit, eight months earlier. It had cost them a contract worth more than Priya's entire company. Nobody had mentioned it in either previous meeting, because nobody mentions the wound while the vendor is performing.

The deal changed shape in that minute. It was never about saving hundreds of hours, that was the polite version. It was about never again standing in front of a customer's auditor with a missing certificate. Priya's proposal, when it finally went in, opened with audit risk and mentioned saved hours on page three. The head of quality forwarded it to his board sponsor within an hour, because she had finally written a document he could get in trouble for ignoring.

That is the whole argument for discovery, told as one meeting. The demo meeting produced enthusiasm. The notebook meeting produced the deal. Enthusiasm is what people give you instead of information. Information is what closes.

Try this

In your next sales conversation, whatever you sell, forbid yourself from making statements for the first fifteen minutes. Only questions. Bring a pen, and write down, verbatim, the exact words the customer uses to describe their problem, not your translation of them. Then use their words, and only their words, in every email, proposal, and conversation that follows. When a proposal says "eliminate certificate chaos before the next customer audit" instead of "streamline document workflows," the customer recognizes their own voice, and people do not argue with their own voice.

A Pipeline That Tells You the Truth

Once you have more than a handful of conversations running, you need a pipeline. I want to be precise about what that word means, because software vendors have decorated it beyond recognition. A pipeline is a list of your open deals, each tagged with how far along it honestly is, what happens next, and when. That is all. It can live in a CRM, a spreadsheet, or, below ten deals, on a whiteboard. The tool is a detail. The truth-telling is the point.

A pipeline exists to answer three questions you cannot answer from memory. Where is each deal really, as opposed to where I feel it is? What is the next concrete step for each one, and whose move is it? And is there enough in here, at honest odds, to pay for the next few months, or do I need to go find more conversations this week rather than polish the ones I have? That third question is the one that saves companies. Deals take time, which means the pipeline is a weather forecast for your cashflow two or three months out, and the runway mathematics of chapter six is exactly why you cannot afford to discover a drought the month it arrives.

The failure mode is universal, and I say that with the confidence of someone who has audited his own pipelines and winced. Left alone, a pipeline becomes a list of people who were nice to you. The friendly conversation from February is still in there at "proposal stage" in July, not because anything has moved but because deleting it feels like defeat. Stages get defined by what you did, "demo given," "proposal sent," instead of by what the customer did, and so the pipeline dutifully records your activity while telling you nothing about your prospects' intentions. Then one evening you multiply the column of hopeful numbers by the column of invented percentages, get a figure that feels like a salary, and relax, which is the most expensive feeling in sales.

A pipeline is not a list of people who were nice to you. It is a list of people with a problem, a budget, and a date.

The cure is exit criteria. Each stage is defined by evidence, something the customer did or said that you could prove to a skeptical stranger, and a deal does not advance without its evidence, no matter how warm the last call felt. Here is a set that fits founder-led selling in a consultative or small-enterprise motion. Rename freely, but keep the spine: every stage earned by customer action, never by yours.

Two warnings about numbers, because pipelines invite arithmetic and early-stage arithmetic lies. First, with eight deals in play, conversion percentages are astrology. You do not have a twenty-five percent win rate, you have two wins, and the difference matters when you are deciding whether the process works or you have simply been lucky twice. Percentages start whispering truth somewhere past thirty or forty closed outcomes; before that, read the stories, not the stats. Second, weight nothing by feel. A qualified deal is in or it is out. The moment you let yourself record a deal as "80% likely," you have invented a number whose only function is to make Sunday evening feel better.

What should the funnel look like between stages? Leaky. Honestly, healthily leaky, and mostly near the top, which is where leaking is cheap. Here is the funnel nobody prints on the sales software marketing page.

Read that diagram the way a plumber reads a wall. A big leak after conversations is healthy: it means you are disqualifying politely and early, spending your hours on real deals. A big leak after proposals is expensive: proposals take a day to write, and if half of them vanish into silence, the real fault is usually two stages back, where misery, money, mandate, or moment went unverified and you proposed to a documentary watcher. And the leak labelled "doing nothing" deserves special respect, because in most founder pipelines the status quo beats every named competitor combined. You will spend more of your selling life losing to a shrug than to a rival, which is why "what happens if you do nothing?" appeared in the discovery table and will keep appearing in this book.

I mentioned in the introduction the year the routing company's sales stalled and we tried to fix it with pipeline stages and commission plans, when the rot was actually three chapters back, in who we were. I will not retell it, but I will extract the one lesson that belongs here: a pipeline is a measuring instrument, not a motor. When sales are sick, the pipeline shows you where the deals die, and that is genuinely valuable, but the cure is almost never "more stages." The cure lives wherever the deals are dying, and sometimes that is not in the sales chapter at all.

The Pricing Conversation

To be clear about lanes: what your product should cost, how it should be packaged, whether you charge per seat or per loaf or per project, all of that is strategy, and it was settled, or at least confronted, back in chapter four. This section is about something smaller and somehow harder: the moment in a live conversation when a human being asks "so what does this cost?" and you have to answer with your voice.

I have watched confident, competent founders fall apart in that moment in ways they would never fall apart in a product discussion. The number comes out wrapped in apology: "so, the price, and obviously we're flexible on this, and it really depends, but roughly, and again this is negotiable, it would be somewhere around..." By the time the number finally lands, the founder has told the buyer four times that the number is not real. Buyers listen. Every softening word is an invitation to negotiate, and worse, it is a signal that you do not believe the price yourself, and if you do not believe it, why on earth would they?

The mechanics of doing it well are almost insultingly simple. Say the number in a full sentence with context on either side, then stop talking. "For your fourteen factories, the platform is 38,000 euros a year, and that includes onboarding and the audit module. Most customers see it pay for itself within the first audit cycle." Then silence. The same five-count silence from the discovery section, redeployed. The pause after a price feels geological. Let it. The buyer is doing arithmetic, comparing, deciding what to ask next, and every word you add while they think is a word spent negotiating against yourself. The first person to speak after a price is quoted usually concedes something. Make sure you at least know that game is being played.

Practice this out loud. I am entirely serious. Stand in your kitchen and say your biggest price into the empty room until it sounds like a fact instead of a confession. Founders practice demos for hours and never once rehearse the twelve seconds where the money happens. The number will feel enormous to you, because you know what the product cost to build and you privately suspect you would not pay that yourself. Irrelevant, both feelings. The customer is not buying your costs. They are buying the shrinkage of a problem, and if discovery did its job, you know exactly what that problem costs them and your price is standing next to that figure looking modest.

Story

Early in the routing company's life, a mid-sized parcel carrier, our biggest prospect by far at the time, got to the pricing moment with me. The operations director looked at my quote, let a silence happen that I now recognize as completely standard, and raised one eyebrow. One eyebrow. I would like to tell you he applied pressure, made threats, invoked competitors. He raised an eyebrow, and I said, "Of course, for a partner like you we could look at something closer to..." and gave away forty percent. Unprompted. He had not asked for a discount. He later told me, once we knew each other well, that he had budgeted for my full price and his eyebrow had meant something about his coffee.

Here is what the forty percent actually cost. Not forty percent, that would have been survivable. The renewal anchored to the discounted price, for years. The two referrals he generously sent arrived expecting his pricing, because in a small industry your prices talk to each other at trade fairs. And inside my own company, the discount taught me, and later my first sales hire, that our list price was fiction, which meant every future negotiation started from fear. It took us three years and one very uncomfortable price increase letter to climb out of a hole I dug in four seconds to make an eyebrow go away.

The rule I carved into the wall afterwards: a discount is never given, it is traded. Money moves only when something moves the other way, a longer commitment, payment up front, a case study with their logo on it, a smaller scope. Not because trading squeezes out more margin, though it does, but because trading is the only way the buyer can trust your prices. A price that collapses when an eyebrow moves was never a price. It was an opening bid, and now everything you say is an opening bid.

Discount requests will come regardless, and from enterprise procurement they will come professionally, on schedule, from people like Priya's Falk who ask for twenty percent the way other people ask for the time. Do not be offended and do not be frightened: it is a ritual, and the person performing it is often measured on the concessions they extract, which means your job is to let them win something you priced in advance. What matters is decoding what the request actually means, because "too expensive" is five different sentences wearing one coat.

Keeping Deals Alive Without Becoming a Pest

Between the great conversation and the signature stretches the middle of the deal, and the middle is where founder pipelines go to die quietly. Not dramatically, no rival swoops in, no angry email arrives. The deal just goes still, like a pond. Understanding why is the key to preventing it: your deal is one of your three most important things, and one of your buyer's forty-seven. They are not ignoring you out of malice or even disinterest. You have simply drifted down their list under the pressure of their actual job, and unless something specific and dated pulls the deal upward, it settles to the bottom like sand.

The single habit that prevents most of this costs nothing: never end any interaction, call, meeting, coffee, corridor chat, without a specific next step, a date, and a named owner. Not "I'll follow up in a couple of weeks," which is sand. Instead: "I'll send the revised proposal Thursday. Can we book thirty minutes Tuesday the 14th to walk through it while it's fresh?" If the buyer will not put anything on a calendar, that is not rudeness, it is information, and your pipeline should hear about it.

The proposal itself deserves a paragraph, because founders reliably misuse it. A proposal is a confirmation, not an exploration. It should contain, in the customer's own recorded words, the problem, the cost of the problem, what you will do about it, what success looks like, and the price they have already heard. Already heard, that phrase carries the whole rule: no customer should ever meet your price for the first time in a PDF. In a conversation you can watch the number land and handle what it stirs up. In a document the number lands alone, gets forwarded to people who never met you, and is compared against things you cannot see. Say the price out loud first, always. The proposal exists so that your champion can sell you internally when you are not in the room, which is what champions actually do with their time. Write it for the people you have not met.

Watch out

If every relationship in a deal runs through one person, you do not have a deal, you have a hostage situation with pleasant weekly calls. Champions get reorganized, overruled, poached, and sent on parental leave. Priya spent a genuinely bad week watching her one gearbox believer polish his LinkedIn profile, with no second contact to call if he vanished. He stayed, she got lucky, and she never ran a deal single-threaded again. The fix is unglamorous: in every meeting, ask "who else should see this?" and collect introductions like the deal depends on them. Two contacts is a minimum. Three or more people who would notice if the deal died is a deal with its own pulse.

Then there is ghosting, the proposal that vanishes into silence. Here is the etiquette that preserves both the deal and your dignity. Follow up two or three times, spaced over a couple of weeks, and make each follow-up carry something: a relevant article, an answer to an open question, a customer result, never the naked "just checking in," which asks the buyer to do your work of having a reason. If silence persists, send the closing email, some salespeople call it the breakup email, and it goes like this: "I don't want to keep cluttering your inbox. I'm assuming this isn't a priority right now, so I'll close the file on my side. If things change, you know where I am, and I'd be glad to pick it up again." Two things happen. Often, nothing, and you have your answer and your hours back. But surprisingly often, this is the mail that gets answered within the day, sometimes with an apology and a signature, because permission to say no, sincerely given, is so rare that buyers reward it. Either outcome beats month four of hopeful silence, because the deadliest thing in your pipeline is not the deal you lose. It is the deal that neither closes nor dies, eating your attention and propping up your forecast, a zombie with a logo.

Try this

Tonight, with a pen, run the pipeline scrub. List every open deal. Next to each one, write three things: the customer's problem in their words, the agreed next step, and its date. Any deal missing one of the three gets exactly one honest email tomorrow morning to establish it. If the email gets no answer within two weeks, the deal moves to "no decision" with a reason logged. The first scrub typically shortens a founder's pipeline by a third and improves their mood by more, because a short true list beats a long flattering one in every way that pays rent.

Losing Gracefully

You will lose deals. Let me say it more precisely: if you are qualifying honestly and pricing with a spine, you should be losing deals, regularly, because a founder who wins everything is either lying to their pipeline or charging half of what the work is worth. The question this section answers is not how to avoid losing. It is what losing is for, because a lost deal, handled well, produces two assets: information and reputation. Handled badly, it produces a small enemy and a lesson you paid for but never collected.

Start with the moment of the no. Whatever you feel, and you will feel it in your stomach, because it is your product and your name, the reply is short and warm: thank them for the time, congratulate the choice if a competitor won, and leave the door visibly open. Do not argue with the corpse. Rebutting a decision already made has never once reversed one, and it converts a neutral outcome into a story the buyer tells about you at dinner. The buyer who chose someone else this year is the buyer whose chosen vendor may stumble next year, and next year they will remember exactly one losing vendor: the gracious one or the bitter one, whichever you decided to be in a five-line email you wrote in four minutes.

Then, once the sting has settled, ask for the debrief. Not in the rejection thread, a week or two later, and not as a plea, as a request for a favor between professionals: "We're a small company and we learn the hard way. Would you give me fifteen minutes to hear honestly why we didn't win? No selling, I promise." Perhaps half will say yes, and what they tell you is worth more than most consulting you could buy, with one warning: treat the first stated reason as a curtain. "It came down to price" is the polite exit, because price is a no that requires no further conversation. Ask one gentle question behind it, "if the prices had been identical, who would have won?", and watch the real reason step out: they never quite believed a six-person company could support them, the other side knew their industry's jargon, the IT director had a preference all along and the process was theater. Log every reason. Six months of logged loss reasons will tell you more about your product, your positioning, and your pitch than any strategy offsite, and it will tell you in the customer's voice.

And sometimes, be the one who ends it. The graceful loss has a proactive cousin, the graceful withdrawal, and it belongs in your repertoire. When discovery reveals that the fit is poor, that their problem is not really your product, that the timeline is fantasy, the strongest move available is to say so and step back, ideally pointing them somewhere genuinely useful. It costs you a deal you were going to lose anyway, slowly and expensively, and it buys you the rarest asset in any market: the reputation of a seller whose yes means yes. People remember the vendor who talked them out of a purchase. They come back years later, with better problems, and they bring their friends.

Story

The same spring the gearbox deal was crawling through procurement, Priya lost a different deal, a mid-sized automotive supplier, to a competitor four times her size. The buyer, a quality director named Anneke, called personally to tell her, which buyers almost never do, and Priya, exhausted and briefly free of strategic ambition, responded with plain honesty: thanks for calling, good choice given your global footprint, one piece of unsolicited advice about migrating certificates from the old system, based on where she had seen it go wrong. Then a two-line note the next morning wishing them a smooth rollout. Total investment: one phone call she did not enjoy and an email.

Eighteen months later, Anneke changed jobs, as ambitious quality directors do. Her new employer had the same document chaos and no vendor. She did not run a comparison this time. She called Priya directly, opened with "you were so decent when we didn't pick you," and closed in five weeks at full list price. Priya, who tracks everything now, tells founders that her single highest-converting pipeline source, measured over years, is people who told her no the first time. Buyers move. Their memory of how you lost moves with them.

Which brings us back, finally, to Stuttgart. The gearbox deal closed in month nine, and it is worth recording honestly what closing looked like, because it looked nothing like the movies. It was not fourteen factories, it was three, a pilot scoped around the audit calendar, with expansion criteria written into the contract in plain language. Falk got his discount, four percent, traded against a three-year term and a reference visit, and seemed as satisfied with the ritual as with the result. The security questionnaire was survived with the help of a freelance consultant and two long evenings. And the head of quality, her champion, stood up in the final meeting and presented the business case himself, from her proposal, in his own words, which by then were also her words, because she had taken them down in a notebook in the meeting where she finally stopped demoing. Priya called me afterwards, from what I choose to believe was the same parking garage. "They signed," she said, and then, before I could ask anything: "Three factories, signature verified, first invoice sent, and I know exactly why they bought." That is the sentence. That is the whole chapter in eleven words, and she got there in nine months, which is faster than it took me by roughly four years.

You Will Not Be the Only Salesperson Forever

Everything in this chapter assumes it is you on the calls, you writing the proposals, you saying the number into the silence. That season does not last forever, and it should not. There will come a point, usually somewhere past the moment when selling is competing with everything else for your calendar and losing, when the company needs salespeople who are not you, and a machine around them: hiring profiles, ramp plans, territories, commission structures that reward the behavior you actually want. That is chapter fourteen's territory, and I will not poach it. The general craft of hiring anyone at all is chapter ten, which is conveniently next.

But there is one thing you must do now, during the founder-selling season, that future chapter is powerless to do retroactively: write it down. Every time you notice what works, capture it somewhere unglamorous. The discovery questions that reliably open people up. The exact phrases customers use for their problems, in their dialects, by industry. The objections that recur and the answers that landed. The loss reasons, logged. The stage definitions and their evidence. The pricing lines you rehearsed in your kitchen. None of it needs to be a manual yet; a running document with a terrible title will do. I called mine "sales stuff.doc" for six years. When the day comes to hire your first salesperson, that document is the difference between handing over a craft and handing over a hope. The founders who skip it end up standing behind their first sales hire six months in, wondering why the magic did not transfer, not realizing the magic was never written anywhere except in their own reflexes.

And here is the quiet gift of doing this phase properly, the one nobody mentions at networking events. Founder-led selling is the last time you will ever be this close to your market with this little in between. No reports, no dashboards summarizing summaries, no account manager's interpretation. Just you, a notebook, and customer after customer telling you, in their own words, what your company is actually for. Some of what they tell you belongs in this chapter's pipeline. Some of it belongs three chapters back, in what you sell and who you are. Take the notes either way. You are not just closing deals out there. You are conducting the cheapest, most honest market research your company will ever get, and you are doing it at the only moment in its life when the person hearing the answers has the power to act on all of them.

The Short Version

  • You are the sales department, and for now you should be: early selling produces learning first and revenue second, and both die if you outsource the conversations. Scaling a sales team is chapter fourteen's problem.
  • "They loved it" is not a stage. The only signals that a deal is moving are specific: a date, a name, a number, a new stakeholder, a question about implementation. Everything else is weather.
  • Pick the sales motion your deal size can afford, and notice when a deal from a different row walks in. An enterprise deal is not a big transactional deal, it is a different animal.
  • Discovery before demo, always. Verify misery, money, mandate, and moment, in the customer's own words, and write the words down verbatim. Their words close deals; yours decorate them.
  • Ask the question, then shut up. The real answer arrives around second four of the silence you were about to smother.
  • A pipeline is a list of people with a problem, a budget, and a date, where every stage is earned by customer evidence, not by your activity. Your biggest competitor in it is doing nothing.
  • Say the price in a full sentence, then stop talking. Rehearse it out loud until it sounds like a fact. The first person to speak after the number usually concedes.
  • Discounts are traded, never gifted. A price that collapses at a raised eyebrow was never a price, and it anchors every renewal, referral, and negotiation that follows.
  • End every interaction with a next step, a date, and an owner, and kill zombie deals with a clean breakup email. A short true pipeline beats a long flattering one.
  • Lose on purpose and lose warmly: log the real reason behind the polite one, and treat every graceful loss as a deposit. Buyers change jobs, and their memory of your no travels with them.
