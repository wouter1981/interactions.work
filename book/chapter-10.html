Chapter 10

Hiring People

The day you hire your first employee, you stop being someone who does the work and start being someone other people bet their mortgage on. Nobody tells you these are different jobs.

Marta called me on a Sunday evening, which was unusual, because Marta guards her Sundays the way other people guard their PIN codes.

"Third one," she said, before I had finished saying hello. "Third resignation in five months. Lena handed me her letter on Friday and I have spent the whole weekend pretending I don't know why."

At the time, Marta's design agency was about fourteen people. She had built it from her spare bedroom into one of those studios that wins the kind of awards other studios pretend not to care about. And for five months, her designers had been quietly leaving. Every exit conversation produced the same phrase, personal reasons, delivered with the same careful eye contact, the kind people make when they have rehearsed not saying something.

"So why are they leaving?" I asked.

There was a long pause. I could hear her kitchen tap running, then stopping.

"Viktor," she said.

Viktor was her best designer. I want to be precise about this, because the story does not work if you round him down: he was genuinely, uncomfortably good. Marta had hired him two years earlier, when the agency was twelve people and drowning in work, and he had walked in with a portfolio that made her a little dizzy and a salary demand she met without negotiating. Clients asked for him by name. A rebrand he led won the agency its biggest award, the one that sat on the shelf by the entrance so that everyone saw it on their way in.

He was also, and it took Marta two years to say this out loud to anyone including herself, corrosive. He rolled his eyes in critiques. He rewrote juniors' work at two in the morning and shipped it to clients without telling them, so they found out in the meeting, in front of everyone. He took the best projects the way weather takes a coastline, gradually, then obviously. To clients and to Marta he was warm, funny, generous with his time. The charm pointed up and out. The corrosion pointed down and in. That is the classic configuration, by the way. It is practically a design pattern.

"He is my best designer," Marta said. "If he leaves, I lose the award work. Two clients would probably follow him out the door."

"What did the three who left cost you?" I asked. "Not emotionally. In euros. Recruiting, onboarding, the projects that slipped, the clients who noticed the new faces. Run the number."

She ran the number. I will tell you what it was, and what she did about Viktor, near the end of this chapter, because the end of the hiring story is where Viktor belongs. But I am telling you the beginning now, up front, for a reason. Every hiring mistake in this chapter, and I have made most of them personally, comes from the same root: deciding what you want after you have already met someone, instead of before. Marta did not hire a problem. She hired a portfolio, and the problem came free with it.

This chapter is about employees: finding them, choosing them, landing them, and, when it comes to that, saying goodbye to them like a decent human being. If your question is about cofounders and equity, that is chapter five, and please do not confuse the two, because a cofounder is a marriage and an employee is not. If your question is about what all these people should report into once you have them, that is chapter eleven. And if you have not yet worked out what your company actually values, which is the raw material every hiring decision is secretly made of, that is chapter one, and it is cheaper to read it before your first job ad than after your fifth exit interview.

The Most Expensive Sentence in Your Company

The most expensive sentence in your company is "we need someone."

It does not sound expensive. It sounds like progress. It usually gets said late in the evening, after the third week in a row of you doing invoices at midnight, and it arrives with a warm feeling of maturity, the sense that the business has grown up enough to need staff. Everyone around the table nods. Someone opens a job board. And at that moment, with the best intentions in the world, you have started the process most founders do worse than anything else they do.

Here is the uncomfortable arithmetic. A hire in most of Europe will cost you, all in, somewhere between 1.3 and 1.8 times their gross salary once you count employer contributions, insurance, equipment, software seats, and the desk they sit at. A 45,000 euro salary is a 65,000 euro decision, per year, before they have produced anything. A mediocre hire produces some value against that. A wrong hire produces negative value: their salary, plus your time managing them, plus the work you redo, plus the customers they handle averagely, plus, and this is the part nobody budgets, the effect on the people around them. The research on this bounces around, but every founder I know who has run their own numbers lands in the same range: a bad hire costs one to two times their annual salary, and a badly handled bad hire costs more, because the handling is where the collateral damage lives.

Now the other side of the ledger, because I am not trying to frighten you out of hiring, I am trying to frighten you into doing it well. A genuinely good hire is the single highest-leverage purchase available to a small business. Not software, not ads, not a rebrand. A person who is right for the job you actually have will return multiples of their cost, year after year, and will make your next three hires better, because good people are the best recruiting asset ever invented. The gap between a good hire and a bad one is not twenty percent. In a ten-person company it is the difference between a good year and a lawyer.

Which brings us to the strange fact at the center of this chapter: hiring is the highest-stakes skill in your company, and it is the one you have practiced least. You have been a customer thousands of times, so sales feels familiar. You have managed your own money your whole life, so the finance chapter at least rhymes with experience. But most first-time founders have been hired a handful of times and have hired nobody, ever. Your entire mental model of recruitment was formed while sitting on the other side of the table, trying to be liked. It is exactly backwards for the job you are about to do.

So we are going to build the skill properly, in the order the decisions actually arrive: what to hire for, how to define it, how to select for it, how to land the person, how to make their first months work, and what to do when, despite all of it, you got it wrong.

What to Hire First: Follow the Drain, Not the Dream

Ask a founder what their first hire should be and you will usually hear a title from a company five times their size. A marketing manager. A developer to build the platform. Once, memorably, at a founder dinner, a head of people, for a company that consisted of two people, both of them at the dinner.

Titles are the wrong starting point. The right starting point is an audit of your own week, and it works like this. Take everything you did in the last month and sort it into three piles. Pile one: things only you can do, because they need your authority, your relationships, or your particular skill. Pile two: things you are good at and enjoy, but that someone else could learn. Pile three: things that drain you, that you do badly, that get done late at night or not at all, and that the business genuinely needs.

Your first hire almost always lives in pile three, and this is where founders flinch, because pile three is boring. It is the invoices, the scheduling, the customer emails, the production work that has become routine, the quote follow-ups from chapter nine that never happen because you hate doing them. Nobody dreams of hiring for pile three. It feels like spending precious money on the dull part of the business. But look at what pile three is doing to you: it is consuming the hours you should be spending on pile one, and pile one is where the company's future gets built. The purpose of your first hire is not to add a capability. It is to buy back the founder's best hours, which are currently the most misallocated resource in the company.

There is a second flinch, which sounds like this: "by the time I explain it, I could have done it myself." True, for the first month. Catastrophically false afterwards. That sentence, repeated weekly, is how founders end up at year four doing the same pile-three work at midnight, except now with fifteen employees waiting on their bottleneck. If you find that sentence coming out of your mouth often, the deeper problem is delegation, and delegation gets its own treatment in chapter twelve. Here I will just say: the explaining is not overhead on the hire. The explaining is the hire. It is you converting knowledge that lives only in your head into something the company owns, and it pays rent forever.

One more thing about the first hire, and it is the emotional part nobody warns you about. The day their contract starts, the weight changes. Until now, if the business had a terrible quarter, you ate pasta and pushed through. Now a terrible quarter has someone else's rent in it. Every founder I know remembers their first payroll run the way they remember their first customer. It is a wonderful, sobering feeling, and you should let it sober you, because it is also the correct amount of seriousness to bring to every hire after this one. You are not filling a seat. You are making a promise about the future of the company to someone who is going to build their life partly on your judgement. Check your runway math from chapter six before you make that promise, not after. The kindest thing you can do for a future employee is to only hire them when you can actually afford them for at least a year of bad news.

A practical European note before we go further, because the first-hire decision is not actually binary. Between "just me" and "an employment contract" sits a whole staircase that founders on this continent underuse in one direction and overuse in the other. A freelancer for two days a week, a payroll company that carries the formal employment so you do not have to learn the paperwork yet, a part-time contract, a fixed-term year: all of these let you buy back pile three while the revenue is still wobbly, and several of them let you and a person try each other out honestly before either of you signs anything long. Jonas staffed his second bakery almost entirely this way for the first year, weekend students and one semi-retired baker on Tuesdays, and converted the ones who fit. The underuse is founders who think hiring means a permanent full-time contract or nothing, and so hire nothing for two years too long. The overuse is its mirror: founders who keep a person on rolling freelance terms for three years because commitment is frightening, while the person does the job of an employee, waits for a promise, and eventually accepts one from someone else. The staircase is for climbing. Use the lower steps to learn, then commit like you mean it, because the best people can tell the difference between a company that is being careful and a company that is keeping its options open at their expense.

And one more fear to name, because it sits under more bad first hires than anyone admits: the fear of hiring someone better than you. It usually disguises itself as budget prudence, "we cannot afford someone that senior," but you can hear the real note underneath, which is "what is my job, if she is better at this than me." Get over it as fast as you possibly can. In a small company, every hire who is merely a diluted copy of the founder lowers the average. The entire game, and I mean this arithmetically, is to hire people who raise it, people you will learn from, people who make you slightly nervous in the interview for the right reasons. At the routing company, the moment things genuinely turned was the year I hired two people who were unambiguously better than me at their jobs and, after a wobble I am not proud of, got out of their way. Your job is not to be the best at everything. Your job, increasingly, is to be the person who assembled the people who are, and if that sentence unsettles you, fold the corner of this page and read chapter twelve when the unsettling gets loud.

Hire for the Company You Are, Not the One in Your Head

Now the mistake I personally own, in its purest form, so you can recognize the shape of it.

Story

At the routing company, when we were eight people, I hired a sales director from a large logistics corporate. Let's call him Ruud. Ruud had twenty years of experience, a suit that fit properly, and a way of saying "go-to-market motion" that made me feel like we had already succeeded. In the interviews, I did not really assess Ruud. I basked in him. He described the sales organization he would build, with account tiers and a partner channel and quarterly business reviews, and I saw the company I dreamed of becoming, and I hired the dream.

Ruud lasted seven months, and I want to be fair to him: he did nothing wrong except believe me. I had sold him the same imaginary company he had sold me. He arrived expecting a marketing department to feed him leads. We had a shared inbox. He expected an assistant, a travel budget, a CRM with data in it. We had a spreadsheet named FINAL_v3. The job we actually needed done, driving to mid-sized haulage firms and drinking coffee with sceptical planning managers until one of them said yes, was a job he had not done himself in fifteen years and, reasonably enough, did not want to do again. He left politely. The polite ones hurt more. It cost us about a year of sales momentum and, I later worked out, roughly 130,000 euros, and every cent of it was me paying for the difference between the company I ran and the company I described in the interview.

The lesson is not "never hire experienced people." The lesson is that every hire is a fit between a person and a company, and you can only get the fit right if you are honest about which company is doing the hiring. Not the one in the pitch deck. Not the one in three years. The one that shows up on Monday, with its shared inbox and its spreadsheet named FINAL_v3.

Being the company you are cuts in two directions, and both matter.

First, it changes who you look for. A ten-person company does not need the person who ran the function at scale. It needs the person who built the function the last time it was this size, or who is hungry to build it for the first time and has the raw material. The skills are different in kind, not degree. Building is deciding what to do with no data, doing the work yourself, and living with the consequences at close range. Running is optimizing a machine someone else built. When you interview someone from a big company, the single most predictive question I know is embarrassingly simple: "tell me, concretely, what you did yourself, with your own hands, in the last month of your current job." Some big-company people have excellent answers. Many discover, live, in front of you, that the honest answer is "attended meetings about work other people did." Better everyone finds out now.

Second, it changes how you sell the job, because make no mistake, you are selling, and everything you learned about honest discovery in chapter nine applies to this pipeline too. The temptation is to compensate for your smallness by borrowing glamour: unlimited growth, huge impact, we're like a family. Resist all of it, especially the family one, families cannot fire each other and you can, so it is a lie with a delayed detonation. What a small company can honestly offer is specific and, to the right person, genuinely valuable: real responsibility from week one, the absence of politics because there is nobody to politick with, direct sight of the customer and the consequences, learning at a speed no corporate can match, and proximity to the founder, which is either a perk or a warning depending on the founder. Say those things, and say the hard parts too. "The salary is decent but not corporate. Some weeks are chaotic. The coffee machine is a kettle." The candidates this loses were going to leave in month four anyway. The candidates it wins arrive with their eyes open, and eyes-open people stay.

Warning

The most dangerous moment in early hiring is when a candidate is impressive in a way that has nothing to do with the job. A famous employer on the CV, a fluent way with strategy words, a portfolio with an award in it. Impressiveness is a feeling in you, not a property of them. Every time you notice yourself feeling flattered that this person would even talk to your little company, treat it as a fire alarm: you have stopped assessing and started courting.

The Scorecard: Decide What Good Looks Like Before You Meet Anyone

Everything in this chapter hangs off one discipline, and it is this: write down what success in the role looks like before you meet a single candidate. On paper. In sentences. Agreed by everyone who will interview.

The document is called a scorecard, and it is not a job description. A job description lists activities: "manage customer inquiries, maintain the CRM, support the sales team." A scorecard defines outcomes: "by month twelve, customers get a first response within four hours and our renewal rate is back above ninety percent." Do you feel the difference? A job description is a list of ways to be busy. A scorecard is a definition of done. People can perform activities beautifully for years without producing outcomes, and your interview process, if it is built on the job description, will select precisely for people who are good at describing activities. Which is to say: good at interviews.

A scorecard has four parts, and fits on one page.

The mission of the role, in one sentence a smart outsider would understand. Not "own the customer success function." Rather: "make sure the customers we worked so hard to win actually succeed, notice it, and stay." If you cannot write this sentence, you do not need a hire yet, you need an afternoon of thinking, and it will be the cheapest afternoon of your year.

Three to five outcomes, each measurable or at least observable, each with a rough date. These are the results the person will be held to, and, crucially, they are what you will build your interview around. You are no longer asking "is this person impressive." You are asking "is this person likely to produce these five outcomes in this company." Different question, different answers.

Behaviours, drawn from the values you actually hold, the real ones from chapter one, not the poster ones. Three or four, phrased so concretely that you could point at a moment in a workday and say "that, that is the behaviour." Not "team player." Rather: "when they finish their own work early, they look for who is drowning."

Deal-breakers, stated in advance, because deal-breakers decided in advance get enforced, and deal-breakers decided in the moment get negotiated away by charm. Marta had no written deal-breakers when she met Viktor. She has them now. The first one reads: "no one gets hired who is rude to anyone we are not paying them to be nice to."

It becomes the spine of onboarding, the agenda of your first quarterly review, and, if things go wrong, the honest reference point for the conversation in the last section of this chapter. One page, working that hard, is the best deal in management.

Try this

Take the next hire you are considering, or the last one you made, and write the scorecard for it now, from memory, in twenty minutes: mission, three outcomes with dates, three behaviours, two deal-breakers. If you are currently hiring and you cannot fill it in, pause the vacancy until you can. If you wrote it for an existing employee and realized they are meeting it, tell them so this week. If you realized they are not, chapter section nine is waiting for you, but read the onboarding section first, because the gap is sometimes yours.

Interviews That Predict Instead of Charm

Here is the dirty secret of the standard job interview: it measures the ability to be interviewed. That is a real skill, it is just not the one you are paying for, unless you are hiring interviewers.

The unstructured interview, the friendly hour of "tell me about yourself" and "where do you see yourself in five years," has been studied for decades, and the results are consistently humiliating: it predicts job performance barely better than chance, while leaving every interviewer certain they are the exception. The mechanics of the failure are worth understanding, because you cannot fix what you cannot see. In an unstructured conversation, you form an impression in the first few minutes, mostly from confidence, fluency, and similarity to yourself. Then you spend the rest of the hour, without noticing, gathering evidence for that impression and letting go of evidence against it. The candidate, meanwhile, is answering hypothetical questions with hypothetical answers, describing not what they do but what they know they should say they do. Everyone knows the right answer to "are you a team player." Interviews built on hypotheticals select for people who know the right answers. Viktor, Marta will tell you, gave a magnificent interview.

An interview is not a conversation to enjoy. It is a prediction you will have to live with, made under charming conditions.

The fix is not cleverness or trick questions. The fix is structure, and structure means three things: every candidate for a role gets the same core questions, the questions are about the past instead of the hypothetical future, and answers get scored against the scorecard on the same day, before the interviewers talk to each other and average their impressions into mush. Structured interviews are less fun. They are also, in study after study and in every honest founder's experience, dramatically better at predicting who will actually do the job. You are running a small business, not a talk show. Optimize for prediction.

Ask about the past, not the future

The single biggest upgrade available to you costs nothing: replace every hypothetical question with a behavioural one. Not "how would you handle an angry customer" but "tell me about the last genuinely angry customer you dealt with. What did they say? What did you do? What happened?" The past has texture that invention cannot fake. Someone who has really talked a furious customer down remembers the specifics, the ugly first email, the thing they got wrong themselves, the exact sentence that turned it. Someone improvising gives you a tidy story with a moral, and tidy stories with morals are what chapter zero taught you to distrust in business books, so distrust them in interviews too.

Then, and this is where the prediction actually happens, follow up. Once is a story, the follow-up is the truth. "What was the hardest part?" "What did your colleague think you got wrong?" "What would the customer say about it, if I called them?" Three follow-ups deep, prepared answers run out and you meet the actual person. You are listening for specificity, for the presence of other people in the story as humans rather than props, and for ownership, the difference between "the project failed because the client kept changing scope" and "I let the client keep changing scope because I hated the confrontation." The second candidate has a flaw and knows it. The first one has the same flaw and will bring it to work for you, unexamined.

The work sample: watch them do the job, briefly, and pay for it

Talking about work predicts work moderately well. Doing work predicts it embarrassingly better, which is why the work sample is the strongest tool in your process, and the most abused one, so let me give you both halves.

The strong version: design a small, realistic slice of the actual job, two to four hours, doable at home or in a session with you. For a designer, a rough concept for a real brief you have already shipped, so you can compare. For a customer role, here are five real (anonymized) customer emails, answer three. For a developer, a small task in a codebase like yours, followed by a conversation about their choices, and the conversation is the point, because you learn more from "why did you do it this way" than from the code itself. For a baker, and I checked this with my brother-in-law Jonas, who you met in the money chapter: a morning shift, paid, watching how they treat the dough, the floor, and the seventeen-year-old apprentice, roughly in ascending order of importance.

The abused version, which candidates rightly resent and which will poison your reputation in exactly the small communities you recruit from: a vague, sprawling assignment that takes a weekend, on a live problem you actually need solved, unpaid. Do not do this. Keep samples short, keep them clearly separate from real deliverables or pay properly for the time, a hundred euros and an invoice is nothing against the cost of a wrong hire, and always, always give real feedback afterwards, both directions. The way you run your work sample is the first sample of you that the candidate gets. Strong candidates are assessing you right back, and they talk to each other.

Deciding: score first, talk second

However good your questions are, the decision meeting can still dissolve everything into soup, and it dissolves in a very specific way. The interviewers gather, the most senior or most confident person speaks first, "I really liked her," and every impression in the room quietly bends toward that opening verdict. Twenty minutes later the group has manufactured a consensus that nobody individually held, and the scorecard sits unopened on the table like a decorative napkin.

The countermeasure takes ten minutes of discipline. Everyone who interviewed writes down their scores against the scorecard, outcome by outcome, behaviour by behaviour, with one piece of evidence per score, before anyone says a word to anyone. Then you compare. The magic is not in the numbers, which are rough, it is in the divergences: when one interviewer scored "tells customers the truth" a five and another scored it a two, somewhere in those two interviews is a contradiction worth digging into, and digging into it is precisely the conversation the decision meeting exists to have. Small companies think this ritual is corporate overkill. It is the opposite. A corporate can absorb a mis-hire in the roundings. For you, one wrong yes is two percent of the whole company.

While I am here, let me take one beloved heuristic outside and deal with it: the airport test, "would I want to be stuck in an airport with this person." I understand its appeal, and I want you to notice what it actually measures, which is similarity to you. Same jokes, same references, same background, same schools. Hire by airport test for three years and you will look up from your desk into a room of your own reflections, agreeing with each other, sharing your blind spots in stereo. The team that wins is rarely the team that would enjoy the same airport. It is the team where the detail-obsessed pessimist and the charging optimist and the person who asks the awkward question in the quiet voice all have a seat, and all get listened to. What you need in common is the values on the scorecard and the standard of the work. Everything else, and I say this with love, is you recruiting drinking company on the payroll.

References: the phone call founders skip

Reference checks have a terrible reputation because most people do them terribly: a dutiful call to two names the candidate supplied, "would you recommend them," "oh yes," goodbye. Conducted like that, skip them, you will learn nothing. Conducted properly, they are twenty of the highest-value minutes in your whole process, because a former manager on the phone will tell you things no interview can surface.

Three upgrades. First, ask the candidate, in the interview, "when I call your last manager, what will they say was your biggest weakness?" Watch the answer, then compare it with the actual call. The gap between the two is a measurement of self-awareness, which is the trait that predicts whether someone can grow. Second, on the call, never ask yes-or-no questions. Ask "what should I know about how to manage them well?" and "in which situations do they struggle?" and then be quiet and let the silence work, exactly like the discovery calls in chapter nine. Third, listen for temperature, not words. Almost no European referee will say anything damning on the record, employment culture and, in some countries, the law lean against it. But enthusiasm is unmistakable and its absence is deafening. "They were fine. Reliable. Yes, fine," delivered flatly, from someone who managed a person for three years, is a red flag wearing a beige coat.

Field note

Know what you may not ask. Across the EU, questions about pregnancy, family plans, health, religion, orientation, and age are off limits in interviews, and rightly so. Beyond legality, every one of those questions is a bad prediction tool anyway: none of them tells you whether this person will hit the outcomes on your scorecard. If a line of questioning would not change a correct hiring decision, it has no business in the room.

The Funnel Is Honest Even When You Are Not

Hiring is a pipeline, exactly like the sales pipeline in chapter nine, and it deserves the same honesty about its leaks. Founders consistently misjudge two things about it: how many candidates the top requires, and how fast the good ones move through the middle. Both misjudgements are expensive, so here is a realistic funnel for a decent role at a small, unknown company, with the leaks labelled the way they actually happen.

Three lessons live in that picture. The first is about the top: one hundred applications sounds like abundance and is not, because the funnel eats them fast, so put real effort into where the vacancy travels. Your own customers, your suppliers, the good people your team used to work with, the small professional communities where your kind of work gets discussed. A personal note from a founder outperforms a job-board posting by an order of magnitude, and referrals from your best people outperform everything, because good people rarely recommend someone who will embarrass them.

The second lesson is speed. Look at the leak on the work sample stage. The best candidates, the ones with options, move through the market in two or three weeks, and every silent day between your stages reads to them as a preview of working for you. A small company cannot outbid a corporate, but it can outrun one, gloriously. Corporates take six weeks and four committees to make an offer. You can meet someone on Tuesday, run the work sample Thursday, take references Friday, and make an offer with a heartfelt phone call inside a week. Speed, decisiveness, and warmth are the small company's recruiting superpowers. Use all three.

The third lesson is the one founders resist: if nobody survives the funnel, run it again. The most expensive words after "we need someone" are "let's just take the best of the three we saw." The best of a weak field is a weak hire with a rosette on it. An empty seat costs you overtime. A wrong hire costs you the seat, the salary, the redo, and a piece of the team's trust in your judgement. Between a vacancy and a doubt, choose the vacancy.

The First Ten Hires, in the Order the Pain Arrives

People ask me for a hiring roadmap the way they ask for a workout plan, hoping the plan will do the lifting. So let me give you one, with the same disclaimer a good trainer gives: this is the typical shape, and your body will disagree with parts of it. The sequence below is what I have seen work across software companies, agencies, and, with the job titles translated, bakeries. What makes it a sequence is not ambition. It is pain. Each hire answers the pain that the previous hires created or exposed.

Notice what is not on the list before number eight or nine: anyone whose job is mainly to think about work rather than do it. In a company of this size, everyone touches the actual product or the actual customer, including you. Notice also the quiet milestone hiding at number seven, the first hire you did not personally select. That is the moment hiring stops being your craft and starts being your company's craft, and it deserves more ceremony than it gets. Write down how you hire, the scorecard discipline, the structured questions, the work sample, the reference calls, and teach it like you would teach any other process you care about. The alternative is that every interviewer in your company reverts to the talk-show format on their first solo flight, because that is the format television taught us all.

One honest caveat about sequence, in the spirit of the introduction: you will not follow it exactly, and you should not. A bakery needs hire number three before hire number two. An agency lives and dies by number six earlier than a software company does. The table is a map of the typical terrain, and, as with everything in this book, when the map and your terrain disagree, believe the terrain. What I will defend at any dinner table is the underlying rule: hire against pain you can point at today, never against an org chart you drew for the company you might be in three years. Growth-story hiring is how funded startups burn their runway, and how unfunded ones burn their founders.

Onboarding: The Ninety Days You Already Paid For

Here is a number that should bother you more than it does: a large share of new hires decide whether they will stay long-term within their first few months, and the single biggest factor in that decision is not salary, not workload, but whether anyone seemed ready for them. And here is the founder's confession that goes with it: by the time the new person walks in, you have spent weeks on the search, you are exhausted by hiring, and the vacancy pain the hire was meant to fix is screaming louder than ever. So the strongest temptation in this entire chapter is to point the new person at the pile and go back to your own pile. Which is roughly like training for a marathon and then, on race morning, deciding you have already done the hard part.

Story

Marta learned onboarding from a designer named Inge, who quit in week three. Inge was, by every signal in the process, an excellent hire. On her first morning, the studio was in full deadline crisis. Her laptop had not arrived. Nobody had decided which projects were hers. Marta, mortified, waved her toward a spare desk and said the sentence she still quotes at herself, in a bad falsetto: "just look around and settle in, we'll do everything properly next week." Next week was the pitch. The week after was the rework from the pitch. Inge spent three weeks guessing what her job was, asking questions of people who were visibly too busy to answer them, redesigning a menu nobody had asked for because it was the only work she could find, and concluding, reasonably, that this was not chaos week, this was the company. Her resignation email was two sentences and one of them was kind.

What Marta built afterwards is almost embarrassingly simple, and she has used it for every hire since. A one-page plan, written before the person's first day, with four lines on it: what you will have done by Friday, by week four, by month three, and the name of the one person whose job it is to answer your stupid questions, with time actually blocked in that person's calendar for it. Plus one rule she stole from a hotelier client: the workspace is completely ready the night before, login working, tools present, one handwritten line from the team on the desk. "It costs two hours," she says. "It buys you a person who tells the story of their first day for years. Both versions of the story last years, actually. You are choosing which one."

Let me turn Marta's page into the general pattern, because it scales down to a bakery and up to a fifty-person firm without modification.

Day one is for belonging, not paperwork. The contracts can wait until the afternoon. What cannot wait is the tour of how things really work, an actual task completed before going home, however small, and lunch where the new person does not have to choose a chair. Human beings decide very quickly whether they are welcome somewhere, and they make the decision on signals this cheap.

Week one is for the customer. Whatever the role, the new person should meet the thing the company exists for: sit in on support, visit a client with you, stand at the counter on Saturday morning, read the last twenty won-and-lost deals. Every week of delay makes the company's purpose more abstract, and people who find their work abstract do abstract work.

The first ninety days belong to the scorecard. You wrote the outcomes before you hired, so onboarding is not a new document, it is the same document with nearer dates. Sit down at day thirty, sixty, and ninety, thirty minutes each, and ask three questions: what is going better than expected, what is harder than expected, and what do you know now that I should have told you on day one. That third question, asked honestly and acted on, will improve your onboarding faster than any template, because every new hire is a consultant on your company's confusions who gets more expensive to interview with every passing month, as the strangeness fades and they stop noticing what is strange.

And say the quiet part early. If something is off in the first weeks, name it in week two, gently, specifically, in private. Founders hoard early doubts out of politeness and cash them in month eight as a grievance the employee never got the chance to fix. That is not kindness. Kindness with a delay mechanism is cowardice, and it also happens to be terrible management. Most early wobbles are fixable precisely because they are early, a misread of priorities, a habit from the old employer, a gap you can train. The probation period, which in most European countries gives both sides one or two months of easy exit, exists exactly so that truth can be spoken while the stakes are still small. Use it as a conversation device, not a trapdoor.

The Brilliant Jerk

And so, back to Viktor, because by now you have the equipment to see exactly what happened to Marta.

She hired without a scorecard, against a portfolio, in an unstructured interview where Viktor was magnificent, which we have established is what unstructured interviews measure. There were no written deal-breakers, so when the first reports of the eye-rolling and the 2 a.m. rewrites drifted up, each incident got adjudicated individually, against his brilliance, and brilliance won every hearing. The math in her head was simple and wrong: best designer, biggest award, key clients, therefore most valuable, therefore protected. What the math left out was that value in a company is not additive, it is networked. Viktor's personal output was a large positive number. His effect on the output of everyone within reach of him was a larger negative one, paid in resignations, in juniors who stopped proposing ideas because proposals got rewritten at 2 a.m., in the growing silence in critiques, which in a design agency is the sound of the product dying.

When Marta finally ran the number I asked her for on that Sunday call, three departed designers, recruitment fees, six months of reduced output while replacements ramped, one client who left citing "turbulence," she got to roughly 180,000 euros, and then she stopped counting because the exercise had made its point. Viktor's salary premium over a merely very good designer was about 15,000 a year. She had been paying a 15,000 euro premium for brilliance and financing a six-figure loss from its side effects, annually, while telling herself she could not afford to lose him.

Look at the grid for a moment, because three of its quadrants are easy and cheap. Skilled and generous, keep them, and by the way, your best people watch what you tolerate more closely than they watch what you reward. Unskilled and corrosive, everyone agrees, though everyone still takes six months. Unskilled but generous deserves real patience, because the deficit is often the role or the onboarding rather than the person, and coaching someone generous into competence is one of the most profitable things a small company can do. The whole character of your company gets decided in the remaining quadrant, and it gets decided in public, in slow motion, because everybody sees the behaviour and everybody sees your response. Every week the brilliant jerk keeps their seat, your stated values, the chapter one ones, get repriced by the team at their true market rate: decoration.

The playbook, when you find yourself where Marta was, is short. One conversation, direct and documented: the behaviour, its cost, the change required, the timeline, usually four to six weeks, and the consequence, stated plainly. No hints, no hoping, no general team workshop about communication styles purchased so you can avoid one specific conversation, and yes, that is a thing founders actually do, I have watched the invoices. Some brilliant jerks genuinely change, more than you would think, because nobody impressive ever told them the truth before, their brilliance has been buying silence since school. The ones who do not change have made your decision for you, and the only remaining question is whether you act on it in weeks or in resignations.

Letting People Go Kindly

I was told, while writing this book, to flag its hardest paragraph when I got to it. This section is where it lives. I have fired people. I have been part of hiring decisions that put good humans in the wrong jobs and then cost them a year of confidence when it failed. If you build a company of any size, you will do both, and how you do them will say more about you than your mission statement ever will. So let me walk you through it the way I wish someone had walked me.

First, the decision itself, which is where most of the cruelty in business actually originates, not in the firing but in the not-firing. Here is the pattern: you know by month five. You act in month fourteen. The nine months in between, which you experience as patience and kindness, the employee experiences as a slowly dropping temperature nobody will explain, fewer projects, tighter feedback, meetings they are no longer in. Their colleagues, meanwhile, are covering the gaps and privately downgrading their opinion of you, because they knew in month four. When the conversation finally comes, it lands on someone who has spent most of a year being quietly demoted from the truth. Delay is not mercy. Delay is you purchasing your own comfort with their time, at their expense, in a currency they cannot see being spent.

Before anything else, apply the test I use for every doubted role: knowing everything you now know, would you enthusiastically rehire this person into this job today? Not "are they terrible," almost nobody is terrible, but enthusiastically, today, this job. If the answer is no three months running, and one honest scorecard conversation with a real chance to change has not moved it, you have your answer, and everything after is execution and decency.

Try this

Tonight, with a pen, list everyone who works for you. Next to each name, answer the rehire test: knowing what you know now, would you enthusiastically rehire them into their current role? For every "no," write one more line: the honest reason, and the date you last told them the substance of that reason to their face. If the second part is blank or old, the next conversation you owe is not a dismissal, it is the truth, with time and help attached. Some of my best people, at the routing company, were "no"s who became "yes"es because the conversation finally happened.

Second, the mechanics, and here Europe is not America, so let me say it plainly for readers on my side of the water. In most European countries you cannot simply end an employment contract because you feel like it. Depending on where you operate there are notice periods, mandatory severance or transition payments, required documentation of underperformance, dismissal permits or court routes in some countries, and, once you pass certain headcounts, works councils with a voice in how things are done. Founders discover this landscape in two moods: outrage, or preparation. Choose preparation. The rules are knowable, your accountant or a labour lawyer will explain your country's version in one billable hour, and that hour, spent before you hire rather than before you fire, is some of the best money in this book. The practical consequences are threefold: take probation periods seriously, because they are the one window where parting is simple and they exist precisely so both sides can act on early truth. Document honestly as you go, not as a trap but as a record of real conversations that actually happened. And build the cost of doing this properly into your plans, because kind and legal both cost money, and being surprised by the cost is how founders end up doing it neither kindly nor legally.

Story

Marta let Viktor go on a Tuesday morning, eleven days after that Sunday phone call. She had done it properly: the documented warning conversation had happened a month earlier, with a specific list and a real chance, and he had responded by being charming to her and worse to the juniors, which told her the warning had been received as a negotiation. The conversation itself took eleven minutes. He was, she says, charming to the end, then coldly furious, then gone, and one client did leave with him, which cost real money and was, she wants me to tell you, still cheap.

What she did not expect was the week after. A senior designer she assumed was Viktor's ally came to her office and closed the door, and Marta braced for a resignation. Instead: "we didn't think you'd ever do it." Within a month, critiques got loud again, in the good way. Within a year, one of the three designers who had left came back, and the agency did the best creative work of its life with no award-winner on staff, because, as Marta puts it now, the talent was always in the building, it was just keeping its head down. The award is still on the shelf by the entrance. She keeps it there deliberately. "People think it reminds me of Viktor," she told me. "It reminds me of the three people it cost, and that I knew for two years and called it patience."

And fourth, the part nobody writes down: what it does to you. The first time you let someone go, done properly and kindly and legally, you will still feel like a small war criminal for about a week. You will replay the conversation, you will see them in the supermarket in your imagination, and you will be tempted to conclude that you are not cut out for this. You are. The feeling is not a malfunction, it is the correct emotional price of holding power over someone's livelihood, and the founders who should worry are the ones who stop feeling it. Pay the price, learn what the hire taught you about your own process, upgrade the scorecard, and go make the next promise more carefully. That is the whole loop of this chapter, and every company that lasts runs it hundreds of times.

The Short Version

  • Hiring is the highest-stakes skill in your company and the one you have practiced least. Treat it as a craft, not an errand.
  • Your first hires buy back the founder's best hours. Hire against pain you can point at today, never against the org chart of an imaginary future company.
  • Hire for the company you are, and sell the job honestly, hard parts included. Eyes-open people stay.
  • Write the scorecard, mission, outcomes, behaviours, deal-breakers, before you meet anyone. Decisions made after meeting a charming person are not decisions, they are courtship.
  • Unstructured interviews measure the ability to be interviewed. Ask about the real past, follow up three levels deep, use a short paid work sample, and make reference calls that listen for temperature.
  • Run the funnel with honest numbers, move fast because speed and warmth are your advantages, and never hire the best of a weak field. Between a vacancy and a doubt, choose the vacancy.
  • Onboarding is the cheapest retention tool ever invented: a ready desk, a real task on day one, the customer in week one, and the scorecard at days 30, 60, 90.
  • The brilliant jerk is a net negative wearing a medal. One documented warning, one deadline, then act. Your values are worth exactly what you tolerate.
  • Delay is not mercy. Apply the rehire test, tell the truth early, learn your country's rules before you need them, and when the end comes, say it in the first minute and land the person as softly as you can.
  • The bad feeling after a kind, well-done goodbye is the correct price of the job. Worry when you stop paying it.
