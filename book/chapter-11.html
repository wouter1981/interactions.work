Chapter 11

Building the Skeleton and Nervous System

As the company grows, it needs a structure to hold its shape and an information flow to feel its own pain, and you have to build both on purpose, because neither one grows on its own.

Marta called me on a Tuesday evening in November, and she opened with a sentence I want you to memorize, because one day, if things go well for you, you will say it too.

"Nobody tells me anything anymore."

You have met Marta before in this book, usually mid-crisis, which is unfair to her because between the crises she built one of the best design agencies I know. By the November in question the agency had grown to twenty-five people. Two floors of a canal building, a real management team, a waiting list of clients. From the outside, the good years. From the inside, Marta was discovering something that no one had warned her about, and that I am now warning you about, which is that a company can grow itself deaf.

The trigger was almost comically small. She had been at the opening of a client's new flagship store, glass of wine in hand, doing the founder thing where you represent the company by standing near its logo. The client's marketing director came over, squeezed her arm, and said, "I just want to say, your team handled the whole rebrand mess so gracefully. Most agencies would have hidden it from us."

Marta smiled, said something warm, and spent the rest of the evening trying to find out, without admitting she was trying to find out, what the rebrand mess was.

It turned out to be substantial. A junior designer had built three weeks of campaign work on top of the client's old brand guidelines, the ones that had been retired in the spring. A file server folder with a misleading name, an account manager who spotted it late, a heroic weekend of rework, an honest and rather elegant apology call to the client. Her team had handled it well. Genuinely well, better than Marta herself would have handled it at twenty-six, and possibly better than she would have handled it now. That was not the part that kept her up.

The part that kept her up was the timeline. The mistake had been discovered five weeks before the store opening. The rework weekend was four weeks before. The apology call, three and a half. And the founder of the company found out from the client, at a party, from a woman holding a tray-sized glass of Grüner Veltliner.

So she did what you do at two in the morning, which is inventory every other thing she might not know. By the time she called me she had found three. Her most senior designer was interviewing at a competitor, which everyone on the design floor knew and no one upstairs did. A retainer client had quietly halved their monthly scope, which finance knew and the account team assumed finance had escalated, and finance assumed the account team had. And the studio had switched project management tools, an actual migration of the actual system the company ran on, and Marta learned about it when her old login stopped working.

"Twenty-five people," she said on the phone. "I hired every single one of them. I know their partners' names. I know who takes oat milk. And I found out about a client crisis from the client. At a party. Wouter, they are hiding things from me."

They were not hiding things from her, and if you are in her position, they are almost certainly not hiding things from you either. That is the diagnosis your brain reaches first, because it is the dramatic one, the one with villains in it. Betrayal, cliques, disloyalty. And in Marta's case it was wrong on every count. Her people liked her. Several of them, we later found out, had individually assumed she already knew each of the things she did not know. One of them had drafted an email to her about the rebrand mess and deleted it because "the Monday meeting seemed like the right place, and then the Monday meeting got cancelled."

What had actually happened was not a people problem. It was a physics problem. Marta's agency had outgrown its skeleton and its nervous system, except that is not quite right either, because the truth is it never had them. It had Marta. She had been the skeleton, she had been the nervous system, and at twenty-five people, one woman's spine and synapses were no longer enough to hold the shape of the thing and feel its pain. Nothing was broken. Something had grown, and the infrastructure had not grown with it.

This chapter is about building that infrastructure on purpose: the structure that holds a growing company's shape, which I will call the skeleton, and the information flow that lets it sense what is happening to itself, which I will call the nervous system. Roles, meetings, documentation, feedback loops, and metrics. The boring words. I promise you the chapter is not boring, because behind each boring word is a way your company is currently either standing upright or quietly going numb.

The Mathematics of Silence

Let me take the drama out of "nobody tells me anything anymore" with some arithmetic, because the arithmetic is the actual villain, and once you see it you will stop suspecting your people.

Information in a small company travels through pairs. A conversation is a pair. A Slack message, a shared cigarette, a car ride to a client, a raised eyebrow across the studio, all pairs. With four people, there are six possible pairs. Everyone hears everything, usually twice. With eight people there are twenty-eight pairs, and it still mostly works, because you all fit in one room and information behaves like smell: whatever is cooking, everybody knows.

At twelve people there are sixty-six pairs. At twenty-five, there are three hundred. At forty, where Marta's agency eventually landed, seven hundred and eighty. The number of possible conversations grows with the square of the number of people, and your working hours do not grow at all. Somewhere between twelve and twenty-five people, the smell model of information flow stops working, and it fails silently, which is the cruel part. No alarm goes off. The company simply stops smelling like anything, and everyone assumes that means nothing is burning.

There is a second mechanism stacked on top of the arithmetic, and it is about you specifically. In the early years, the founder is the hub. Every important piece of information passes through you because every important decision passes through you, and people learn the habit: when in doubt, tell the founder. But as the company grows, two things erode that habit at the same time. First, you become busy in a new way, visibly busy, calendar-blocked busy, and every person who might have mentioned something small to you now performs a quick cost-benefit calculation in the doorway of your office and concludes that their small thing does not justify interrupting your big thing. Multiply that doorway calculation by twenty-five people and by every working day, and you have built a company that systematically filters out small things. Unfortunately, every large problem you will ever have starts its life as a small thing.

Second, you have become the boss in a way you were not before, even if you hate the word, even if you sit in the middle of the room and take out the recycling. Hierarchy has a gradient, and information flows down the gradient easily and up the gradient badly. Telling the founder good news is a pleasure. Telling the founder ambiguous news is a chore. Telling the founder bad news is a risk, and people manage risk by waiting, softening, and delegating the telling to someone else, who is also waiting. None of this is cowardice. It is what humans do around power, and you have power now, whether you ordered it or not.

Add a third mechanism: diffusion of responsibility. In a company of six, if you know something important, you know you are probably the only path it has to the founder. In a company of twenty-five, there is always a plausible someone else. Surely the account director mentioned it. Surely it came up in the management meeting. The rebrand mess reached everyone in Marta's company who could act on it, and reached her not at all, because every single person who knew was certain she already did. Information does not just fail to arrive in a growing company. It fails to arrive while everyone believes it has arrived, which is why the founder is always the last to know and always astonished.

So the silence is structural, not personal. That should comfort you, and then it should worry you in a more productive direction, because structural problems do not respond to the fixes founders reach for first. Marta's initial plan, sketched in that midnight phone call, was to have more coffees. Walk the floor more, ask more questions, be more approachable. All fine things, none of them scale. She would have been buying back individual pairs of communication at retail price, three hundred pairs and rising, with hours she did not have. What she actually needed was to stop being the infrastructure and start building some, and that is the rest of this chapter.

An Anatomy Lesson

Every growing company has to build two different things at roughly the same time, and most founders build at most one of them, which is why the metaphor in this chapter's title is worth taking seriously for a few minutes.

The skeleton is structure. Roles, reporting lines, decision rights. Who owns what, who answers for what, where one person's job ends and another's begins, and at which joints the company bends when it needs to move. The skeleton's job is to hold the company's shape without anyone actively holding it up. When the skeleton is missing, the company keeps its shape the way a tent keeps its shape, with the founder as the pole. Take the founder out for two weeks, the tent sags. You can measure how much skeleton your company has by imagining your own uninterrupted holiday and counting the things that would simply stop.

The nervous system is information flow. Meetings, documentation, metrics, feedback loops. Its job is not to hold shape but to carry signal: to make sure that when something happens at the fingertip, the brain finds out, at a speed proportional to how much it hurts. When the nervous system is missing, the company can look magnificent and be injured at the same time, because nothing connects the injury to anyone who could act on it. Marta's rebrand mess was exactly this, a burn on the hand that the head learned about five weeks later, from a bystander, at a party.

The two systems fail differently, and the failures have different smells, which is useful for diagnosis. A company with nerves but no bones is all signal and no shape: everyone knows everything, everyone discusses everything, and nothing gets decided, because no question has an owner. It feels warm and collaborative and it moves like a jellyfish. A company with bones but no nerves is the opposite: a crisp org chart, tidy job titles, clean reporting lines, and total numbness. Decisions get made confidently by people who have no idea what is actually happening three metres away. Big companies usually die the second death. Small companies usually die the first. Companies in between, the twenty-five-person kind, manage to have both at once, jellyfish in the studio and skeleton in the boardroom, and the two halves stop speaking.

One warning before we start building. The order matters less than founders think, but the pairing matters enormously. Every bone you add needs a nerve attached, and every nerve needs a bone. Give someone ownership of client relationships without a channel through which they report on client relationships, and you have created a silo with a nice title. Create a weekly reporting ritual without anyone actually owning the things being reported on, and you have created a book club. Bone, then nerve, then check that they are connected. That is the whole method, applied five times, and it is what the next sections do: roles, meetings, documentation, feedback loops, metrics.

A boundary note, so you know what this chapter is not. How you personally lead the people inside this structure, delegation, feedback, your own bottleneck behaviour, is chapter twelve. Whether you adopt a packaged operating system for the rhythms described here, EOS or one of its cousins, is chapter thirteen. And finding the people who fill the roles is chapter ten, which you have just read. This chapter is the architecture between those three: the shape of the machine, not the operator and not the brand of the machine.

Bones: Roles That Carry Weight

Start with the skeleton, because signal without structure has nowhere to land. And start the skeleton with a distinction that sounds pedantic and turns out to run the whole show: the difference between a job title and a role.

A job title is what goes on a business card and a contract. A role is a bundle of ownership: a set of things that one person sees, decides, and answers for. Titles are for the outside world. Roles are for the inside one, and inside a company of eight to forty people, they almost never line up one to one. Your studio manager might hold four roles: capacity planning, supplier relationships, the office itself, and onboarding logistics. Your senior developer might hold two: the architecture of the product and the security of it. The title is singular. The ownership is plural, and it is the ownership you have to make explicit, because ownership is where things fall when nobody catches them.

The test for whether a role is real, as opposed to decorative, has three parts, and a role has to pass all three. The owner sees it: information about that domain reaches them by default, without their having to go hunting. The owner decides it: within agreed limits, they can act without asking. And the owner answers for it: when the domain goes well or badly, everyone, including them, knows whose name is on it. Sees, decides, answers. A role that sees and answers but cannot decide is a scapegoat position. A role that decides but does not answer is a hobby with a budget. You will find both in most growing companies, usually created by accident, usually occupied by someone quietly going mad.

Two rules govern the whole exercise, and they do most of the work.

Rule one: every important thing has exactly one owner. Not zero, which is how the rebrand mess happened, the retired brand guidelines were a domain nobody owned, so nobody archived the old folder. And not two, which feels safer and is worse, because shared ownership is a machine for manufacturing assumptions. Marta's halved retainer sat between finance and the account team for six weeks precisely because both owned client revenue a little, which meant neither owned it at all. When I hear "we all own quality here," I translate it in my head to "no one owns quality here, and we have agreed not to mention it." If a thing genuinely needs two people, split the thing, not the ownership. One owns the promise to the client, the other owns the process that delivers it. Now it is two things, each with one name on it.

Rule two: the org chart describes the company you are, not the company you are imitating. At twenty-five people you do not need a head of people, a chief revenue officer, and an operations director because a scale-up on LinkedIn has them. You need the actual load-bearing work of your actual company mapped to actual humans. Draw it from the work upwards, not from the template downwards. List the ten domains that would hurt if they were dropped for a month, client relationships, delivery quality, cash and invoicing, the pipeline, the product or the craft itself, the team's wellbeing, the tools everyone runs on, and so on, then put one name next to each. That page, one column of domains and one column of names, is worth more than any organogram, and it fits on one page for years.

Joints: Where the Decisions Live

Bones alone are rigid. What makes a skeleton useful is the joints, the agreed places where movement happens, and in a company the joints are decision rights: who may decide what, up to what limit, after consulting whom. Most growing companies never write these down, and the result is a strange double failure. Half the decisions queue up outside the founder's door, because nobody is sure they are allowed to make them. The other half get made three times by three different people, each certain it was theirs. You get bottleneck and chaos simultaneously, which people wrongly read as a contradiction. It is not. It is what missing joints look like.

The fix is a short, boring, glorious table. For each recurring decision type, write down who decides, who must be consulted before they decide, and who must be told after. Here is roughly what Marta's looked like once we built it, translated so a software founder or a bakery owner can steal it equally well.

Notice what the table is really doing. It is not distributing power for ideological reasons. It is pre-answering the doorway calculation. The person hovering at your office door, wondering whether their €300 software subscription justifies interrupting you, now has an answer that does not involve your door at all. Every row you add to that table buys back founder hours and, more importantly, buys back speed for everyone else. And notice the last column, "told after," because it is the nerve attached to this particular bone. A decision right without a reporting duty creates silos. The account lead may give the 8% discount alone, and finance hears about it the same week, every time, no exceptions. Autonomy in the decision, transparency in the aftermath. That trade is the joint working as designed.

Notice also what stays with the founder, and why. Firing a client and guarding the brand promise are not there because they are the biggest decisions by euros. They are there because they are identity decisions, the kind that define what sort of company this is, and identity is the one domain the founder cannot delegate at this size. If that sentence raises questions about what your company's identity even is, that is chapter one, and it is cheaper to answer it there than here.

A caution about the most common bone-setting mistake at this stage: the player-coach. You promote your best designer to lead the design team, and you let her keep a full client load, because you cannot spare her hands. Now she has two roles that fight for the same hours, and the coaching role loses every single time, because clients phone and team members do not. Six months later you conclude she "was not management material," when what actually happened is that you set a bone across a joint and were surprised the limb would not bend. If someone is to own a team's output, take real weight off them first. The skeleton has to obey physics.

Try this

Take a pen and list the last ten meaningful decisions made in your company, anything from a hire to a discount to a tool change. For each one, write who made it, who believed they should have made it, and how long it waited in someone's head before it was made. You are looking for two patterns: orphans, decisions that circled for weeks because nobody was sure they owned them, and doubles, decisions made more than once by different people. Every orphan and every double is a missing row in your decision table. Write those rows tonight. The table can be seven lines long and still change your month.

Synapses: Meetings That Move the Company

Now the nerves, and we start with the most maligned organ in business: the meeting.

Founders at this stage tend to hold one of two theological positions on meetings. The first camp believes meetings are where work goes to die, cancels as many as possible, and is proud of the empty calendar. The second camp responds to every dropped ball by scheduling a recurring meeting about that category of ball, and after two years of this, the calendar looks like a mosaic and nobody can find three consecutive hours to think. Both camps are managing their feelings about meetings rather than the function of them, and the function is this: a meeting is a synapse. It is a place where signal jumps from one part of the company to another. A company with too few synapses is numb. A company with too many is having a seizure. The question is never "do we like meetings," it is "where does signal need to jump, how often, and what is the cheapest reliable way to make it jump there."

Cheapest matters, because meetings have a price and almost nobody prices them. A one-hour meeting with six people is six working hours, and at a blended cost of, say, €60 an hour once you include everything an employer pays, that meeting costs €360 each time it runs. Weekly, that is around €17,000 a year. That is a decent used car, every year, for one recurring slot in the calendar. You would never approve a €17,000 invoice without asking what it buys. Meetings arrive without invoices, which is the only reason they survive unexamined.

The Three Honest Purposes

Every meeting worth having does one of three jobs, and a meeting that cannot say which job it does is already dead, whatever the calendar says.

Synchronize. Move current state between people so their next week of decisions is made with the same map. Sync meetings should be short, rhythmic, and ruthless about staying at the level of state, what changed, what is stuck, what is coming, and not sliding into solving. The moment a sync meeting starts solving a problem, it is stealing time from the five people who are not needed for that problem.

Decide. Put a specific question, the options, and the people with the authority and the context in one room, and leave with a decision and an owner. Decision meetings need preparation to exist beforehand, which is why they pair so well with the written material from the next section. A decision meeting without a written question at the top is a debate club.

Create. Do actual joint work: shape a proposal, workshop a design, take apart a failure. Creating meetings are the only kind allowed to be long, and they earn the length by producing an artifact, something that exists afterwards and did not exist before.

Synchronize, decide, create. Status theater is not on the list, and status theater is what most recurring meetings decay into: a circle of people reading out loud to a founder things that could have been written down, while everyone else rehearses their own paragraph. The tell is that nobody in the room changes their plans based on anything they hear. Signal jumps to no one. The synapse fires into the void, weekly, at €360 a firing.

Story

Two weeks after the Grüner Veltliner incident, Marta and I sat down with her calendar and did the audit you are about to see in table form. We found eleven recurring meetings. She could explain the purpose of six of them. The other five included a Thursday "alignment hour" that had been created two years earlier, during a crisis with a hotel client, to coordinate daily fixes. The hotel client had left, on good terms, fourteen months before. The meeting had continued, weekly, attended by seven people, because it was in the calendar and the calendar is a form of law. Nobody could say what it was for, and nobody had felt entitled to kill it, because meetings, unlike expenses, have no owner and no invoice.

Marta did the arithmetic on that one meeting herself, on a napkin, and I watched her face do something complicated. Seven people, one hour, roughly fifty runs. "That meeting cost more than my first year's salary at the agency," she said. Then she went quiet for a moment and said the more important thing: "And the rebrand mess never came up in it. Seven people, an hour a week, and the biggest fire in the company never once made it onto the table." That is the double crime of a dead meeting. It burns hours, and it squats in the place where a live synapse should be, so everyone feels informed while knowing nothing.

The audit that catches this is simple enough to do in an afternoon, and I think every company should run it once a year, the way you service a boiler. List every recurring meeting. For each one, write down its cadence, its yearly cost in hours, the job it claims to do, the job it actually does, and a verdict: keep, fix, or kill. Be as honest in the "actually does" column as you would be about a competitor's meetings. Here is Marta's, abbreviated, with the arithmetic done at that same blended €60 an hour.

Three patterns in that table generalize to almost every company I have seen inside. First, the fix is very often "write it down first, then meet about the exceptions." A written weekly update takes each person ten minutes to write and two minutes per reader, and it frees the meeting to do the one thing writing cannot, which is argue productively. Second, the meetings most worth protecting, the one-to-ones, are precisely the ones that get cancelled first, because their cost is visible and their value is invisible until the quarter after you stop doing them. A cancelled one-to-one is exactly how a senior designer ends up interviewing at a competitor with everyone downstairs knowing and nobody upstairs. Third, every meeting needs one named owner, the person who writes the agenda, starts it on time, and holds the standing right to conclude it is no longer needed. A meeting without an owner cannot be killed, and what cannot be killed becomes the Thursday alignment hour.

Set the cadences to match the speed of the underlying reality, not the gravity of the topic. Things that change weekly get weekly synapses: delivery, pipeline, cash if it is tight. Things that change quarterly get quarterly ones: strategy, the shape of the team, pricing. The classic error is inverting this, meeting weekly about strategy, which does not change week to week, so the meeting invents strategy weather to discuss, while cash, which changes daily, gets one anxious look a month. And if you want a full prefabricated cadence, an annual, quarterly, weekly rhythm with names and worksheets, that is what operating systems like EOS sell, and they are chapter thirteen; here it is enough that each meeting knows its job, its price, and its owner.

Long-Term Memory: Documentation People Actually Use

Meetings move signal through space, from person to person. Documentation moves it through time, from the person who knew to the person who will need to know, months later, possibly after the first person has left. A company without documentation is a company with anterograde amnesia: charming, energetic, and condemned to relive its own mistakes with a fresh sense of discovery each time. The junior designer who built three weeks of work on retired brand guidelines was not careless. She did exactly what the company's memory told her to do, because the company's memory was a file server where the old truth and the new truth sat side by side, identically confident.

Founders resist documentation for one respectable reason and one embarrassing one. The respectable reason is that they have seen the failure mode: the wiki nobody reads, the process manual that was obsolete before the ink dried, the quality handbook written for a certification audit and never opened since. The embarrassing reason is that while everything lives in the founder's head, the founder is indispensable, and some part of every founder likes being the only one who knows where the bodies are buried. I will deal with the respectable reason here. The embarrassing one is chapter twelve's problem, and I promise it gets dealt with.

The failure mode is real, but it comes from writing the wrong things. Companies that drown in useless documentation almost always documented processes first, in exhaustive imperative prose, how to do everything, in eleven steps, with screenshots that expired the next quarter. Useful company memory has three shelves, and process is only one of them, and not the first.

The Three Shelves

Shelf one: what is true right now. The current price list. The current brand guidelines, with the old ones visibly archived, not sitting alongside. Who owns what, the one-page role list from earlier in this chapter. The current org chart, the current client list, the current standard contract. This shelf is small, a dozen documents, and its whole value is a single guarantee: if it is on this shelf, it is current, and if it is current, it is on this shelf. One person owns the shelf, its contents each have owners, and anything superseded is moved out the same day its replacement arrives. Marta's rebrand mess was a shelf-one failure costing five figures. The shelf costs an afternoon a month.

Shelf two: what we decided, and why. A decision log. One page per meaningful decision: the date, the question, the options considered, what was chosen, why, and who owned it. Ten minutes to write, and it appreciates in value every year, because the "why" is the part of a decision that dies first. Two years from now nobody will ask what tool you chose, the tool is right there being used. They will ask why, usually in a tone suggesting the decision was idiotic, and either the log answers them in thirty seconds, or the company re-litigates the whole question from scratch, with the added spice that the people who remember the context have left. I started a decision log at the routing company in year five, after we spent an entire offsite reversing a pricing decision for reasons that turned out to be the exact reasons we had originally made it. The log would have cost ten minutes. The offsite cost a weekend and two friendships' worth of goodwill.

Shelf three: how we do things. The runbooks and processes, yes, but written under a strict admission rule: a process gets written down the second time someone asks how to do it, not before, and it gets written by the person who answered, in twenty lines or fewer, linked from the place where the work actually happens. Not a manual, a set of recipes, each one earned by a real repeated question. This rule keeps shelf three honest. Documentation written on demand describes work that actually recurs. Documentation written in a visionary mood describes work the author wishes recurred, and that is the wiki nobody reads.

Where should the shelves live? In the most boring tool you already have, and I mean that as rigorous advice, not a joke. The gains here come from the guarantee and the habit, never from the software, and every hour spent evaluating knowledge platforms is an hour of shelf-one work not done. One place, searchable, linked where people work. If your company can hold the guarantee in a folder tree, hold it in a folder tree.

Field note

Documentation is written for readers, not writers. Before saving any document, ask the reader's three questions: how will they find this, will they trust it is current when they do, and can they act on it in under five minutes? A document that fails the first question does not exist. One that fails the second is worse than not existing, because a reader burned once by a stale document never trusts the shelf again, and the guarantee, once broken, is very expensive to rebuild.

One more thing documentation quietly buys you, and at this company size it is not a small thing: calm onboarding. Every new person who joins a company with a real shelf one and shelf three gets productive weeks earlier and interrupts their colleagues hundreds of times less. The mechanics of onboarding belong to chapter ten, but the raw material of onboarding is this section, and companies that complain new hires take six months to be useful are usually companies where the first three months are spent conducting an oral history project.

Reflexes: How Fast Does Bad News Travel

Here is the section that would have saved Marta her November, so let me put its central claim in the plainest terms I can manage. Good news travels through a company on its own. Bad news needs infrastructure. If you build nothing, you will still hear about every won pitch, every happy client, every launch, usually within the hour, with photographs. What you will not hear about is the senior designer interviewing elsewhere, the retainer quietly halved, the project three weeks late and being heroically concealed by people who intend to fix it before you notice. Not because anyone is malicious, but because of the gradient we met earlier: carrying bad news uphill costs the carrier something, and unless you build structures that lower the cost, most people, most days, will rationally decide not to pay it.

Think of it as a reflex arc. When you touch a hot stove, your hand pulls back before your conscious brain knows anything happened, because the body does not route pain through committees. A healthy company has the same property: the people closest to the burn act immediately, and the signal still travels up, at pain-proportional speed, so the rest of the organism can adjust. Marta's agency, remember, had half of this. The hand pulled back beautifully, her team fixed the rebrand mess with real skill. The signal simply never travelled. And a company that acts locally but never informs centrally will handle each fire well and learn nothing across fires, because learning lives at the level that never heard.

Silence in a growing company is not peace. It is packet loss.

So you build the reflexes deliberately. Four of them cover most of what a company this size needs, and none takes more than an hour a week from anyone.

The written weekly update. Every role owner writes a short weekly note, and the format is the whole trick: what moved, what is stuck, what is coming, and, the load-bearing line, what I am worried about. Four headings, ten minutes, published where the management team and ideally everyone can read it. The worry line is the reflex. It creates a cheap, low-drama, socially normal channel for exactly the information that the gradient suppresses. Saying "I am worried the Meridian project is drifting" in week two is easy, precisely because nothing has happened yet. The update line costs the writer nothing and buys the company three weeks of options. Without the channel, that same worry has to mature into a confession before anyone voices it, and confessions arrive late by definition.

The post-project debrief, especially for the good ones that were secretly bad. Within two weeks of any project ending, the team spends forty-five minutes on three questions: what actually happened versus what we planned, what surprised us, what will we do differently. Written down, on shelf two, and the founder does not run the meeting and ideally is not in the room, because the gradient attends every meeting you attend. The discipline that matters is doing them always, not just after disasters. Debriefs held only after failures become tribunals, and people prepare defences instead of observations. Debriefs held always become boring, and boring is the goal. A boring debrief is a company examining itself without flinching.

The skip-level conversation, done honestly. Once a quarter, sit down with people who do not report to you, one at a time, and ask questions that are actually answerable: what is harder than it should be, what would you fix tomorrow if you ran the place, what do you think I am wrong about. Then, and this is the part that determines whether the channel lives or dies, do something visible about at least one thing you hear, and credit the channel: "several of you told me the review process is strangling small projects, so we are changing it." The first time you do that, the channel's bandwidth triples. And a warning from someone who got this wrong at the routing company: never use a skip-level to check up on the manager in between, and never repeat what you heard with a name attached. One traceable quote reaching the wrong ear closes the channel for a year, and everyone in the company will know why, even though nobody will say it in front of you.

The closed loop. The most neglected reflex, and the cheapest. When anyone reports a problem, something visible must happen: it gets fixed, or it gets a named owner and a date, or someone explains honestly why it will not be fixed. Any of the three keeps the nerve alive, even the no. What kills the nerve is the fourth outcome, silence, the report that vanishes into the founder's inbox. People do not stop reporting problems because they are ignored once. They stop because they are ignored twice, and then they train the people around them: "don't bother, nothing happens." When a founder tells me "my people never raise problems," I ask what happened to the last three problems someone raised. There is usually a pause, and inside the pause is the answer.

Try this

Measure your bad-news latency. Take the last three pieces of genuinely bad news that reached you: a slipping project, an unhappy client, a person on the way out. For each, write two dates: when the first person in the company knew, and when you knew. The gap is your latency, and the founders I make do this typically find weeks where they would have guessed days. Then, for the worst one, trace the route: who knew, who they told, where it stalled, and what the stall would have needed to un-stall. You are not hunting the person who stayed silent. You are hunting the missing reflex, the update line, the debrief, the closed loop that was not there. Build that one first.

Sense Organs: Metrics That Carry Signal

Reflexes carry pain. Metrics are the other half of the sensory system, the eyes and ears, the numbers that let a company perceive things too slow or too diffuse for anyone to feel directly: margins eroding a point a quarter, a pipeline thinning, one client growing until it is quietly holding the company hostage. Nobody feels these in their fingertips. You need instruments.

And here I have to make a distinction that took me an embarrassing number of years to learn, so I will give it to you at the price I wish I had paid: the difference between a metric that carries signal and a metric that is decoration. Companies do not usually suffer from too few numbers. They suffer from dashboards full of decorative ones, numbers that are tracked because they are easy to track, presented because they look rigorous, and connected to no decision whatsoever. Decoration is worse than ignorance, because ignorance at least knows itself. A founder with a dashboard feels informed, and a wall of green numbers is the corporate equivalent of a body that cannot feel pain: it photographs well and walks into fires.

A metric carries signal if it passes four tests, and it has to pass all four, which most dashboard numbers do not.

Someone owns it. One person, the role owner from earlier, whose number it is, who knows it moved before the meeting and arrives with the story of why. A metric without an owner is weather: interesting, discussed, acted on by no one. This is the bone-and-nerve pairing again, a sense organ has to be wired to a limb that can move.

It can change a decision. Before adopting any metric, name the decision it informs and the threshold that would trigger it: if utilization stays over 90% for six weeks, we hire or we refuse work; if one client passes 30% of revenue, we actively rebalance sales effort. No nameable decision, no threshold, no metric. Track it in a spreadsheet if it comforts you, but keep it off the dashboard, because attention is the scarcest resource in the room and decoration spends it.

It is allowed to look bad. The metric measures reality, not the team's morale or the founder's mood. The instant a number is punished for being red, it stops being a sense organ and becomes a performance, and everyone downstream of it starts optimizing the number instead of the thing. You have then paid to blind yourself.

It leads where possible. Revenue tells you what happened two quarters ago in your pipeline. Proposals sent, first conversations booked, project margin at the halfway mark, these tell you what revenue will do while there is still time to act. A dashboard of only lagging indicators is a rearview mirror, honest and useless.

Story

At the routing company, somewhere around thirty-five people, we built the dashboard of my dreams. Forty-one metrics, refreshed live, on a television in the office. Sign-ups, uptime, support response times, feature usage, revenue in three cuts. Visitors were impressed. I was impressed, and I had commissioned it. And in the two quarters that television glowed its confident green, the company had its worst stretch in years: our biggest customer was integrating a competitor, our sales cycle had lengthened by six weeks, and two of our best engineers were burning out on a rewrite I kept extending. The television knew none of this. Forty-one numbers, and not one of them owned by anyone, not one wired to a decision, not one capable of turning red for the things that actually threatened us. It was a stained-glass window: expensive, beautiful, and there to be admired, not seen through.

We eventually replaced it with eight numbers on a shared page, each with a name next to it, reviewed weekly by the person named. And we added the rule I now give to every founder who will hold still long enough: a dashboard on which nothing is red is broken. Reality always contains at least one problem. If your instruments disagree with that, the fault is in the instruments, and the repair is a standing question at the weekly review: "what should be red here that isn't?" The first time someone answers honestly, your dashboard starts working, and it never looks impressive on a television again.

Two traps to step around as you build this. The first is measuring what is easy instead of what is load-bearing, hours logged instead of margin earned, because the tool exports one and not the other. The tool's convenience is not an argument; the number you need is the number you need. The second trap is the metric review that never changes anything, ten minutes of nodding at numbers before the real agenda. The cure is mechanical: every red number gets a sentence in the decision log, either what we are doing about it or why we are choosing to accept it this quarter. Written acceptance is honourable. Silent nodding is how a company practices ignoring its own instruments, weekly, until it gets good at it. And to keep this chapter in its lane: which numbers deserve to be company-wide goals and how they cascade into quarterly targets is chapter thirteen's territory; here we only care that the instruments exist, are owned, and are allowed to hurt.

How Much Skeleton, and When

By this point in the chapter, a certain kind of reader is compiling an enthusiastic to-do list: role charters, decision tables, three shelves, four reflexes, a dashboard. Before you build all of it this weekend, we need to talk about dosage, because structure is a medicine, and like all medicine it has a therapeutic range. Too little and the disease proceeds. Too much and you have poisoned the patient with the cure, and a small company poisoned by structure is a genuinely sad thing to visit: nine people, a steering committee, a change request form, and a founder who read a book like this one with too much appetite.

The dosing principle is this: add a bone when the pain is recurring, not the first time it twinges. Every structure in this chapter is a response to a pattern. One orphaned decision is a Tuesday; the same decision orphaned three times is a missing row in the table. One person asking how to invoice a client is a conversation; the second person asking is a shelf-three recipe. One late project is a project; late projects as a genre are a missing halfway-mark metric. The discipline of waiting for the second or third occurrence protects you from building structure for problems you do not have, which is the more common failure among founders who read books, while the founders who do not read books reliably fail in the other direction and wait for the ninth occurrence, by which point the pattern has cost five figures and a resignation.

Rough thresholds, which you should treat as weather forecasts rather than physics. Up to about eight or ten people, you need almost nothing from this chapter: one page of who-owns-what, a weekly sync, a shared folder with a current price list, and honesty at lunch. The pairs arithmetic still works at that size, and premature structure just adds friction to a company whose whole advantage is that it has none. From ten to about twenty, build the first full set: real roles with the sees-decides-answers test, the decision table, the weekly written updates, the small dashboard. Somewhere between twenty and thirty comes the wall Marta hit, where a second layer of ownership becomes unavoidable, some people now owning domains through other people, and this is exactly where founders stall, because adding that layer feels like admitting the family has become a company. It has. It became one around hire eleven, and pretending otherwise does not preserve the family feeling, it just deprives the company of joints while keeping the founder as the load-bearing pole. Past thirty-five or forty, the machine needs a formal operating rhythm tying all of this together, and that conversation, frameworks included, is chapter thirteen, with the what-breaks-next inventory in chapter fourteen.

How do you know your dose is right? The company tells you, in symptoms, and it is worth learning to read both directions. Too little skeleton sounds like: every decision waits for the founder, the same fire twice, "I assumed someone else was handling it," a two-week holiday that requires a month of preparation and a month of repair. Too much sounds like: a meeting to prepare for a meeting, people quoting process at each other instead of talking, a form where a conversation used to be, and the phrase "that's not my job" arriving in a company too small to contain it. When in doubt, under-build and listen. It is far easier to add a bone to a company in visible recurring pain than to remove one that a department has grown fond of.

Warning

Do not import your skeleton from a company ten times your size, and be especially careful if you hire senior people from large organizations, because they will, with the best intentions, prescribe the anatomy they know: job families, committees, approval chains, quarterly business reviews with pre-reads for the pre-reads. Big-company structure solves big-company problems, most of which are problems of coordination at a scale you have not reached and may never reach. Your problems are different: speed, clarity, and not being deaf. Steal individual tools from the big companies by all means, they have excellent tools, but let the recurring pain of your actual company, not someone's former employer, decide which bones get built.

And a final word on this section addressed to the founder who has read this far while privately planning to build none of it, because structure feels like the death of the thing they loved. I have real sympathy for this position, I held it for years. Here is what dissolved it. The absence of structure does not mean nobody holds the company's shape and carries its signal. It means you do, personally, with your calendar and your evenings and your marriage, and every year the company grows, the toll goes up. The pole in the middle of the tent does not experience the tent as free and unstructured. Structure, built right and dosed right, is not what kills the early magic. It is what spares one human from being consumed to fake it.

Marta, Eighteen Months Later

I want to end with what actually happened at the agency, partly because you have earned an ending and partly because the honest version resists a tidy arc in ways that are themselves instructive.

Story

Eighteen months after the November phone call, I visited Marta's agency, thirty-two people by then, on an ordinary Wednesday. At the Monday all-hands two days earlier, twenty minutes, never cancelled, an account lead had stood up and said, in front of everyone including two people who had joined that month, that the agency's biggest retainer client was making unhappy noises about response times, that it was her worry line from three weeks running, and that she and the studio lead had changed the staffing on the account and would know within a month if it had worked. Total drama: none. Total elapsed time between the first unhappy noise and the founder knowing: two days, and Marta had heard it through the weekly update before the all-hands said it out loud.

"That exact situation," Marta told me, "is the rebrand mess. Same size, same client type, same slow leak. In the old company I would have found out at a party." Then, because she is honest, she gave me the other half of the ledger. The first decision table had needed three rewrites, because she kept unconsciously drafting herself into the "consulted" column of everything, and her management team kept crossing her out. Two of the four reflexes had died on the first attempt and needed relaunching with owners who actually believed in them. One person, a talented designer who had loved the old formlessness, had looked at the new skeleton and left, sincerely and without bitterness, and Marta still counts it as a real cost. "The book version of this story ends with the all-hands," she said. "The true version is that we built it wrong twice first, and it cost us someone I liked."

That is the shape of the work: not a renovation you complete but a fit you maintain, because the company keeps growing and the skeleton that fits at twenty-five pinches at forty. What changes permanently is the founder's job description. Marta now knows less raw detail about her company than she has ever known, and learns the important things faster than she ever has. Those two facts feel contradictory and are actually the whole point. You do not build the skeleton and nervous system so that you can know everything again. That version of the company is gone, and chasing it is how founders become bottlenecks with excellent intentions. You build them so the company can know things without you, act on them without you, and tell you what hurts while there is still time to do something about it.

Which leaves one last uncomfortable question sitting in the founder's chair: now that the company has a spine that is not you and senses that are not yours, what exactly is your job, and are you actually any good at it yet? That is chapter twelve, and I would bring something to write with.

The Short Version

  • Past a dozen people, "nobody tells me anything anymore" is arithmetic, not betrayal. Communication pairs grow with the square of headcount, and the founder-as-hub model fails silently.
  • Build two systems on purpose: a skeleton (roles, reporting lines, decision rights) that holds the company's shape, and a nervous system (meetings, documentation, feedback loops, metrics) that carries its signal. Every bone needs a nerve attached, and every nerve a bone.
  • A real role passes three tests: the owner sees it, decides it, and answers for it. Every important thing has exactly one owner, and "we all own it" means nobody does.
  • Write the decision table: who decides what, up to what limit, consulting whom, telling whom after. Every row buys back founder hours and everyone else's speed.
  • Price your meetings in euros and audit them yearly: keep, fix, or kill. Every meeting gets one owner and one honest job, synchronize, decide, or create. Write status down; meet about exceptions.
  • Company memory has three shelves: what is true now (guaranteed current), what we decided and why, and how we do things (written on the second ask, in twenty lines, linked where the work happens).
  • Good news travels alone; bad news needs infrastructure. Weekly worry lines, always-on debriefs, honest skip-levels, and closed loops. Measure your bad-news latency in days, not vibes.
  • A metric carries signal only if someone owns it, it can change a named decision, it is allowed to look bad, and it leads where possible. Ten numbers beat forty-one. A dashboard on which nothing is red is broken.
  • Dose the structure: add a bone when pain recurs, not on the first twinge, and never import a big company's anatomy into a small company's body.
  • The goal is not to know everything again. It is a company that can know, act, and feel pain without routing it all through you.
