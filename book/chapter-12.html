Chapter 12

Evolving Your Own Management and Leadership Skills

The biggest bottleneck in your company shaves your face every morning, and this chapter is about what happens after you finally admit it.

The first real holiday I took from the routing company, three years in, lasted nine days. I had prepared for it like a military operation: briefings, handover documents, a laminated sheet of phone numbers taped inside the server cabinet, and a solemn speech about how I trusted everyone completely. Then I flew to Portugal, checked my email eleven times a day, and came back braced for smoke.

There was no smoke. That was the horrifying part.

Nothing had burned down. Nothing had happened at all. I walked in on Monday morning and found forty-one decisions waiting for me, stacked in neat piles like airport luggage when the belt stops: a discount request from sales, a candidate the team liked but had not called back, a supplier contract printed out with a sticky note that said "when Wouter returns," a developer who had spent four days on the wrong feature because he was unsure and would not risk guessing wrong. My company, the thing I bragged about at birthdays, had paused without me. Like a video. Nine days of my team's working life, spent waiting for one man to come back from the beach.

I remember standing at the whiteboard where someone had actually written a list titled "For W," feeling two things at once. The first was a warm, shameful little glow. They need me. I matter. The second, arriving a few seconds later and staying for years, was the correct reaction: I have built a machine with a single point of failure, and the single point of failure gets sunburned.

I did what I did in those years whenever something confused me more than it should. I drove to Henk's house and sat at his kitchen table, and I complained.

Story

I gave Henk the full performance. My team would not take initiative. They escalated everything. Quality dropped the moment I stopped checking. I had hired adults and somehow ended up running a kindergarten with payroll. Henk drank his coffee, black as always, and let me finish, which should have been my first warning, because Henk never let me finish when I was right.

Then he stood up, walked me into his hallway, and positioned me in front of the mirror by the coat rack, hands on my shoulders like he was lining up a fence post.

"Look. You see him?"

I said I saw him.

"That is the manager of everyone you just complained about. Every complaint you made in my kitchen is a note about this man's work. His people wait? He taught them waiting is safe and guessing is dangerous. His people escalate everything? He grabs the wheel every time the car wobbles, so now nobody else steers. Quality drops when he looks away?" Henk tapped the glass, once, with one thick finger. "Then he built quality out of his own eyeballs instead of building it into the work. That is a design problem, and there is the designer."

I started to defend myself and he talked over me without raising his voice, the way he did everything.

"When one machine makes bad parts, maybe the machine. When every machine makes bad parts, the factory. You are the factory, boy. Come back when you have a complaint about the man in the mirror. That one we can fix."

It took me an embarrassingly long time to forgive that conversation, and slightly longer to understand it. This chapter is everything I learned after I did.

Here is the shape of the thing. Somewhere between roughly ten and forty people, every founder-led company runs into the same wall, and the wall is the founder. The job changed underneath them, quietly, without a memo, and they kept doing the old job with more hours and more heroics. The skills that built the company, doing everything, deciding instantly, being the best at the craft, caring about every detail, stop being the engine and start being the ceiling. Chapter 11 was about building the company's skeleton and nervous system. This chapter is about the remaining piece of infrastructure nobody audits: you.

Fair warning: this is one of the three chapters the introduction flags for emergencies, and if the field note sent you here on a bad night, welcome. You are load-bearing, that is all, and we are going to fix that.

How to Know You Are the Bottleneck

Nobody thinks they are the bottleneck. The bottleneck experience, from the inside, feels like being indispensable, which feels wonderful. You are busy, needed, consulted on everything, cc'd on everything, thanked constantly. Every symptom of the disease arrives disguised as a compliment.

So do not diagnose it by feeling. Diagnose it by queue. A bottleneck, in a factory or a founder, is the place where work stacks up and waits. You find it the same way Henk found it on a shop floor: you walk around and look for the piles.

Here are the piles, translated for a company:

  • The decision queue. Count every decision currently waiting on you and only you, down to the 200 euro ones. If the number surprises you, and it will, you have your answer.
  • The latency test. How long does a routine question take to get answered in your company? If the honest answer is "however long until Wouter reads Slack," your calendar is your company's clock speed.
  • The holiday test. The one I failed. Leave for a week, offline for real. If the company breaks, you have a competence problem in the team, which is chapter 10. If the company pauses, you have a you problem, which is this one. The pause is worse than the fire, because a fire at least tells you where it is.
  • The sentence test. Listen for "let me check with" followed by your name. In small doses that is prudence. As a company motto, it is a diagnosis.
  • The meeting test. Do discussions visibly reshape themselves when you enter the room? Do people wait to hear your view before offering theirs? Congratulations, your opinions have stopped being contributions and started being weather.

To see what the queue costs in practice, follow one item through it. A customer asks your account manager for a 15 percent discount on a renewal. She knows the customer, knows the margin, knows the answer is "10 percent against a two-year term," and she is right. But discounts are Wouter territory, so she writes it up and sends it to you. You are in interviews all day, so it waits. The customer emails her again on Thursday; she replies with something warm and empty. You read the request Friday evening, tired, between two other queued items, ask a clarifying question she answered in the original message, and by the time the answer is agreed, the customer has spent eight days learning that this supplier cannot answer a simple question in less than a week. The discount you finally approve is the one she proposed on Monday. Total value you added: zero. Total cost: eight days of latency, one slightly insulted account manager, one customer doing arithmetic about what support response times must look like if pricing takes this long. Now multiply by forty-one.

The cruel arithmetic is that all of this used to be fine. At five people, the founder touching everything is the operating model, and a good one. Every decision passing through you is fast, consistent, and informed by the whole picture, because at five people you genuinely hold the whole picture. At fifteen, you hold most of it, and touching everything costs a little. At thirty, you hold maybe half of it, and touching everything means half your decisions are made with worse information than the person who brought you the question already had. They knew the customer, the code, the oven, the situation. You knew a two-minute summary, and you overruled them with it.

That is the moment the compliment becomes a tax. Past it, every hour of company growth adds to the queue behind you, and the queue does something more expensive than waiting: it teaches. Every time someone brings you a decision they could have made and you make it, brisk and competent, you teach them that bringing decisions is how things work here. Each answer trains the asker. A year of that training produces exactly the team I complained about at Henk's table: capable adults who have rationally concluded that guessing is punished and waiting is free.

And be honest about one more thing, because I was not for years: part of you built the queue on purpose. Being the person everything waits for is the last version of being the person who does everything, and giving it up means finding out who you are when the company can breathe without you. Some founders keep the bottleneck the way other people keep an untidy garage: clearing it would raise questions they would rather not answer. If a small voice just objected to this paragraph, that voice is the one we are here to interview.

And say it plainly: they are behaving correctly. Given the incentives you built, waiting for you is the smart move. Which is why speeches about initiative change nothing. I gave the speech, twice. It works for about a week, until the next time you grab the wheel, and then everyone drifts back to the old rules, a little more cynical than before. The fix is changing what happens when they decide without you, and we will get to that in the delegation section, the longest in this chapter because it deserves to be.

Your strengths built the company. Applied one company size too late, the same strengths build the ceiling.

What Got You Here Stops Working

There is a well-worn phrase in the coaching world, what got you here won't get you there, and like most well-worn phrases it is worn because it is load-bearing. But I want to make it more uncomfortable than the poster version, because the poster version lets you off easy.

The poster version says you will need new skills. True, and toothless. The uncomfortable version says the exact behaviors that built your company will, past a certain size, damage it. Same founder, same moves, opposite effect.

Take the greatest hits of the early years:

  • Doing it yourself. Year one, this is the whole company. Year six, every task you do yourself is a task someone was not trained on, plus a signal that you do not trust the person paid to do it.
  • Deciding instantly. Year one, speed is your only advantage over the big slow competitors. Year six, your instant decisions land on people who were halfway through deciding it themselves, and each one teaches the lesson from the previous section.
  • Being the best at the craft. Year one, of course you are, there is nobody else. Year six, if you are still the best developer, baker, or designer in the building, that is an indictment of your hiring, and your best people can feel the ceiling an inch above their heads.
  • Caring about every detail. Year one, the details are the product. Year six, a founder who reviews everything is a queue with opinions.
  • Heroics. Year one, the all-nighter saves the customer and becomes a founding legend. Year six, your heroics paper over broken processes that would otherwise get fixed, and they set a standard of self-harm that your best people either imitate, resent, or leave over.

Notice what is not on the list: your judgment, your care for customers, your standards. Those transfer. It is the delivery mechanism that expires. The job stops being "apply my talent to the work" and becomes "build a machine that applies talent to the work," and those are so different that they might as well be different professions. The first is a craft. The second is a craft about crafts, and nobody is born with it, which is good news, because it means the awkwardness you feel is the standard tuition. Everyone pays it.

Why the year of lag? Because the old skills keep paying out small, immediate rewards long after they have stopped paying out large ones. Fix a bug yourself and you get the hit of visible progress in twenty minutes. Coach a developer to fix it and you get nothing today, a slightly slower fix this week, and a person who never brings you that bug again, which pays out forever but feels like nothing. The old job has better dopamine. The new job has better compounding. Every founder I know, me very much included, chased the dopamine for at least a year past the crossing, and most of us needed either a mirror speech or a small disaster to stop.

Delegation: The Skill Everyone Claims and Almost Nobody Has

Ask a room of founders whether they delegate and every hand goes up. Ask their teams and the hands come down. The gap exists because most of what founders call delegation is one of two cheaper things wearing delegation's coat.

The first is dumping: handing over a task with no context, no authority, and no definition of done, then expressing disappointment at the result. "Can you take care of the newsletter" is dumping. The person now owns your anxiety but not your information.

The second is puppeteering: handing over a task while keeping every decision. The person executes your choices with their hands. It looks like delegation on the org chart and feels like data entry from the inside. Puppeteering is worse than doing it yourself, because it costs two salaries to produce one person's judgment, and it slowly teaches a good hire to stop thinking.

Real delegation transfers decisions, not tasks, at an explicitly agreed level of autonomy. The load-bearing phrase there is "explicitly agreed." Almost every delegation disaster I have caused or witnessed came from two people holding different unspoken assumptions about who decides what, discovering the difference in production.

The tool that fixed this for me is old, unglamorous, and works: a delegation ladder. It names the levels of autonomy so that two people can point at a rung instead of guessing. Mine has six rungs, and the exact wording matters less than the fact that both of you can say the number out loud.

Three rules make the ladder work, and skipping any of them turns it back into decoration.

Rule one: assign rungs per topic, not per person. Your operations lead might be rung 5 on supplier choices, rung 3 on pricing, and rung 1 on anything involving lawyers. That is a map. The failure mode is granting one global trust level per human, which forces you to set it at the level of their weakest area, which makes strong people feel handcuffed everywhere because of one domain.

Rule two: climb one rung at a time, on schedule. The ladder is an escalator, and it should be moving. When you hand something over at rung 3, say in the same breath when you expect it to reach rung 4: "Three months of recommendations, then you decide and tell me." Without a schedule, rung 3 becomes a permanent residence, and you have built an approval bureaucracy with extra steps.

Rule three: when the ladder breaks, fix the rung, not the person. A bad outcome at rung 4 is information. Maybe the rung was right and the decision was one of the acceptable losses of letting humans decide things. Maybe the boundaries were fuzzy. The one thing you must never do is the thing you will most want to do, which is grab the topic back to rung 1 in front of everyone. I know exactly what that costs, because I paid it.

Story

Four years into the routing company I finally delegated pricing to my sales lead, a careful, sharp man who had earned it. Rung 4, though we did not have the language then: decide, tell me straight away. For two months it went fine, which means I found something else to worry about.

Then he closed a three-year deal with a fleet operator at a discount that made my eyes water. Sixty-something thousand euros lighter than my mental price. He had reasons, decent ones, reference customer, strategic segment, their procurement had squeezed hard. I did not hear the reasons, because I was not listening. I was composing.

And in the Monday meeting, in front of everyone, I announced that all pricing above a certain size would now come across my desk again "for a while." I remember feeling decisive. Leaderly, even. A correction, swift and clear.

Here is what I actually bought for my sixty thousand euros. My sales lead never made an unprompted decision again in the time he worked for me, and he left within the year, politely, for a company that let him do his job. The rest of the team, who had watched, updated their rules: autonomy here is real until the first mistake, so keep receipts and get sign-off. My support lead started forwarding me refund requests she had been handling herself for a year, "just to be safe." One public snatch-back, and delegation died in every corner of a thirty-person company, for at least a year. Nobody announced it. It just went quiet, the way trust does.

The deal, for the record, turned out fine. The customer renewed twice and referred us to two others. The only lasting damage from the whole affair was the part I did in the Monday meeting.

Warning

The snatch-back is delegation's cause of death, and it is always available in a flattering disguise: quality control, prudence, "just for now." If a delegated decision goes wrong, correct in private, adjust the rung openly and calmly if you must, and say what would need to be true to climb back. Grab the wheel in public and the lesson lands on the twenty people watching, and their lesson costs more than any single bad decision ever will. If you cannot leave a topic delegated, be honest and take it back formally, once, with an explanation. Even that is survivable. Yo-yo autonomy is not.

The handover conversation, word for word

Because "delegate more" is advice the way "eat better" is advice, here is the actual conversation, using a real example: handing customer refunds under 500 euros to your support lead. It takes fifteen minutes and has five parts, and I now refuse to consider anything delegated until all five have been said out loud.

The what and the why: "I want you to own refund decisions under 500 euros. You handle these conversations all day; you have better information than I do by the time it reaches me, and my queue is adding days of delay to customers who are already unhappy." The rung: "Rung 4. You decide, you tell me the same day in the channel, one line is enough. I am aiming for rung 5 within a quarter, where I just see them in the weekly summary." The boundaries: "Under 500 per case, under 2,000 a month total. Anything involving a legal threat or one of our ten biggest accounts still comes to me. If you are unsure whether something is inside the box, that question is always welcome and never counts against you." The definition of good: "I care that customers feel treated like adults and that we spot patterns. A refund that buys us a loyal customer is money well spent. Ten refunds for the same bug is a bug, and I want to hear about it." The failure clause, which almost everyone skips and which does most of the work: "You will make some calls I would have made differently. That is expected and priced in. If one goes properly wrong, we look at it together, in private, and we fix the boundary, not your authority. The only ways to lose this are hiding a mistake or ignoring the box."

Fifteen minutes. Compare it to what usually happens, which is "you can handle refunds now, right?" tossed over a shoulder, followed six weeks later by shock on one side and whiplash on the other. Every delegation disaster I have audited, mine and other people's, was missing at least two of the five parts, and it was usually the boundaries and the failure clause, which is to say, the two parts that require you to think before you speak.

What to hand over first

Founders always want to start by delegating the thing they hate most. Wrong order. The thing you hate is usually either genuinely yours to keep, which is why it feels heavy, or so tangled that it will fail in the hands of whoever receives it, and the failure will "prove" that delegation does not work here. Start instead where three things overlap: decisions that recur weekly or more, decisions that are reversible or financially bounded, and decisions where someone on the team already has better information than you. Refunds, scheduling, routine supplier orders, first-round interview screens, small discounts inside a written band. None of these are glamorous, and that is the point. Each one is a low-stakes repetition of the trust loop: hand over, watch the dip, hold your nerve, watch them exceed you, climb a rung. Every handover clears your queue and, more useful, drills the exact muscle you will need when the material gets expensive. Theirs too.

One more thing about delegation, because it is the part I resisted longest: the dip is mandatory. Whoever takes over will, for a while, do the thing 70 or 80 percent as well as you, slower, with mistakes you would not have made. That is the middle of delegation, the same way falling off a bike is the middle of learning to ride one. Your choices are a temporary 80 percent that grows into 105 percent, because they will exceed you at anything you let them own outright, or a permanent 100 percent that caps the company at the size of your calendar. Written down, nobody picks the second option. Live, at 17:40, watching someone draft a clumsy customer email you could fix in ninety seconds, everybody does. The whole discipline of delegation is refusing that ninety-second fix, every day, until the dip has passed.

Try this

Run a one-week bottleneck audit. Keep a page with three columns: O for decisions only you could legitimately make (spending above your comfort line, firing, signing), A for decisions where you added real value but were not required, and N for decisions that should never have reached you. Every time someone brings you a decision this week, one tally mark, no exceptions. On Friday, count. Every founder I have talked into this predicted mostly O and found mostly N. Then take the three fattest N clusters and assign each a name and a rung from the ladder, out loud, this month.

Feedback: The Currency You Have to Spend Both Ways

If delegation is the structural half of leading, feedback is the circulatory half, and founders are bad at it in both directions, for opposite reasons. We flinch from giving it because we like being liked, and we stop receiving it because of a physics problem nobody warned us about. Take them in turn. Chapter 11 covered feedback loops as company plumbing, meetings, metrics, retrospectives. This section is about you personally as a sender and receiver, which no amount of plumbing replaces.

Giving it: small, fast, and about the work

The feedback founders avoid is the small daily kind: the report that was late again, the tone in the meeting, the corner that keeps getting cut. We skip it because each single instance is too minor to justify an awkward conversation, and so we save them up, unconsciously, at compound interest. Then, months later, some 4 percent trigger releases 100 percent of the stored account, and the person on the receiving end learns two things: that they had been failing for months, and that nobody thought them adult enough to be told. Both are your fault, not theirs.

The fix is boring and works: shrink the unit. Feedback the size of a sentence, delivered within a day or two, in private, about behavior rather than character. The pattern I finally settled on has three moves and no acronym. Say what you observed, plainly: "The proposal went out with pricing errors twice this month." Say the impact, honestly: "The customer now checks our numbers, which is exactly the doubt we cannot afford." Then hand over the pen: "What is going on, and what would fix it?" That last move matters most, because half the time the answer surprises you. The proposal was late because it waited three days for your review, dear bottleneck, and the person rushed the final hour they actually got. Feedback at this size is maintenance, and like all maintenance it is cheapest on schedule and ruinous deferred.

To make the size of the unit concrete: the whole conversation should fit in the gap between two meetings. "Hey, two minutes. The demo yesterday, you talked through the customer's question twice instead of answering it, and I watched them switch off. You know this material better than anyone, so what happened?" Then you listen, and it turns out he had been told, by you, in passing, three weeks earlier, never to promise dates, and had over-corrected into answering nothing at all. Ninety seconds, one behavior, same week, and you both walked away smarter. The annual-review version of that same feedback, delivered in month eleven as "communication skills: needs development," would have helped nobody and insulted everyone.

Two habits multiply the effect. First, keep the positive-to-corrective ratio honest. If the only feedback you emit is corrective, people learn to flinch when you approach, and your praise budget is the cheapest leadership money you will ever spend, provided the praise is specific. "Good job" is noise. "You told the customer no without making them feel stupid, and I have been trying to learn that for ten years" is currency. Second, never let feedback travel through you sideways. When Anna complains to you about Tomas, walk Anna back to Tomas's door, coach her on the sentence if she needs it, and follow up that it happened. Accept the parcel even once and you become the company's courier of resentments, and the delivery route only gets longer.

Receiving it: you live inside a distortion field now

Here is the physics problem. The moment you sign someone's payslip, the truth they send you passes through a filter of self-preservation. The filter comes standard, has nothing to do with your character, and does not care that you are friendly. You decide raises. You decide who stays. However approachable you feel, and I felt very approachable, complaints about my approachability notwithstanding, people will round their opinions toward what is safe. The scary thing about the distortion field is that it is silent. Nobody tells you that nobody tells you things. The information simply stops arriving, and its absence feels exactly like everything being fine.

I learned to treat signals of dissent as endangered and therefore protected. When someone disagreed with me in a meeting, especially someone junior, especially clumsily, the only acceptable first response was some visible form of "thank you, say more," even when they were wrong, especially when they were wrong. The first punished dissenter is the last dissenter. You do not get to correct the facts until you have rewarded the courage, and if that sounds like coddling, consider what you are otherwise breeding: a company that watches you walk toward a cliff in respectful silence.

Approachability is the passive half. Go extract the truth as well, because vague invitations produce nothing. "Any feedback for me?" is a question with one safe answer and everyone knows it. What works are narrow, specific, pre-committed questions asked in one-on-ones: "What is one thing I should stop doing?" "Where did I slow you down this month?" "What do you all discuss that you would not raise with me in the room?" And then the actual test, because the questions are the cheap part: what your face does during the first answer, and what you do about it within two weeks. Feedback to a founder is a market. If honesty pays, measured in visible change and public credit, supply increases. If honesty costs, measured in your defensiveness or a chill in your tone, the market closes, quietly, and it stays closed for a long time. People will assure you the market is open. On this one topic, trust the traffic and nothing else.

Self-Awareness: Which Kind of Problem Are You?

Every founder is a problem for their company in some specific, patterned way: reliably, along the grain of their personality, under stress, on repeat. Having a failure mode is universal. The damage comes from founders who cannot name theirs, who therefore cannot see themselves doing it, and who therefore explain the wreckage some other way, usually by complaining about their team at a mentor's kitchen table.

Self-awareness sounds like a retreat-and-incense word, so let me make it mechanical: self-awareness is knowing your default move under pressure before you make it, early enough to insert one breath between the trigger and the move. That is all. The move itself may never go away. Mine has not. But a named failure mode with a pause in front of it does a tenth of the damage of an unnamed one running on automatic.

Here are the seven I have seen most often, in myself and across every founder I know. Most of us have one home mode and one travel mode for special occasions. Read the table looking for yourself, not for your cofounder, although I know exactly which row you looked at first.

Mine, for the record, are Hero home and Bottleneck away, and they travel well together: the Hero rescues, the rescue routes more decisions to the Hero, the queue grows, the Bottleneck settles in and calls it dedication. What matters is whether you know yours.

Once you have named your mode, tell your team, and give them a key to the door. Sounds like oversharing; bear with me, because it is mechanics. The failure mode fires fastest exactly when you are least able to observe it, under stress, in the room, mid-sentence, which means the people best positioned to catch it are the ones watching you do it. But "feel free to call me out" is worthless, because calling out the boss mid-move is the exact thing the distortion field forbids. What works is agreeing on a lightweight, pre-authorized signal. At the routing company, after I finally confessed my Hero mode to the leadership team, we agreed that anyone could say "cape check" when they saw me reaching for a rescue, and that my only permitted response was to stop talking and ask what they were seeing. People used it maybe once a month. It embarrassed me every time. It was also, euro for euro, the cheapest management development program I ever ran, and the first time a junior developer used it, grinning, terrified, correct, I knew the distortion field had a hole in it at last.

How do you find out, given the distortion field from the previous section? Three sources, in ascending order of discomfort. First, your history: look at your last three team-related messes and ask what they have in common, and the common element standing in all three photographs is usually you, doing your move. Second, your exes, professionally speaking: people who used to work for you have left the distortion field, and a former employee at a café table will tell you in twenty minutes, if you ask like you mean it, what current ones would not say in twenty months. Third, ask your current team, but pay the toll upfront by guessing first, out loud: "I think my failure mode is grabbing the wheel. How close am I?" Naming a candidate yourself moves the conversation from "criticize the boss," which nobody sane accepts, to "correct the boss's aim," which people enjoy.

Energy: You Are the Instrument, and You Are Playing It Drunk

Founders manage money with spreadsheets, forecasts, and a healthy paranoia we spent all of chapter 6 installing. Then they manage the machine that makes every important decision, their own nervous system, like a rental car. The tank is somebody else's problem, redlining is normal, and maintenance is for people with smaller ambitions.

Here is the argument for taking energy seriously, made in business terms rather than wellness ones, because I would have ignored the wellness ones and did for years. Your company's most expensive product is your judgment. Every significant decision, pricing, hiring, firing, the tone you take in the Monday meeting, is manufactured by a biological machine whose output quality is measurably a function of sleep, recovery, and load. Run that machine depleted and it does not feel like impairment from the inside. That is the trap. Tired judgment feels exactly like judgment. The dashboard has no warning light. The only gauge is the decisions themselves, which quietly get worse, and which you defend with full confidence, because confidence is the last thing to go.

Story

The autumn the routing company was seven years old, I ran a stretch of about four months on fumes: a big customer migration, a hiring push, a newborn at home, and my personal conviction, held since age nineteen, that rest was something I would purchase in bulk later, after the next milestone. I felt sharp the whole time. I want that on the record. I felt clear, fast, decisive, honestly a little heroic.

In those four months I made three decisions that between them cost more than any two full years of my sleep would have. I hired a senior manager against the visible hesitation of everyone who interviewed him, because the search was exhausting me and he could start Monday. Eleven months and one department's morale later, we paid him to leave. I signed a partnership agreement I had skimmed, tired, on a train, with an exclusivity clause that took fourteen months and actual lawyers to escape. And I snapped, in front of the whole team, at our best developer over a delay that turned out to be my fault, an instruction I had given in one meeting and forgotten by the next. She accepted my apology. She also, and I do not blame her, started answering recruiters.

I told Henk about the manager and the contract, framing both, I am ashamed to say, as bad luck. He listened, looked at me for a while over the coffee, and delivered the shortest performance review of my life.

"Tired men sign anything."

Then, because he could see me preparing to argue: "In the warehouse we had rules. A man on a forklift works so many hours, then he stops, not because he feels tired, because the clock says so. Feeling tired comes after the accident. You are driving a bigger forklift, boy, and you have the pallet over everyone's heads."

Notice the mechanism in Henk's rule: by the clock, not by feel. That is the entire trick, because feel is precisely the sensor that fatigue disables first. The practical program that came out of that autumn was three policies, none of which requires a retreat or a single scented candle.

First, sort your work by what it does to you, not just what it needs from you. Two identical hours on the calendar can carry very different costs. Some work charges you, and you leave with more capacity than you brought. Some work drains you at a fair price, fine, that is the job. And some work corrodes you, costing double what it produces. The mix is personal, and finding yours takes a week of honest bookkeeping, which is the exercise below. The point of knowing is placement: corrosive work goes in your strongest hours in small doses, or gets delegated up the ladder first, and big decisions never get made in the hours after it.

Second, schedule recovery like an obligation, because it is one. Sleep is the whole foundation, and every founder folklore story about four-hour nights is either a lie, a rare mutant, or a slow-motion invoice. Real holidays, offline ones, are the test of whether the machine runs without you, and we established at the top of this chapter what my test found. Exercise is the cheapest mood stabilizer on the market. None of this is news. What made it operational for me was Henk's framing: by the clock, in the calendar, defended like a customer meeting, because the customer is every decision I will make that week.

Third, install a fatigue interlock on big decisions. Mine is a rule with three clauses. No irreversible decision after a bad night. No signature the same day as the pitch, ever, however good it sounds, especially however good it sounds. And when I notice I am rushing a big call because deciding feels like relief, that feeling is now itself a red flag: wanting a decision over with is the tired brain begging to put the pallet down, and it will drop it anywhere.

Try this

Keep an energy ledger for one week. In your calendar, after every meeting or work block, type a single character: plus if it gave you energy, minus if it took a fair amount, double minus if it corroded you. Do not philosophize, just mark and move on, ten seconds each. On Friday, review the week and answer two questions with a pen. Which double-minus work could move up the delegation ladder within a month, and to whom? And which big decisions did you make within two hours of a double-minus block? That second answer tends to end the argument about whether any of this applies to you.

Field note

If your honest ledger is minus signs wall to wall, week after week, and the plus column is empty even for the parts of the business you used to love, you have moved past what this chapter can do for you. That pattern deserves a conversation with a professional, a real one, the kind with a diploma rather than a podcast, and booking it is company work of the first order. Everything in the company runs downstream of you being functional, which is the least romantic and most practical reason to take it seriously.

Getting Coached Before You Think You Need It

Somewhere in the last few pages, possibly at the mirror, possibly at the ledger, you may have had the thought every founder has at this point in the chapter: fine, but I can work on this myself. I want to spend a section explaining why that thought, which feels like self-reliance, is actually the failure modes protecting themselves. The Hero does not want a coach because heroes rescue, they are not rescued. The Smartest Person does not want a coach because what would that say. The Bottleneck has no slot in the calendar. Every mode votes against help, unanimously, and each casts its vote in the voice of prudence.

Here is the plain case. Every serious performer in every serious field has someone whose job is to watch them work and tell them the truth. Athletes, obviously. Surgeons increasingly. Singers always. The theory is identical everywhere: you cannot see yourself perform, the people around you are paid by you and therefore filtered, so improvement requires an outside eye with no stake in flattering you. Founders are the strange exception, the one performer who is expected to improve alone, in the dark, using only books and the occasional catastrophe. It is a stupid exception, and it persists because of price tags and pride, so let us handle both.

The price tag first, since it is easier. A good executive coach in Europe runs somewhere between 150 and 400 euros a session, call it three to six thousand a year at a sensible rhythm. Founders who spend that on a conference without blinking will stare at the coaching number like it is a yacht. For calibration: the mishired senior manager from my tired autumn cost north of a hundred thousand euros, all in, and a coach who had made me examine why I was rushing would have paid for two decades of herself in that one avoided signature. Coaching is expensive the way smoke detectors are expensive.

The pride is harder, and the fix is a reframe that happens to be true: coaching is not remedial. The best get more of it, not less, in every field except ours. Hiring a coach does not announce that you are broken. It announces that you have noticed the company's ceiling is made of you, and you have decided to raise it on purpose rather than by accident.

Now, the word "coach" gets used for four different kinds of help, and buying the wrong kind for your situation wastes money and sours founders on all of it. Here is the map I wish someone had handed me.

A word for the peer group row, because founders consistently underrate it. Running a company is lonely in a specific, unshareable way: your team should not carry your fears, your partner has heard enough, and your friends with salaries think a slow quarter means a smaller bonus. The first time I sat in a room of eight founders and said an ugly true number out loud, the cash number, the real one, and nobody flinched, two of them nodded, and one said "oh, that year, here is what I did," something unclenched in me that I had not known was clenched. You will get tactics from a peer group, but the tactics are the gift wrap. The gift is calibration: discovering that your secret disasters are standard equipment, and that the confident founder across the table is exactly as scared as you are, on a two-month offset.

Most founders eventually want three of the four, in some rotation. Henk, to be clear, was a mentor, not a coach, and the difference matters: he told me what he saw and occasionally what to do, from his own road. The coach I finally hired, two years after the pricing disaster and one year after the tired autumn, did something Henk never did. She said almost nothing, asked me to describe the last three times I had grabbed the wheel, and then asked, mildly, "What are you afraid happens in the thirty seconds after you don't?" I had no answer, and finding one took four sessions and did more for my delegation than every book I had read on it combined, this one now included.

Two practical notes on choosing a coach, since the market is unregulated and full of confident people with laminated methodologies. First, interview three, and ask each to coach you for twenty minutes on something real rather than describe their framework. Buy the one who made you think, not the one who made you comfortable. Second, agree upfront on one observable behavior change for the first three months, something your team could notice without being told, and at month three, ask the team if they noticed. A coach should survive the same measurement culture you apply to everything else you buy. If accountability structures for your whole leadership team are the itch, that is chapter 13's territory, and coaching pairs unreasonably well with it.

The New Job Description Nobody Gives You

If you strip this chapter to its skeleton, it says: somewhere past the crossing on that curve, your job changes from producing the work to producing the people and conditions that produce the work. I want to finish the argument by being concrete about what the new job actually consists of, because "work on the business, not in the business" is a bumper sticker, and you cannot schedule a bumper sticker into a Tuesday.

The new job has, as far as I can tell, four duties that only you can do, which is the correct test for what stays on a founder's desk. Deciding direction: the small number of genuinely irreversible, company-shaping calls, a handful a year, which deserve your best hours and your fullest tank, and which is why the energy section is in this chapter and not in a wellness pamphlet. Casting: choosing and growing the few people who lead the rest, since every leader you get right delegates a hundred future decisions in one move. Setting standards: not by reviewing everything, which is the Bottleneck's counterfeit of standards, but by what you visibly celebrate, tolerate, and refuse. Your company's real values are set by your worst tolerated behavior on your busiest week, a thought that belongs to chapter 1 but earns its keep here. And keeping the truth flowing: tending the market for honesty from the feedback section, because you are the only person with the power to make truth-telling safe, and the only one whose flinch can shut the market down.

What does a week look like once the shift has actually happened? Mine, in the good years, was roughly this, and I offer it as one sample rather than a template. About a third of the week in one-on-ones and coaching conversations, which felt like too much until I noticed it was the only work that compounded. One long block, defended with violence, for the direction work: thinking, writing, customer visits, the questions with ten-year answers. A visible, predictable rhythm of showing up in the operating meetings, which chapter 11 built, as a participant with one vote, not as the weather. And, embarrassing to admit, deliberately empty space, because a founder scheduled to 100 percent is a founder who does all their thinking in fatigue debt and answers every hallway question with their worst brain. The old me would have read that paragraph and seen a part-time job. The old me was also the reason forty-one decisions once waited nine days for a man to come back from Portugal.

Notice what the new job does to your old scoreboard. Your day used to end with evidence: code shipped, bread baked, deals signed, a satisfying pile of done. The new job produces almost no daily evidence. You coach, you unblock, you ask questions in other people's meetings and then, heroically, leave without deciding anything. For months this feels like being paid to be vague. The scoreboard that eventually replaced my pile of done was a single evening question: what can the company do without me this month that it could not do without me last month? When that answer keeps having content, you are doing the new job, whatever your visible output says. And the honest endpoint of this chapter, which I promise arrives feeling like a promotion rather than a funeral: the company's dependence on you should be trending toward zero in operations and toward everything in direction. You are trying to become unnecessary on purpose, in every room except the one where the future is decided.

The Mirror, Again

Years after the hallway, a few months after I sold the routing company, I sat at Henk's kitchen table again. He was older, slower on the stairs, coffee still black, still faintly cigar-scented, still, as far as anyone could prove, a lifelong non-smoker. I told him the story he had never heard in full: the forty-one decisions, the pricing meeting I would pay money to unhappen, the tired autumn, the coach, and the strange final years when the company mostly did not need me and was better at almost everything than it had been when it needed me constantly.

He listened the way he listened, like the story had to clear customs. Then he jerked his thumb toward the hallway.

"Go look."

I went and stood in front of the same mirror, feeling like an idiot, which I have come to understand was a load-bearing part of Henk's method. He shuffled up behind me.

"Complaints?"

I thought about it properly, because he would know if I did not. I said: he still grabs the wheel sometimes. He is still slightly too pleased when there is a crisis with his name on it. He books the difficult conversation about two weeks later than he should.

Henk nodded, satisfied, as if a machine had finally passed inspection. "Good. Same man." A pause, the length of a sip. "Better manager." He was already walking back to his coffee when he delivered the last line over his shoulder, and it is the line I would put on the cover of this chapter if covers worked that way.

"The mirror never gets better, boy. Only the looking."

That is the whole chapter, and the whole phase. You do not graduate from your failure modes, your flinches, your love of the wheel. There is no version of you, five books and two coaches from now, that stands in the hallway and files no complaints. What changes, if you work at it, is the speed of the looking: how fast you catch the move, how honestly you name it, how much company grows in the space between who you are and what you do about it. Everyone else in your company gets a manager. This chapter was about making sure you have one too, and that he is not the last person in the building to hear the truth.

The Short Version

  • Diagnose bottlenecks by queue, not by feeling. Count the decisions waiting on you; the holiday test does not lie. A company that pauses without you is worse than one that burns.
  • Every complaint about your team is a note about their manager. Read it in the mirror first.
  • The skills that built the company become the ceiling: doing, deciding instantly, being the best at the craft. The curves cross quietly, and you will notice about a year late because the old job has better dopamine.
  • Delegate decisions, not tasks, at an explicit rung on the ladder, per topic, climbing on a schedule. The 80 percent dip is mandatory and temporary. The permanent 100 percent caps the company at the size of your calendar.
  • One public snatch-back kills delegation company-wide for a year. Correct in private, adjust rungs calmly, never yo-yo.
  • Give feedback the size of a sentence, within days, about behavior. Saved-up feedback compounds into an explosion that is your fault, not theirs.
  • You live in a distortion field: honesty flows to you only if it visibly pays. Ask narrow questions, reward clumsy dissent, and judge the market by traffic, never by reassurance.
  • Know your failure mode, Hero, Seagull, Bottleneck, Ghost, Weathervane, Smartest Person, or Nice One, well enough to insert one breath before the move.
  • Your judgment is manufactured by a biological machine. Tired judgment feels exactly like judgment; manage energy by the clock, not by feel, because tired men sign anything.
  • Get coached before you think you need it. Mentor, coach, peer group, therapist solve different problems; coaching costs like a smoke detector, mishires cost like a fire.
