Chapter 13

The Entrepreneurial Operating System

A good operating rhythm will quietly save your company, and a framework can teach you one, as long as you remember that scaffolding is not the building.

The best-run management meeting I have ever attended took place in the back room of a bakery, between a rack of cooling tins and a pallet of flour, and it was chaired by a kitchen timer shaped like a chicken.

This was a Tuesday, two in the afternoon, in the original shop of my brother-in-law Jonas, the one in Deventer where the whole thing started before there were nine of them. I was there to pick up a birthday cake for my sister, which is the kind of errand you volunteer for when you want an excuse to stand in a warm room that smells like a childhood you didn't actually have. Jonas waved me toward a stool in the corner and said, "Sit, we start in two minutes, you can have coffee if you don't talk."

Around a scarred worktable sat five of his shop managers on an assortment of folding chairs and upturned crates. The other four were on a tablet propped against a bread tray, calling in from Almelo, Zutphen, wherever. On the wall, next to the oven schedules, hung a laminated A4 sheet covered in numbers. On the whiteboard, in Jonas's furious block capitals, a single word: STUCK. Below it, a numbered list.

At 14:00 exactly, Jonas wound the chicken and the meeting started. Two managers were still connecting. Nobody waited and nobody recapped for them when they arrived, which told me more about the culture than an hour of interviews would have. First, a round of the table, thirty seconds each: one good thing from your shop this week. A retirement cake with a marzipan tractor. A trainee who showed up early three days running. Then the numbers. Fifteen of them, read aloud off the laminated sheet in under five minutes, each one owned by a named person, each one answered only with "on track" or "off track." Off track got a silent tick on the whiteboard and, crucially, no discussion. Not yet. Then a check on what Jonas calls the big three, the handful of things the company has promised itself this quarter. On track, on track, off track, tick.

And then, for a full hour, they argued. Properly argued, about the three items from the STUCK list that the room had voted most urgent. Voices were raised regarding rye bread. Someone said "that is not the real problem though, is it" and the room went quiet and then went somewhere more honest. Each argument ended the same way: a name, a date, a sentence on the whiteboard. At 15:25 the chicken went off. Everyone rated the meeting out of ten, out loud, no hedging. Two people said seven, and Jonas asked each of them why, and wrote down the answers. At 15:30 the room was empty and the ovens had their people back.

I sat in the corner with a cake box on my knees and a strange feeling in my chest, because I recognized the shape of what I had just watched. I had run that exact meeting, by that exact clock, perhaps four hundred times at the routing company. "You're running EOS," I said, when Jonas came over. "That was a Level 10 meeting. There's a book. It's called Traction."

Jonas looked at me the way he looks at people who use the word "artisanal." "I'm running a bakery," he said. Then, relenting, because there was family cake involved: "A man at a franchise fair gave me the book years ago. I read it in a weekend. Good book. Terrible words." He walked me through his edits. The book says Rocks, for the quarterly priorities. "I bake bread, Wouter. I'm not calling anything a rock. We call them the big three." The book says Vision/Traction Organizer, a two-page document the whole company shares. His hangs by the flour shelf and is called the one-pager. The book wanted him to appoint an Integrator. He refused on the grounds that, and I quote, "we are not the Avengers." The meeting rating he kept exactly as written, because, he said, it was the only place his managers would tell him the truth about his own meeting.

"I kept the machine," Jonas said, handing me the cake, "and I threw away the costume."

That sentence is this entire chapter, and you could skip to the summary now if you trusted me, which you should not yet. The most jargon-allergic man I know runs the most disciplined operating rhythm I have ever seen, and those two facts are not in tension, because the discipline and the costume are separable. This chapter is about the machine: what a business operating system actually is, a tour of EOS and its two most famous cousins, why the cadence matters more than any of the vocabulary, and how to tell the difference between a company running a system and a company performing one. Because the failure mode here is specific and common, and it has a name I will use more than once: theater with better paperwork.

You Already Have an Operating System

Every company has an operating system. Yours included, right now, today. The question is not whether you have one but whether anyone can describe it.

By operating system I mean something unglamorous: the agreed way your company decides things, sets priorities, keeps score, and kills problems, week after week, whether or not anyone feels like it. Not the org chart, not the strategy, not the values on the wall. The rhythm. Who looks at which numbers, and when. Where a problem goes when someone spots one. How the company chooses what matters this quarter, and how it notices, before the quarter is cremated, that the choice was wrong.

In a company of five people around one table, the operating system is osmosis, and osmosis works fine. Everyone hears everything. Priorities are set by whoever is most alarmed, which at that size is usually correct. If nobody has written any of this down, then the true answer to "how does this company run" is: on the founder's attention and mood. I said in chapter twelve that the founder becomes the bottleneck, and this is the bottleneck's plumbing. You are the scorecard, the issues list, and the meeting cadence, all held in one increasingly tired head.

Then the company grows, and somewhere between twelve and thirty people the osmosis stops working, quietly, without sending you a notification. You will know the symptoms when I list them, because this list is why this chapter exists:

  • Decisions get relitigated, not because people are difficult, but because nobody can remember the decision being made, or where, or by whom.
  • Priorities set on Monday are dead by Thursday, killed by whichever customer or crisis shouted most recently.
  • The numbers arrive monthly, from the bookkeeper, as archaeology. Everyone is surprised, always, in both directions.
  • The same problems get discussed warmly, repeatedly, and inconclusively, like relatives at a funeral. Everyone knows about the problem. The problem has tenure.
  • Everything important waits for you, and you are the least available person in the building.

A word on borders, because this book has been strict about them. Chapter eleven was about the skeleton and the nervous system: roles, structure, information flow, and how to run meetings that are not a hostage situation. If your individual meetings are bad, go there. This chapter assumes your meetings are tolerable one at a time and asks a different question: do they add up to anything? A company can have perfectly decent meetings and still have no operating system, the way a person can own excellent individual clothes and still be badly dressed. And chapters two and three own the mission and vision themselves. I will not re-explain them here, not even a little, because I promised. This chapter cares only about the machinery that keeps those documents connected to what people actually do on a Tuesday.

So why do packaged frameworks exist, and why would a self-respecting founder buy one instead of building their own rhythm? Two honest reasons. The first is that every growing company reinvents the same five or six mechanisms, badly, in the wrong order, while distracted. A framework is somebody else's version with the bugs already found, debugged across a few thousand companies more than you will ever run. There is humility in buying it, the good kind. The second reason is subtler: a framework's real product is not the tools, it is the permission. It is much easier to tell your team "we are trying this system, it has a book, other companies use it" than to say "I have personally invented some meetings." The book takes the argument out of the room. That is worth actual money, and I say that as someone who resisted it for two full years longer than I should have.

The Six Jobs, in Plain Language

EOS, the Entrepreneurial Operating System, organizes itself around six components, and I am going to use its list even though this chapter will not insist you use its system, because the list is a genuinely good census of the jobs any operating rhythm must do. Strip the branding and the six components are six questions. Every framework you will ever meet, and every homemade system that works, answers these same six, just with different furniture.

Vision. Not writing it, you did that in chapters two and three. Circulating it. The job here is that everyone in the company can say where this is going and how, without rehearsal and without you in the room. Alignment decays like everything else, and the operating system's job is scheduled maintenance.

People. Right people, right seats, assessed honestly on a schedule rather than in the annual panic. The hiring and the letting go live in chapter ten, and your own growth as a leader in chapter twelve. The operating system just makes sure the question "is everyone in the right seat" gets asked out loud at intervals, instead of only during resignations.

Data. A small set of numbers, reviewed weekly, that catches trouble while trouble is still cheap. Chapter eleven made the case for signal over noise in your metrics. Here the emphasis is on the cadence: a good number reviewed monthly is a history lesson, the same number weekly is an early warning.

Issues. A place where problems go to be written down, named honestly, and killed, instead of circulating forever in the corridor. Most companies do not lack knowledge of their problems. They lack a scheduled venue where a problem can be executed with witnesses.

Process. The core ways of working, documented just enough that you can train from them and improve them. Not a wiki nobody reads. The twenty percent of activity that produces most of the value, written down at the altitude of a recipe rather than a legal contract.

Traction. The framework's word for the whole point: a short list of priorities per quarter, and a meeting pulse that checks them weekly. Ambition converted into dated commitments, and commitments checked at a frequency that leaves time to correct.

Here is the whole set at a glance, with the official tooling and, because I find it clarifying, the translations Jonas actually uses across nine bakeries.

Try this

Take a pen and write the six jobs down the left side of a page: vision circulation, people review, weekly numbers, issue killing, documented process, quarterly priorities. Next to each, write what in your company does that job today. Name the actual mechanism, a meeting that exists, a document with a date on it, a number someone owns. The rule: "we talk about it a lot" does not count as a mechanism, and neither do you. The blank rows are the diagnosis, and they are usually blank in the same two or three places for everyone.

EOS Without the Merchandise

Now the tour. EOS comes from Gino Wickman's book Traction, and it was built explicitly for companies like the ones in this book: founder-led, roughly ten to two hundred fifty people, past the kitchen-table stage and drowning slightly. Its genius, and I mean this as the highest compliment I can pay a business book, is that there is almost nothing original in it. Wickman assembled mechanisms that already worked, filed off their rough edges, connected them into one machine, and wrote the manual in language a tired founder can absorb on a Sunday. Original thinking is overrated in operating systems. You do not want an avant-garde heartbeat.

What follows is the machine, tool by tool, with commentary. I ran a lightly modified version of this for years at the routing company, so the commentary comes with scars attached.

The two-page vision

The V/TO compresses your entire strategic existence onto two pages: core values, core focus, a ten-year target, your marketing strategy, a three-year picture, a one-year plan, this quarter's priorities, and the long-term issues list. If you did the work in chapters one through three, you already possess every ingredient, and the V/TO is merely the storage format. That is its value. Not the thinking, the compression. A vision that fits on two pages can be read aloud to the whole company twice a year, and EOS insists that you actually do this, which is the part everyone skips and the part that matters. Wickman's phrase is "shared by all," and he means shared the way bread is shared, not the way a network drive is shared.

Seats before people

The Accountability Chart looks like an org chart and is not one. You draw the seats the business needs, define each seat by its five core responsibilities, and only then write names in, one name per seat, though one person may hold several seats in a small company. The discipline is that the structure is designed for the business you are, not accreted around the people you happen to employ, which is how most org charts actually form, like coral. Chapter eleven covered structure properly, so I will only add the one EOS idea I find genuinely load-bearing: every seat has exactly one owner, and every important function in the company appears on exactly one seat. Shared ownership is the polite Northern European way of saying no ownership.

EOS also makes much of the pairing it calls Visionary and Integrator, the founder who generates ideas and weather, and the operator who converts both into order. It is a real pattern, and if you recognized yourself in chapter twelve's description of the founder as bottleneck, the Integrator conversation is worth having. Just know that this corner of EOS comes with its own sub-industry of books and assessments, and you should wander in with your wallet in your front pocket.

Rocks, or whatever you decide to call them

Every ninety days, the leadership team picks three to seven priorities for the company, and each leader picks a few of their own. Each has one owner and a due date, and "done" is defined on the day it is set, not negotiated in week twelve. That is the whole tool. Its power is entirely in its fewness, and its fewness is entirely the point. The routing company's first attempt produced eleven company priorities in one quarter, which is a way of announcing that you have decided not to decide. If everything is a rock, you have gravel.

Ninety days, it turns out, is a magic length. Long enough that something real can get built, short enough that people can feel the deadline from day one, and short enough that a wrong bet costs you one season instead of one year. The quarter is the largest unit of time a company can actually steer. Years are for accountants and postcards.

The scorecard

Five to fifteen numbers, one row each, reviewed every single week, thirteen weeks visible at a time so trends have nowhere to hide. Every number has an owner and a goal, so the reading of the scorecard is a fast public ritual of on-track and off-track, not a seminar. The craft is in choosing numbers that lead rather than lag, numbers that twitch this week about problems that would otherwise surface in the quarterly accounts. Sales calls made, quotes sent, orders in, complaints opened, cash collected. I will say more about leading numbers when we get to 4DX, which built a whole religion on them, and more about scorecards that lie when we get to the theater section, which is where mine did.

The Level 10 meeting

The weekly pulse, ninety minutes, same day, same time, always held, never long. The agenda is fixed and timed: five minutes of good news to remind everyone the company employs humans, five for the scorecard, five to check the quarterly priorities, five for headlines from customers and staff, five to review last week's to-dos, then sixty full minutes of solving the top issues, then five to conclude, recap the to-dos, and rate the meeting out of ten. It is called a Level 10 because of that closing score, and the ambition that the meeting should earn a ten from its participants.

Notice the arithmetic, because it is the whole design: sixty of the ninety minutes are for solving. Most companies run this meeting inverted, seventy minutes of reporting things everyone already knew, then ten minutes of a real problem handled in the doorway with coats on. The Level 10 spends a third of its time confirming reality at speed, and two thirds changing it. How to conduct a meeting so people actually speak is chapter eleven's territory. What EOS adds is the ratio.

IDS, the actual engine

Issues go on a list the moment anyone spots one. Weekly, the team picks the three most important, and takes them through Identify, Discuss, Solve. Identify is the hard one and the valuable one: the issue as reported is almost never the issue, and the discipline is to keep asking what the real problem is until the room agrees it has been named. Discuss means everyone says their piece once, openly, and the meeting kills the polite recycling of positions. Solve means the issue leaves the list as either a decision, a to-do with a name and a date, or an honest admission that it belongs on the long-term list. The three outcomes all count. Circulation does not.

I have come to believe that most issues in a small company die at the Identify stage, from nothing more violent than being named precisely, in public, with the right people present. It sounds too simple to be a management technology. So does washing your hands, and it took surgeons decades.

Story

Jonas's Almelo shop kept running out of rye bread by eleven in the morning. Before the system, this problem had enjoyed a rich six-week social life: mentioned at every gathering of managers, diagnosed over coffee by everyone, owned by no one. The baking team blamed the forecast, the forecast blamed the weather, one manager had a theory involving a new gym opening nearby, because there is always a theory involving a gym.

The first Tuesday the stuck list existed, "Almelo rye, out by 11:00" got voted into the top three. Identify took most of the allotted time, and it should have. Under questioning it turned out the shop manager was ordering less rye than her own sales suggested, on purpose, because her waste number was on the numbers page with her name next to it, and unsold rye at closing time made that number worse. She was not failing. She was optimizing, with precision, for the exact target the leadership had given her. Two of Jonas's numbers were fighting each other, and the customers were the battlefield.

Solve took four minutes: waste targets adjusted per shop and per product, sold-out-by-noon added to the numbers page as its own tracked failure, one line, one owner. Jonas told me later it was the moment he stopped being embarrassed about running a bakery with a laminated scorecard. "Six weeks of opinions," he said. "Eleven minutes of looking."

So that is EOS, and here is my honest verdict after years inside a version of it. The machine is excellent. The tools are unoriginal in the best way, the manual is clear, and the whole thing is tuned for exactly the companies its critics have never run. What deserves your skepticism is the periphery: an ecosystem of certified implementers, add-on books, and licensed software that would very much like the system to feel un-self-installable. It is self-installable. Jonas installed it between a Saturday and a Monday with a highlighter and a contempt for vocabulary. And watch the scripture tendency, the team member who starts saying "that's not how EOS says to do it" in the tone of a man citing case law. The book is a tool. When people start serving the tool, skip ahead to my theater section, because you are already in it.

The Cousins: OKRs and 4DX

EOS is not the only boxed operating rhythm on the shelf, and depending on the shape of your company it may not be the best fit. Two cousins deserve a proper look, partly because they are good, and partly because one of them will be proposed to you at some point by a new hire from a bigger company, with the confidence of a missionary, and you should be able to have that conversation from knowledge rather than from allergy.

OKRs: ambition with a scoreboard

Objectives and Key Results were invented by Andy Grove at Intel in the seventies, carried to a very young Google by John Doerr in 1999, and eventually written up in Doerr's Measure What Matters. The mechanism is small enough to fit in one sentence: each quarter you set a handful of Objectives, which are qualitative and ambitious, and under each you write three to five Key Results, which are numbers, and at the end of the quarter you score yourself in the cold light of day.

The Objective is the direction: memorable, motivating, slightly out of reach. The Key Results are the evidence: measurable, dated, impossible to argue with afterwards. "Become the obvious first choice for mid-size fleets in the Benelux" is an objective. "Win 14 of the 30 open tenders, raise trial-to-paid conversion from 22 to 35 percent, get 10 reference customers on stage or on record" are key results. At quarter's end, each key result scores between zero and one, and here is the culturally radical part: on ambitious objectives, around 0.7 is considered success. OKRs are deliberately set beyond reach, and the system only works when a 0.7 is met with respect rather than a performance-improvement plan. Doerr also distinguishes committed OKRs, where 1.0 is simply expected because you promised, from aspirational ones, where 0.7 means you aimed properly.

That stretch culture is the whole trick, and also the whole risk. Done honestly, OKRs separate ambition from sandbagging: people can commit to bold targets precisely because falling short of bold is not punished. Done dishonestly, the system collapses in one of two directions. Either everyone sandbags anyway, and you get a wall of 1.0 scores measuring nothing but self-protection, or some well-meaning person couples OKR scores to bonuses, at which point every number in the building starts lying to you at once. I will come back to that with a warning label later, because it deserves one.

Where OKRs shine is in organizations whose output is measurable and whose teams need to align without a daily line of sight to each other: product and software companies, mostly, and anything over about fifty people. Where they curdle is in the cascade. Big companies fell in love with waterfalling OKRs down every layer, each objective spawning children, until middle managers spent the first three weeks of each quarter negotiating spreadsheets, a process with the approximate energy cost of the quarter itself. If you are under thirty people, my advice is blunt: company-level OKRs only, one set, visible to all. The moment individual OKRs appear in a small company, you have built a bureaucracy with extra steps and worse humor.

Notice also what OKRs do not give you. There is no meeting cadence in the box, no issues mechanism, no scorecard beyond the key results themselves, nothing about people or process. OKRs are a goal-setting layer, not a full operating system, and most OKR disappointment I have witnessed traces to companies that installed a goal vocabulary and expected it to run their week.

4DX: one goal, defended against your own business

The 4 Disciplines of Execution, from Chris McChesney, Sean Covey and Jim Huling, is the least famous of the three among software people and the most loved among people who run operations: hotels, retail chains, factories, and, if he ever admitted to reading two business books, bakeries. It begins from the best single insight in the entire genre, which the authors call the whirlwind: the day job eats goals. Not because people are lazy or the goals are wrong, but because the urgent work of keeping the shop running is a hungry, screaming, legitimately important thing, and any goal that requires new behavior must be defended against it deliberately or it dies of natural causes by February.

The four disciplines are the defense. First, focus on a Wildly Important Goal, one, at most two, phrased as "from X to Y by when." Not five goals. The book is fanatical about this and the fanaticism is earned. Second, act on lead measures. A lag measure is the result you want and cannot directly push on, monthly revenue, staff turnover, customer satisfaction. A lead measure is something your team can influence this week that predictably moves the lag: tastings booked for the wedding-cake line, callbacks made within an hour, prep checklists completed before opening. Finding a true lead measure, influenceable and predictive, is genuinely hard and genuinely valuable, and it is the second-best idea in the genre. Third, keep a compelling scoreboard, made by the team, visible to the team, simple enough that anyone can tell in five seconds whether they are winning. Fourth, create a cadence of accountability: a twenty-minute weekly session in which each person reports on last week's commitments, reviews the scoreboard, and makes one or two specific commitments for the coming week. Commitments to teammates, the book notes, get kept at rates that commitments to bosses never reach.

4DX is not a full operating system either. It says nothing about vision, structure, or issues, and it assumes the whirlwind is already reasonably well run. What it is, is the best available tool for a specific and common situation: a business that must execute one significant change while continuing to run itself at full speed. If EOS is an operating manual and OKRs are an ambition layer, 4DX is a crowbar, and there are quarters when a crowbar is exactly the tool.

There are other cousins, and I will wave at them from the road. Verne Harnish's Scaling Up, descended from his earlier Rockefeller Habits, is EOS's more corporate sibling: a one-page strategic plan, a heavier emphasis on daily huddles, and far more content on cash and finance, which makes it worth a read alongside chapter six, and again when chapter fourteen becomes your life. Holacracy is the one where the company replaces managers with a constitution, and everything I have to say about it fits in one sentence: I have never met a company that adopted it and got quietly better, and I have met two that adopted it and got loudly worse. And then there is the most common framework of all, the homemade one, which is what most healthy companies over about forty people actually run: a mongrel assembled from two books, one bad experience, and a founder who finally got tired of surprises. That is not a compromise. That is usually the destination.

Field note

If you want the sources rather than my summaries, the reading order I suggest is: Traction first, because it is the most complete and the fastest read, then The 4 Disciplines of Execution for the whirlwind and lead measures, then Measure What Matters if you are heading past fifty people or into anything venture-shaped. Read all three in a month and steal shamelessly. Between them they cost less than one hour of any consultant who will later summarize them to you with a straight face.

The Cadence Is the Product

Strip any of these systems for parts and you find the same chassis underneath: a nested set of rhythms, each one winding the next like gears in a watch. Annual, quarterly, weekly, daily. The vocabularies differ, the chassis never does, and I have slowly concluded that the chassis is the product. The tools are just each framework's way of making you keep the rhythm.

Here is the wheel, from the outside in.

Annually, the leadership team leaves the building for two days. Genuinely leaves, phones down, somewhere with bad reception and good walks. You check the vision against reality, and this is a check, not a rewrite; the writing happened in chapter three and should mostly survive contact. You look at the year's numbers without flinching, you decide the one-year plan, and you have the arguments that have been politely postponed for months. Two days feels indulgent to a founder who has not done it. It is the highest-leverage sixteen hours in the calendar.

Quarterly, a day, ideally away from the office, at minimum away from the ovens. You score the quarter that just ended, honestly, including the priorities that failed, because a failed priority examined in daylight is tuition, and a failed priority quietly renamed is theater. Then you pick the next quarter's few. The quarterly is where strategy and reality negotiate, four times a year, with witnesses, and it is the meeting I would keep if I were forced to give up all others.

Weekly, the pulse: the ninety minutes, or the WIG session, or Jonas's Tuesday, whatever your chassis calls it. Numbers first, at speed, then the stuck things, argued to names and dates. The weekly is the flywheel that makes the quarterly real, because a quarterly priority checked thirteen times cannot drift far, and a quarterly priority checked zero times was a wish with a date on it. Same day, same time, held even when it is inconvenient, especially when it is inconvenient, because the weeks when the meeting feels impossible are the weeks containing the information.

Daily, optionally, a huddle: ten minutes, standing up, no chairs because chairs are how ten minutes becomes forty. What is today, what is stuck, who needs whom. Operational businesses, shops, kitchens, support teams, get enormous value from this. A six-person software company may not, and should skip it without guilt. Of all the rings, this is the only optional one.

The compounding is the point. A year is four quarterly bets, each quarterly bet is thirteen weekly corrections, each week is five days of people knowing what matters today. Miss the weekly and the quarterly priorities die of neglect by week six. Miss the quarterly and the annual plan becomes a document you find in December, like a gym membership. The rings only work wound together.

What does the wheel actually buy you? Protection from your own nervous system, mostly. A company without a cadence is steered by recency: the loudest customer, the scariest email, the founder's most recent aeroplane idea. Chapter twelve talked about founder weather, and a cadence is climate control. When something alarming happens on a Wednesday, a company with a rhythm has a place to put the alarm, on the list, for Tuesday, where it will be judged against the other alarms instead of automatically winning by freshness. Half the value of Jonas's stuck list is that it converts panic into an agenda item. Panic does not survive being scheduled.

Story

I once sat in on one of Jonas's quarterlies, upstairs from a café in Deventer, nine managers, a paper flipchart, and enough coffee to float a barge. The morning was scoring: the previous quarter's big three read out, one done, one half-done, one dead, and the dead one got twenty full minutes of autopsy, no blame, all mechanism, until the room could say exactly why it died. It died, for the record, because it had secretly been two projects wearing one coat.

The afternoon was the part I stole. Choosing the next big three, the managers generated fourteen candidates, every one of them reasonable, which is exactly the problem with growing companies: nobody proposes stupid priorities, they propose fourteen sensible ones. Jonas handed out three sticky dots per person, votes on the flipchart, no campaigning allowed. Then he drew a line under the third-place item, and said the sentence I have since repeated to every founder who will hold still.

"Everything below this line is officially nobody's fault this quarter."

The room exhaled. You could hear it. The eleven items below the line did not disappear, they went to the long-term list, but a manager who did not fix the Zutphen storage problem this quarter would not be quietly judged for it, because the company had decided, out loud, together, that the storage problem loses to the trainee program. That is what choosing actually means, and most companies never do it. They rank things and then hold people accountable for the whole list anyway. The relief in that room was the sound of an operating system working.

Choosing One, or Building Your Own

So which one? The honest answer is that the choice matters less than the commitment, the way the choice of gym matters less than the going. But there are real differences in fit, and after watching a few dozen companies wear these systems, here is how I would decide.

Start with size. Below about ten people, do not install a full framework. I want to be emphatic about this, because frameworks are most seductive exactly when you are smallest and most overwhelmed, and that is when they cost the most relative to what they return. A six-person company that adopts full EOS spends a scandalous fraction of its week administering itself, and worse, it learns to enjoy it, because administering a company feels like running one and is much easier. Below ten, you need the minimal kit: one weekly hour with numbers and a stuck list, a quarterly half-day to score and choose, one annual day to look up from the road. That is a complete operating system for a small company. It fits on an index card and costs nothing.

From ten to fifty, the shape of your work decides. If your company is operational, output measured in loaves, tickets, shipments, projects delivered, EOS or a lightly translated version of it is the strong default, with 4DX layered on in any quarter that contains one big scary change. If your company is a product company where the hard question is not "are we executing" but "are we ambitious and aligned," OKRs start earning their keep, though I would still steal EOS's weekly mechanics underneath them, because OKRs alone will not run your Tuesday. And whatever you pick, pick one. I have watched a company run EOS and OKRs and a homegrown KPI tree simultaneously, and the only thing all three systems agreed on was that the meetings were now full.

Then there is the question nobody asks out loud: what is your culture's tolerance for vocabulary? This is a real constraint, as real as cash. Marta's design agency could never have adopted anything with the word "synergy" adjacent to it; forty designers would have laughed the system out of the building by lunch, and the laughter would have been correct, because in that culture jargon signals that thinking has stopped. Jonas solved it by translation. The routing company solved it by translation. I have come to think of translation as a compulsory step, not a stylistic one: renaming the tools in your company's own words is how the system stops being the book's and starts being yours. Rocks become the big three. The V/TO becomes the one-pager. The Level 10 becomes the Tuesday. The words are yours, and then, slowly, so is the machine.

One rule before you customize anything else, though, and I hold this one firmly: run the mechanics by the book for two full quarters before you change them. Not because the book is sacred, but because you do not yet know which parts are load-bearing. Every framework has pieces that look ornamental and are structural, and the meeting rating is my favorite example. Everyone cuts it first, it feels like kindergarten, and it is quietly the system's only feedback loop on itself, the one place the operating system gets operated on. Cut it and the meetings decay at a rate of about one minute of drift per week, and nobody says anything, because you removed the place where they would have said it. Two quarters by the book, then translate the vocabulary freely and prune the mechanics carefully, one change at a time, like a chef adjusting a recipe rather than a toddler improving a clock.

At the routing company we came to EOS the usual way, which is to say backwards, through failure. Around thirty people I read Traction on a holiday, came home glowing, and installed the entire apparatus in one heroic fortnight: accountability chart, scorecard, rocks, the vocabulary, all of it, announced at an all-hands with slides. It lasted eleven weeks. The team had correctly identified it as weather, another founder enthusiasm to be waited out, like the standing desks. The second attempt, a year later, I did everything opposite: leadership team only, no announcement, no vocabulary, just a weekly meeting with a fixed shape and a numbers page, run for two quarters before we so much as named it. That one held for years, and pieces of it are probably still running there now, under whatever the new owners call meetings. The lesson cost me a year: an operating system is not installed, it is grown, and the founder's enthusiasm is the least reliable soil there is.

Theater with Better Paperwork

Now the failure mode, the one from the chapter's subtitle in the table of contents, and I want to describe it carefully because it does not look like failure. It looks like compliance. It looks, from the founder's chair, like success.

The meetings happen, on time, rated nine. The scorecard is filled in, and green. The rocks are set each quarter and reviewed each week. The V/TO is laminated. Every ritual of the operating system is performed, and the company underneath it is exactly as confused, congested and founder-dependent as it was before, sometimes more so, because now the confusion has a schedule. This is theater with better paperwork, and it is astonishingly common, and the reason it is common is that it is comfortable. The rituals of an operating system can be performed without any of the conflict the system exists to force, the way you can go through every motion of a workout without ever picking up anything heavy.

You learn to spot it by the smell. The scorecard drifts toward numbers that are easy to collect and pleasant to read, activity instead of truth: demos given rather than deals closed, tickets resolved rather than customers kept. Ops people call these watermelon metrics, green on the outside, red inside, and every scorecard trends toward watermelon unless someone actively resists. The issues list stops being a killing floor and becomes a parking lot, the same issue appearing week after week in slightly different words, because naming it precisely would mean naming a person, or a pricing mistake, or the founder. Rocks are set at seven per person, hover at ninety percent done for twelve weeks, and are quietly rephrased into next quarter, unautopsied. The meeting ratings are all nines, which sounds like health and is actually a politeness index; a team that never rates a meeting below eight has stopped using the rating to say anything. And the vocabulary, this one is subtle, becomes a moat: leadership speaks fluent framework, everyone else needs subtitles, and the operating system becomes a private language in which the people with the actual information cannot raise an issue without feeling like tourists.

An operating system is a machine for scheduling uncomfortable conversations. Remove the discomfort and all you have left is the schedule.

That is the diagnosis underneath every symptom on the list. Vision alignment, scorecards, issue lists, quarterly autopsies, every tool in every framework is a way of forcing a specific uncomfortable conversation to happen on a calendar instead of at a crisis. Which numbers are actually bad. Which priority actually failed, and by whose hand. Which seat has the wrong person in it. Companies do not adopt frameworks because they lack calendars, they adopt them, whether they know it or not, to schedule conflict they have been avoiding. And a company that adopts a framework in order to keep avoiding it, running the rituals while omitting the discomfort, has bought a gym membership to carry around. The paperwork improves. Nothing else does.

Warning

The fastest way to turn a working system into theater is to bolt money to it. The week you tie bonuses to the scorecard, or salary reviews to OKR scores, is the week every number in the building acquires a motive to flatter you. Economists call this Goodhart's law: when a measure becomes a target, it stops being a measure. Pay people well, judge performance broadly, and let the operating system's numbers stay what they must be to be useful, which is safe to be ugly.

Story

Year two of the routing company's operating rhythm, the good version, the one that held. Our scorecard had grown, a number here, a number there, each addition reasonable at the time, until it had twenty-three rows, and one autumn I realized it had been entirely green for six consecutive weeks. During those same six weeks, two of my best engineers resigned and our cash position dipped enough that I made the mistake of doing runway arithmetic during a wedding, not my own, mercifully. Twenty-three green numbers, and the company was having its worst quarter in three years.

I printed the scorecard and went through it with a pen, and the pattern was so obvious it was embarrassing. Demos given. Tickets closed. Uptime. Releases shipped. Support response time. Every row measured activity, effort, motion, things a hardworking team will keep green through sheer professionalism right up until the day the company dies. Not one row would have twitched when a strategic customer went quiet, when offers stopped converting, when the engineers started interviewing. We had built a dashboard for the engine and nothing for the road.

We cut it to nine numbers, and the rule I imposed then is the one I now give away: every scorecard needs at least one number you would rather not show your mother. Offer-to-close rate, ugly. Regrettable departures, trailing twelve months, uglier. Weeks of runway at current burn, chapter six's favorite, never once flattering. The meeting got tenser and the company got healthier, in that order, within a quarter, and I stopped confusing a green dashboard with a good week.

If you recognized your company in the right-hand column, resist the reflex conclusion, which is that the framework failed and a different one is needed. Founders in theater switch frameworks the way insomniacs switch pillows. The framework did not fail, it was defanged, and the next one will be defanged too, by the same hand, probably yours, because the discomfort the system keeps trying to schedule is usually a conversation the founder does not want to have. Chapter twelve has more to say about that mirror. The repair is not a new system. The repair is picking the single most theatrical instrument, usually the scorecard, and putting one true, ugly number back on it, and then defending that number's right to be ugly in front of everyone. Systems come back to life the same way they die: one honest row at a time.
