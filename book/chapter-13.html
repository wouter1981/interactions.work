Chapter 13

The Entrepreneurial Operating System

A good operating rhythm will quietly save your company, and a framework can teach you one, as long as you remember that scaffolding is not the building.

The best-run management meeting I have ever attended took place in the back room of a bakery, between a rack of cooling tins and a pallet of flour, and it was chaired by a kitchen timer shaped like a chicken.

This was a Tuesday, two in the afternoon, in the original shop of my brother-in-law Jonas, the one in Deventer where the whole thing started before there were nine of them. I was there to pick up a birthday cake for my sister, which is the kind of errand you volunteer for when you want an excuse to stand in a warm room that smells like a childhood you didn't actually have. Jonas waved me toward a stool in the corner and said, "Sit, we start in two minutes, you can have coffee if you don't talk."

Around a scarred worktable sat five of his shop managers on an assortment of folding chairs and upturned crates. The other four were on a tablet propped against a bread tray, calling in from Almelo, Zutphen, wherever. On the wall, next to the oven schedules, hung a laminated A4 sheet covered in numbers. On the whiteboard, in Jonas's furious block capitals, a single word: STUCK. Below it, a numbered list.

At 14:00 exactly, Jonas wound the chicken and the meeting started. Two managers were still connecting. Nobody waited and nobody recapped for them when they arrived, which told me more about the culture than an hour of interviews would have. First, a round of the table, thirty seconds each: one good thing from your shop this week. A retirement cake with a marzipan tractor. A trainee who showed up early three days running. Then the numbers. Fifteen of them, read aloud off the laminated sheet in under five minutes, each one owned by a named person, each one answered only with "on track" or "off track." Off track got a silent tick on the whiteboard and, crucially, no discussion. Not yet. Then a check on what Jonas calls the big three, the handful of things the company has promised itself this quarter. On track, on track, off track, tick.

And then, for a full hour, they argued. Properly argued, about the three items from the STUCK list that the room had voted most urgent. Voices were raised regarding rye bread. Someone said "that is not the real problem though, is it" and the room went quiet and then went somewhere more honest. Each argument ended the same way: a name, a date, a sentence on the whiteboard. At 15:25 the chicken went off. Everyone rated the meeting out of ten, out loud, no hedging. Two people said seven, and Jonas asked each of them why, and wrote down the answers. At 15:30 the room was empty and the ovens had their people back.

I sat in the corner with a cake box on my knees and a strange feeling in my chest, because I recognized the shape of what I had just watched. I had run that exact meeting, by that exact clock, perhaps four hundred times at the routing company. "You're running EOS," I said, when Jonas came over. "That was a Level 10 meeting. There's a book. It's called Traction."

Jonas looked at me the way he looks at people who use the word "artisanal." "I'm running a bakery," he said. Then, relenting, because there was family cake involved: "A man at a franchise fair gave me the book years ago. I read it in a weekend. Good book. Terrible words." He walked me through his edits. The book says Rocks, for the quarterly priorities. "I bake bread, Wouter. I'm not calling anything a rock. We call them the big three." The book says Vision/Traction Organizer, a two-page document the whole company shares. His hangs by the flour shelf and is called the one-pager. The book wanted him to appoint an Integrator. He refused on the grounds that, and I quote, "we are not the Avengers." The meeting rating he kept exactly as written, because, he said, it was the only place his managers would tell him the truth about his own meeting.

"I kept the machine," Jonas said, handing me the cake, "and I threw away the costume."

That sentence is this entire chapter, and you could skip to the summary now if you trusted me, which you should not yet. The most jargon-allergic man I know runs the most disciplined operating rhythm I have ever seen, and those two facts are not in tension, because the discipline and the costume are separable. This chapter is about the machine: what a business operating system actually is, a tour of EOS and its two most famous cousins, why the cadence matters more than any of the vocabulary, and how to tell the difference between a company running a system and a company performing one. Because the failure mode here is specific and common, and it has a name I will use more than once: theater with better paperwork.

You Already Have an Operating System

Every company has an operating system. Yours included, right now, today. The question is not whether you have one but whether anyone can describe it.

By operating system I mean something unglamorous: the agreed way your company decides things, sets priorities, keeps score, and kills problems, week after week, whether or not anyone feels like it. Not the org chart, not the strategy, not the values on the wall. The rhythm. Who looks at which numbers, and when. Where a problem goes when someone spots one. How the company chooses what matters this quarter, and how it notices, before the quarter is cremated, that the choice was wrong.

In a company of five people around one table, the operating system is osmosis, and osmosis works fine. Everyone hears everything. Priorities are set by whoever is most alarmed, which at that size is usually correct. If nobody has written any of this down, then the true answer to "how does this company run" is: on the founder's attention and mood. I said in chapter twelve that the founder becomes the bottleneck, and this is the bottleneck's plumbing. You are the scorecard, the issues list, and the meeting cadence, all held in one increasingly tired head.

Then the company grows, and somewhere between twelve and thirty people the osmosis stops working, quietly, without sending you a notification. You will know the symptoms when I list them, because this list is why this chapter exists:

  • Decisions get relitigated, not because people are difficult, but because nobody can remember the decision being made, or where, or by whom.
  • Priorities set on Monday are dead by Thursday, killed by whichever customer or crisis shouted most recently.
  • The numbers arrive monthly, from the bookkeeper, as archaeology. Everyone is surprised, always, in both directions.
  • The same problems get discussed warmly, repeatedly, and inconclusively, like relatives at a funeral. Everyone knows about the problem. The problem has tenure.
  • Everything important waits for you, and you are the least available person in the building.

A word on borders, because this book has been strict about them. Chapter eleven was about the skeleton and the nervous system: roles, structure, information flow, and how to run meetings that are not a hostage situation. If your individual meetings are bad, go there. This chapter assumes your meetings are tolerable one at a time and asks a different question: do they add up to anything? A company can have perfectly decent meetings and still have no operating system, the way a person can own excellent individual clothes and still be badly dressed. And chapters two and three own the mission and vision themselves. I will not re-explain them here, not even a little, because I promised. This chapter cares only about the machinery that keeps those documents connected to what people actually do on a Tuesday.

So why do packaged frameworks exist, and why would a self-respecting founder buy one instead of building their own rhythm? Two honest reasons. The first is that every growing company reinvents the same five or six mechanisms, badly, in the wrong order, while distracted. A framework is somebody else's version with the bugs already found, debugged across a few thousand companies more than you will ever run. There is humility in buying it, the good kind. The second reason is subtler: a framework's real product is not the tools, it is the permission. It is much easier to tell your team "we are trying this system, it has a book, other companies use it" than to say "I have personally invented some meetings." The book takes the argument out of the room. That is worth actual money, and I say that as someone who resisted it for two full years longer than I should have.

The Six Jobs, in Plain Language

EOS, the Entrepreneurial Operating System, organizes itself around six components, and I am going to use its list even though this chapter will not insist you use its system, because the list is a genuinely good census of the jobs any operating rhythm must do. Strip the branding and the six components are six questions. Every framework you will ever meet, and every homemade system that works, answers these same six, just with different furniture.

Vision. Not writing it, you did that in chapters two and three. Circulating it. The job here is that everyone in the company can say where this is going and how, without rehearsal and without you in the room. Alignment decays like everything else, and the operating system's job is scheduled maintenance.

People. Right people, right seats, assessed honestly on a schedule rather than in the annual panic. The hiring and the letting go live in chapter ten, and your own growth as a leader in chapter twelve. The operating system just makes sure the question "is everyone in the right seat" gets asked out loud at intervals, instead of only during resignations.

Data. A small set of numbers, reviewed weekly, that catches trouble while trouble is still cheap. Chapter eleven made the case for signal over noise in your metrics. Here the emphasis is on the cadence: a good number reviewed monthly is a history lesson, the same number weekly is an early warning.

Issues. A place where problems go to be written down, named honestly, and killed, instead of circulating forever in the corridor. Most companies do not lack knowledge of their problems. They lack a scheduled venue where a problem can be executed with witnesses.

Process. The core ways of working, documented just enough that you can train from them and improve them. Not a wiki nobody reads. The twenty percent of activity that produces most of the value, written down at the altitude of a recipe rather than a legal contract.

Traction. The framework's word for the whole point: a short list of priorities per quarter, and a meeting pulse that checks them weekly. Ambition converted into dated commitments, and commitments checked at a frequency that leaves time to correct.

Here is the whole set at a glance, with the official tooling and, because I find it clarifying, the translations Jonas actually uses across nine bakeries.

If you take nothing else from this chapter, take the list. You can reject every framework in the coming pages and still owe your company an honest answer, per row, to the question: what, in our shop, currently does this job? For a lot of founder-led companies the honest answer, four rows out of six, is "the founder, when he remembers." That is not an insult. It is a to-do list.

Try this

Take a pen and write the six jobs down the left side of a page: vision circulation, people review, weekly numbers, issue killing, documented process, quarterly priorities. Next to each, write what in your company does that job today. Name the actual mechanism, a meeting that exists, a document with a date on it, a number someone owns. The rule: "we talk about it a lot" does not count as a mechanism, and neither do you. The blank rows are the diagnosis, and they are usually blank in the same two or three places for everyone.

EOS Without the Merchandise

Now the tour. EOS comes from Gino Wickman's book Traction, and it was built explicitly for companies like the ones in this book: founder-led, roughly ten to two hundred fifty people, past the kitchen-table stage and drowning slightly. Its genius, and I mean this as the highest compliment I can pay a business book, is that there is almost nothing original in it. Wickman assembled mechanisms that already worked, filed off their rough edges, connected them into one machine, and wrote the manual in language a tired founder can absorb on a Sunday. Original thinking is overrated in operating systems. You do not want an avant-garde heartbeat.

What follows is the machine, tool by tool, with commentary. I ran a lightly modified version of this for years at the routing company, so the commentary comes with scars attached.

The two-page vision

The V/TO compresses your entire strategic existence onto two pages: core values, core focus, a ten-year target, your marketing strategy, a three-year picture, a one-year plan, this quarter's priorities, and the long-term issues list. If you did the work in chapters one through three, you already possess every ingredient, and the V/TO is merely the storage format. That is its value. Not the thinking, the compression. A vision that fits on two pages can be read aloud to the whole company twice a year, and EOS insists that you actually do this, which is the part everyone skips and the part that matters. Wickman's phrase is "shared by all," and he means shared the way bread is shared, not the way a network drive is shared.

Seats before people

The Accountability Chart looks like an org chart and is not one. You draw the seats the business needs, define each seat by its five core responsibilities, and only then write names in, one name per seat, though one person may hold several seats in a small company. The discipline is that the structure is designed for the business you are, not accreted around the people you happen to employ, which is how most org charts actually form, like coral. Chapter eleven covered structure properly, so I will only add the one EOS idea I find genuinely load-bearing: every seat has exactly one owner, and every important function in the company appears on exactly one seat. Shared ownership is the polite Northern European way of saying no ownership.

EOS also makes much of the pairing it calls Visionary and Integrator, the founder who generates ideas and weather, and the operator who converts both into order. It is a real pattern, and if you recognized yourself in chapter twelve's description of the founder as bottleneck, the Integrator conversation is worth having. Just know that this corner of EOS comes with its own sub-industry of books and assessments, and you should wander in with your wallet in your front pocket.

Rocks, or whatever you decide to call them

Every ninety days, the leadership team picks three to seven priorities for the company, and each leader picks a few of their own. Each has one owner and a due date, and "done" is defined on the day it is set, not negotiated in week twelve. That is the whole tool. Its power is entirely in its fewness, and its fewness is entirely the point. The routing company's first attempt produced eleven company priorities in one quarter, which is a way of announcing that you have decided not to decide. If everything is a rock, you have gravel.

Ninety days, it turns out, is a magic length. Long enough that something real can get built, short enough that people can feel the deadline from day one, and short enough that a wrong bet costs you one season instead of one year. The quarter is the largest unit of time a company can actually steer. Years are for accountants and postcards.

The scorecard

Five to fifteen numbers, one row each, reviewed every single week, thirteen weeks visible at a time so trends have nowhere to hide. Every number has an owner and a goal, so the reading of the scorecard is a fast public ritual of on-track and off-track, not a seminar. The craft is in choosing numbers that lead rather than lag, numbers that twitch this week about problems that would otherwise surface in the quarterly accounts. Sales calls made, quotes sent, orders in, complaints opened, cash collected. I will say more about leading numbers when we get to 4DX, which built a whole religion on them, and more about scorecards that lie when we get to the theater section, which is where mine did.

The Level 10 meeting

The weekly pulse, ninety minutes, same day, same time, always held, never long. The agenda is fixed and timed: five minutes of good news to remind everyone the company employs humans, five for the scorecard, five to check the quarterly priorities, five for headlines from customers and staff, five to review last week's to-dos, then sixty full minutes of solving the top issues, then five to conclude, recap the to-dos, and rate the meeting out of ten. It is called a Level 10 because of that closing score, and the ambition that the meeting should earn a ten from its participants.

Notice the arithmetic, because it is the whole design: sixty of the ninety minutes are for solving. Most companies run this meeting inverted, seventy minutes of reporting things everyone already knew, then ten minutes of a real problem handled in the doorway with coats on. The Level 10 spends a third of its time confirming reality at speed, and two thirds changing it. How to conduct a meeting so people actually speak is chapter eleven's territory. What EOS adds is the ratio.

IDS, the actual engine

Issues go on a list the moment anyone spots one. Weekly, the team picks the three most important, and takes them through Identify, Discuss, Solve. Identify is the hard one and the valuable one: the issue as reported is almost never the issue, and the discipline is to keep asking what the real problem is until the room agrees it has been named. Discuss means everyone says their piece once, openly, and the meeting kills the polite recycling of positions. Solve means the issue leaves the list as either a decision, a to-do with a name and a date, or an honest admission that it belongs on the long-term list. The three outcomes all count. Circulation does not.

I have come to believe that most issues in a small company die at the Identify stage, from nothing more violent than being named precisely, in public, with the right people present. It sounds too simple to be a management technology. So does washing your hands, and it took surgeons decades.

Story

Jonas's Almelo shop kept running out of rye bread by eleven in the morning. Before the system, this problem had enjoyed a rich six-week social life: mentioned at every gathering of managers, diagnosed over coffee by everyone, owned by no one. The baking team blamed the forecast, the forecast blamed the weather, one manager had a theory involving a new gym opening nearby, because there is always a theory involving a gym.

The first Tuesday the stuck list existed, "Almelo rye, out by 11:00" got voted into the top three. Identify took most of the allotted time, and it should have. Under questioning it turned out the shop manager was ordering less rye than her own sales suggested, on purpose, because her waste number was on the numbers page with her name next to it, and unsold rye at closing time made that number worse. She was not failing. She was optimizing, with precision, for the exact target the leadership had given her. Two of Jonas's numbers were fighting each other, and the customers were the battlefield.

Solve took four minutes: waste targets adjusted per shop and per product, sold-out-by-noon added to the numbers page as its own tracked failure, one line, one owner. Jonas told me later it was the moment he stopped being embarrassed about running a bakery with a laminated scorecard. "Six weeks of opinions," he said. "Eleven minutes of looking."

So that is EOS, and here is my honest verdict after years inside a version of it. The machine is excellent. The tools are unoriginal in the best way, the manual is clear, and the whole thing is tuned for exactly the companies its critics have never run. What deserves your skepticism is the periphery: an ecosystem of certified implementers, add-on books, and licensed software that would very much like the system to feel un-self-installable. It is self-installable. Jonas installed it between a Saturday and a Monday with a highlighter and a contempt for vocabulary. And watch the scripture tendency, the team member who starts saying "that's not how EOS says to do it" in the tone of a man citing case law. The book is a tool. When people start serving the tool, skip ahead to my theater section, because you are already in it.
