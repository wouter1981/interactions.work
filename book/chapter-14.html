Chapter 14

Scaling Up

Scaling multiplies whatever you already are, which is wonderful news or terrible news, and most founders find out which one after signing the lease.

My brother-in-law Jonas opened his ninth bakery on a Tuesday in March, and by the following January it had very nearly taken the other eight down with it.

You have met Jonas before, in the money chapter and again in the chapter about operating systems, where he runs his weekly leadership meeting at a flour-dusted table with an agenda taped to the wall. By the time of the ninth bakery he was, by any reasonable standard, good at this. Eight shops, all profitable. Head bakers he trusted. A rhythm of numbers and meetings that meant he knew by Thursday lunch how every location's week was going. He had turned one bakery into eight over eleven years, and every one of those openings had gone roughly the way he planned, which is to say over budget by a manageable amount and stressful in ways he recognized.

The ninth was different, and the ways it was different make up a fairly complete catalogue of the mistakes in this chapter, which is why I asked his permission to hang the whole thing on it.

The ninth bakery was in the big city, forty minutes from home instead of fifteen. It was on a corner, in a listed building with tall windows, twice the floor space of anything he had. It was going to be his first shop with a full café: thirty seats, table service, lunch. The rent was 4,800 euros a month against a portfolio average of about 2,100. The previous tenant, a restaurant, had gone broke, which is why Jonas got the lease on friendly terms, and which he chose to read as luck rather than as a message.

He financed the fit-out from the operating cash of the other eight shops. No loan. "We are profitable," he told me at a birthday party, holding a beer, sounding like a man reading from a brochure about himself. "Why would I pay a bank interest to lend me my own money?" The fit-out was budgeted at 180,000 euros. A listed building, it turned out, does not let you just punch an oven flue through a seventeenth-century wall. Permits, a heritage consultant, a custom extraction system, twelve weeks of delay. The fit-out landed at 271,000 euros, and the opening moved from March to June, then to September, then it was March again, a full year late, and eleven newly hired staff had spent months being paid to deep-clean an unopened shop.

Meanwhile, quietly, the other eight bakeries were being eaten. Maintenance got deferred. The flour supplier's payment terms got stretched, then stretched again. And because the ninth shop kept stumbling, Jonas did the thing that felt like leadership: he sent his best people to fix it. His two strongest head bakers went to the city. Within a quarter, the quality in three of his older shops dipped in that way customers never mention and never forgive, and the reviews sagged, and revenue across the old shops went down four percent while their best people stood in a beautiful unopened café forty minutes away, arguing with a heritage consultant about a flue.

The worst week came in January. Payroll for nine locations, the stretched flour invoices finally due, the fit-out's last instalment, and a VAT payment, all in the same ten days. Jonas sat at his kitchen table at two in the morning with a spreadsheet, moved his personal savings into the company, and made a phone call he had sworn after the events of chapter six he would never make again: he called his flour supplier and asked for time. Eight profitable bakeries. Eleven years of doing everything right. One growth step, and he was back to begging at two in the morning.

The ninth bakery survived, and so did the other eight, and we will get to how, because the rescue teaches as much as the wreck. But hold on to the shape of the thing: a working business, a proven owner, an expansion that looked from every angle like more of the same, and eighteen months of it being nearly the end. That is what scaling is like when it goes only slightly wrong. This chapter is about making it go slightly wrong on purpose, with your eyes open and your hands on the controls, instead of very wrong by surprise.

Growing Is Not Scaling

People use these words interchangeably, and the confusion is not harmless, so let me split them the way I wish someone had split them for me.

Growing is getting more output by adding proportionally more input. One more baker, one more oven, so much more bread. Ten more customers, one more support person. The machine stays the same shape and you turn the handle faster. Growth is honest work and most healthy businesses do it most of the time.

Scaling is changing the machine so that output grows faster than input. A production kitchen that bakes for five shops. A sales team that closes deals the founder never touches. A second country. Onboarding that turns a stranger into a competent colleague in three weeks instead of six months of shadowing whoever has time. Scaling means the business gets structurally bigger gears, and the whole point of bigger gears is that they do not spin up gently.

The reason the distinction matters is that the two things break differently. When growth goes wrong, you have too much or too little of something, and you adjust. Hire one more, buy one more, wait a quarter. When scaling goes wrong, the machine itself is wrong, and no amount of handle-turning fixes it. Jonas's ninth bakery was not one more bakery. It was a different business wearing his logo: a café with table service, a city clientele, a manager-run location. He priced it, staffed it, and financed it like bakery number nine, when it was actually company number two.

There is a second reason to keep the words apart. Scaling has a property that growth does not, and it is the most important sentence in this chapter, so it gets the fancy formatting.

Scaling multiplies whatever you already are. If there is a crack in the model, scale is how you turn it into a canyon.

A mediocre product at one location is a mediocre product with rent of 2,100 euros. At nine locations it is a mediocre product with rent of twenty thousand. A sales process that works because you personally are charming does not scale into a charming sales team; it scales into a confused one, because charm was never written down. A culture held together by everyone sitting within earshot of you scales into no culture at all. Whatever is quietly wrong in your business right now is the thing scale will make loud. This is why the honest first move of any scaling decision is not a growth plan. It is an inspection.

One more piece of vocabulary and then I will stop. In this chapter, a step is one deliberate act of scaling: a new location, a new market, a sales team, a production facility. Steps are lumpy. You cannot open forty percent of a bakery or hire sixty percent of a German subsidiary. The lumpiness is the whole problem, and the whole opportunity, and it is why the picture of healthy scaling looks nothing like the chart in your pitch deck. We will draw the real picture shortly.

When to Scale, and When You Only Want To

Founders decide to scale for two kinds of reasons: because the business is pulling them, or because something inside them is pushing. Only one of these is a good reason, and it is worth being embarrassingly honest about which one is operating on you.

The pull signals

The business pulls when demand is arriving that you cannot serve. You are turning customers away, or making them wait so long that turning them away would be kinder. Your shop has a queue out the door on Saturdays and the queue is not a novelty, it has been there for a year. Your software has a waitlist you did not build as a marketing trick. Customers are asking, unprompted, whether you also operate in Antwerp, whether you can handle a fleet of four hundred vans, whether they can buy the bigger version. Your unit economics are boringly repeatable: the last shop, the last cohort, the last segment behaved like the ones before it, and the margins held without you personally standing in the room.

Pull evidence is external, specific, and slightly annoying. It shows up as work you had to decline. If you cannot point at concrete demand you refused in the last quarter, be suspicious of your own enthusiasm.

The push signals

The push comes from inside, and it wears convincing costumes. Boredom, dressed as ambition: the current business runs well, running things well is dull, and a new location or a new country is a legitimate-sounding way to make life exciting again. Envy, dressed as strategy: a competitor opened in Rotterdam, so apparently the race is on. Ego, dressed as vision: nine sounds so much better than eight when someone at a birthday party asks how many you have. And sometimes it is a spreadsheet, dressed as inevitability: a projection, yours or an investor's, in which the growth is already assumed and your job is merely to go execute the assumption.

None of these feelings are shameful. I have scaled out of boredom, and I got the outcome boredom deserves. The problem is not having the push. The problem is letting the push write cheques that only pull can cash. Scaling driven by push has a signature: the plan works only if everything goes right, and the person proposing it gets irritated when you ask what happens if it does not.

The readiness inspection

So here is the inspection I promised. Seven questions, each answerable with evidence rather than mood. Jonas and I filled this in together, retroactively, a year after the ninth bakery's worst January. He scored two out of seven, laughed the laugh of a man who has stopped being defensive about it, and said, and I am quoting him exactly because it deserves it: "I would not have let me open a market stall."

Look at question seven and then look back at the ninth bakery. New city, new format, new size, first café, first manager-run opening, first fit-out in a listed building. Jonas did not take a step. He took five steps at once, wearing a blindfold made of eight previous successes. The previous successes were real. They were also all the same step, repeated, which is the most dangerous kind of experience there is: it feels like general competence and it is actually one well-rehearsed move.

A word on the other direction, because it exists. Sometimes not scaling is the risk. If your business has real network effects, if a market window is genuinely closing, if regulation or a platform shift is about to reward whoever is biggest, then sitting still can be the gamble. These situations are real and they are rarer than the people selling urgency want you to believe. In fifteen years I have seen perhaps three businesses genuinely punished for scaling too late, and I have lost count of the ones punished for scaling too early. The asymmetry is your friend: the too-late founders lost some upside. Several of the too-early ones lost the company.

Try this

Print the readiness checklist. Score your business, alone, honestly, in pen. Then give a blank copy to the most sceptical sensible person who knows your company, a cofounder, your bookkeeper, your longest-serving employee, and have them score it without seeing yours. Compare. The questions where you said yes and they said no are not disagreements. They are the exact places scale will hurt you, discovered for the price of a coffee.

The Stair-Step, Not the Hockey Stick

Every pitch deck contains the same chart. Revenue along the left, time along the bottom, and a curve that hugs the floor politely for two years and then leaves the top right corner of the slide like it heard good news. The hockey stick. I have drawn it myself, with a straight face, more than once.

Here is what I have never seen: a real business, up close, that grew like that chart. I have seen businesses whose long-run summary, viewed from far enough away, smooths into something curve-shaped. But zoom in on any of them, mine included, and the curve dissolves into stairs. A step up, then a long flat. Another step, another flat. Sometimes a step down, which the summary chart forgets the way memory forgets, as discussed in the introduction.

The stairs are not a failure to achieve the curve. The stairs are what healthy scaling actually looks like, and each part of the stair has a job.

The riser is the step itself: you open the location, enter the market, hire the sales team, build the production kitchen. During the riser, everything gets deliberately worse before it gets better. Costs jump immediately, revenue follows late. Margins dip. The error rate rises, because new people and new process always produce errors. Your own calendar becomes a casualty. A riser is controlled overload, and the only honest way to enter one is knowing it will feel like a mild emergency for months.

The tread is the flat, and this is the part ambitious founders get wrong, because the flat looks like stagnation and is actually digestion. On the tread you stabilize the new thing: the location finds its regulars, the sales hires reach quota, the errors get engineered out. Margin climbs back to where it belongs. Cash refills. Your bench recovers, and the people stretched by the last step grow into their new size. The tread is where the business becomes, again, the kind of business that passes the readiness checklist, which is what qualifies it to take the next riser.

Notice the little dip after each riser in the drawing. That is not decoration. Nearly every step down in scale, a new site, a new team, a new market, performs slightly worse than the thing it copied, at first, because the copy is missing ten years of invisible corrections. The dip is normal. The dip becoming the new normal is not, and the difference between those two is whether anyone is watching the quality numbers from question six of the checklist.

Three practical rules fall out of the stair shape.

One step at a time. A riser consumes management attention, cash buffer, and error tolerance, and you have a fixed supply of all three. New market or new product or new sales motion or new format. Pick one, finish digesting it, then pick the next. Founders resist this because sequential feels slow. It is slow the way not crashing is slow.

Size the step to what you can survive, not to what you can imagine. The question is never "how big could this be?" It is "if this step performs at half the plan for twice the time, do the existing stairs still hold?" Jonas's ninth bakery fails this test instantly: its overrun was funded by the working capital of the other eight, so a stumble in the new thing became a haemorrhage in the old thing.

Respect the tread. After a step, the boring quarters where you fix onboarding and rebuild the cash buffer are not a pause in the strategy. They are the strategy. Most self-inflicted scaling disasters I know are really tread-skipping: a founder who took step two while still bleeding from step one, usually because a competitor did something, or a landlord called with a beautiful corner unit, or the flat part simply got dull. If you want a number, the founders I know who scaled well spent one to two years per stair, and the ones who spent less usually gave the time back later, with interest, in the currency of crisis.

What Breaks, and in What Order

The introduction to this book spoiled the answer: when you scale, what breaks is people, then process, then cash, then you, roughly in that order. Having now watched the sequence from inside twice and from the next barstool a dozen times, I will stand by the order, with one correction: cash breaks third but it is usually the first thing you notice, because cash is the only one of the four with the courtesy to send a bank statement.

Size changes what a company is. Not gradually, either. There are thresholds, and while every business crosses them at slightly different numbers, the pattern is so consistent that you can nearly navigate by it. People break first

Not because people are weak, but because a scaling step changes every job in the company without asking anyone's permission, and it changes some jobs into jobs their current holders never applied for.

The classic case deserves naming because you will meet it: the loyal early person who ran everything at twelve people and is drowning at forty. She was your third hire. She did five jobs at once, brilliantly, when five jobs fit into one heroic human. Now each of those jobs is a department, and the skill that made her great, personally gripping every detail, is precisely the skill that no longer works. Meanwhile she has watched you hire polished strangers above the people who built the place, and she is wondering, quietly, whether loyalty was a mistake.

There are only three honest moves, and all of them start with a conversation most founders delay a year too long. You can grow the person: real support, a coach, an explicit new role description and time, which works more often than cynics claim but only when the person actually wants the bigger job rather than wanting to want it. You can redesign sideways with dignity: the title that fits what they are genuinely great at, named openly, celebrated, not whispered about, and I have seen a "head of operations" become a "founding baker" and gain status rather than lose it. Or you can part kindly, which chapter 10 covers and which is sometimes the truest respect available. The one unforgivable move is the silent one: hiring over someone without the conversation, and letting them discover their demotion in the org chart of a company they helped build.

The second people-fracture is directional: whether to hire ahead of the step or behind it. Hire ahead for anything with a long ramp, managers, salespeople, senior engineers, head bakers, because starting the search when you already need them means six months of the gap doing damage. Hire behind for pure capacity, where the work itself trains people fast. Getting this backwards is expensive in both directions: capacity hired ahead is payroll watching you burn cash, and leadership hired behind is a step taken with nobody steering it.

And then there is the moment nobody warns you about, so I will: the first employee you did not interview, did not choose, and could not pick out of a lineup. At the routing company it happened around employee thirty-five, in a kitchen, when a friendly man introduced himself and I realized mid-handshake that I had no idea whether he was a new hire or a customer. It is a small vertigo and it means the machine is working, and it also means the culture is now being transmitted entirely through channels that are not you, which is the subject of this chapter's final section.

Process breaks second

Small companies run on heroics, and heroics are genuinely the right technology at small scale: cheap, fast, flexible, and everyone knows who the heroes are. The problem is that heroics scale linearly at best. Every step you take adds surface area faster than it adds heroes, until one day the same act of improvisation that used to save the week now causes the week that needs saving, because three other teams had quietly built on top of the thing the hero just improvised around.

The fix is process, a word that makes founders itch, so let me define it harmlessly: process is the recipe leaving someone's head. Nothing more. Jonas's croissant recipe was, for eleven years, partly a document and partly the hands of the woman who ran his first shop. That was fine, right up until shop nine needed the croissants and the hands were forty minutes away.

My working rule is the rule of the third repetition. The first time you do something, do it however works. The second time, notice you are doing it again. The third time, write it down: one page, numbered steps, verbs first, written by the person who actually does the work rather than by whoever owns the nicest template. A playbook written this way is a floor, not a ceiling; people are free to beat it, and obliged to update it when they do.

Two failure modes, and you should fear them equally. The first is no process, which at scale produces chaos with your logo on it: nine bakeries making nine slightly different croissants, or a support team giving four customers four contradictory answers, each delivered with confidence. The second is process theater, which chapter 13 warned you about: forms that protect nobody, approvals that exist because of one bad day in 2019, documentation nobody reads maintained by someone everybody avoids. The test for any process is brutally simple: if it disappeared tomorrow, who would notice by Friday? If the answer is nobody, it is not process, it is sediment.

If you want early warning that process is breaking ahead of the step you are planning, watch three numbers: rework (how often the same thing is done twice), waiting (how long work sits between people), and escalations to the founder per week. That last one is the single best process gauge I know, because it counts, precisely, the number of times per week the machine could not answer a question without opening the founder's head to look inside.

Cash breaks third, quietly, then all at once

Chapter six taught the mechanics: cash is fact, profit is opinion, and the two travel on different calendars. If that chapter is fuzzy in your memory, go raid it, because scaling is the exam. Here I only need to add the one thing about cash that is specific to scaling, and it is this: growth eats cash before it feeds you, always, in every business model I have ever examined, and the meal is bigger and earlier than the spreadsheet says.

Walk through why. A scaling step means spending now for revenue later. The fit-out is paid before the first croissant. The salesperson is paid for six to nine months before her deals close. The German entity pays a notary, a lawyer, a payroll provider, and an office deposit before a single German invoice goes out, and when that invoice does go out, a fine upstanding German enterprise will pay it in sixty days. Every euro of the step leaves early and comes home late, and in between, the ordinary business must keep paying its ordinary bills. That in-between is where profitable companies die, and there is an old bookkeeper's word for it that deserves revival: overtrading. Selling more than your working capital can carry. It is the only way I know to go broke while your accountant is congratulating you.

Warning

The most dangerous month of a scaling step is not the month things go wrong. It is the month things go right. New location busy, new reps closing, big orders landing. Every one of those successes consumes cash immediately, ingredients, stock, payroll, onboarding, and pays you back on someone else's schedule. If you have not pre-funded success, good news will bankrupt you faster than bad news, because bad news at least has the decency to make you spend less.

The protections are unglamorous and they work. Fund the step with committed money, a facility arranged before you need it, an investor already wired, a buffer already saved, never with the hopeful cashflow of the old business, and yes, that pointed sentence is about a bakery. Budget the step at what it costs, then plan your survival at 150 percent of that cost and six months of delay, and if that survival plan does not exist, neither does your readiness. Keep the old business's working capital in a separate pocket the step cannot reach without a deliberate, documented decision. And if the step needs outside money, the instruments and their trade-offs live in chapter six; this chapter only insists that you arrange the umbrella while it is sunny.

Story

The rescue of the ninth bakery, since I promised. In the bad January, Jonas finally did what he later called "the thing I should have done in the leases-and-lawyers phase": he separated the step from the business. He went to his bank, with eleven years of clean numbers, and got a proper loan against the fit-out he had already paid for, effectively refinancing the step and putting the working capital back into the eight shops where it belonged. The interest he had refused to pay out of principle cost him, in the end, about 9,000 euros a year. He worked out on the back of a flour invoice that funding the step from operating cash had cost him roughly 70,000 in deferred maintenance, degraded quality, lost revenue, and stretched-supplier goodwill, plus one January he does not discuss in front of his wife.

Then he shrank the step until it matched his readiness. The café concept, the part that was a new business rather than a ninth copy, was cut back to a counter and eight seats, no table service. His head bakers went home to their shops within a fortnight, and the quality numbers in the old shops recovered in a quarter. The city shop reopened as what he actually knew how to run, a bakery, and only after it had been boringly profitable for a year did he add the café back, one waiter at a time. It is his best location now. "The building was never the problem," he told me. "I tried to climb two stairs and invent a third one, in the dark, carrying the other eight shops." Jonas has never read a business book. It took me four years and a ruined friendship-adjacent sale process to learn what he just said in one sentence.

You break last, and loudest

The fourth thing that breaks is the founder, and it breaks last because founders are load-bearing and stubborn, which means the structure absorbs enormous strain before the crack shows. Chapter 12 is the full treatment, the delegation ladder, the failure modes, the mirror speech Henk gave me that I still flinch at, so here I will only add what scaling specifically does to you.

Every riser on the staircase quietly rewrites your job description without notice. At one location you are a baker. At four you are a coach of bakers. At nine you are the designer of a system that coaches bakers, and the system does not care that you got into this because you love the smell of a proofing room at five in the morning. Scaling steadily replaces the work you chose with work you never chose, and the grief about that is real, unnamed, and behind more self-sabotage than any cash crisis. I have watched founders, myself included, dive back into the old hands-on work during the exact weeks the company most needed them on the system, not because it was useful but because it was home.

So during a step, watch yourself like you watch the quality numbers. The reliable early signals, in my experience: you become the bottleneck and start being praised for how hard you work around it, you stop sleeping through the night, you cancel the same recurring one-on-one three times, and you catch yourself feeling needed by the chaos and slightly disappointed by the calm. When two of those are true, the founder is the next thing on the breakage list, and chapter 12 is the raid target.

Scaling the Sales Engine

Chapter nine taught you founder-led selling, and founder-led selling is not a phase you failed to grow out of; it is the correct technology for finding out what your customers actually buy and why. This section is about the step beyond it: turning the thing only you can do into a thing an engine does, because the founder-led motion has one hard ceiling, and the ceiling is your calendar.

You will know you have hit the ceiling by feel: the pipeline is full, the close rate is fine, and growth has flattened anyway, because every deal still routes through your Tuesdays. The wrong response is the one nearly everyone tries first, which is to work more Tuesdays. The second wrong response is nearly everyone's second attempt, which is to hire a wonderful salesperson and give them your job. I know because I did it, twice, in the same year.

Story

At the routing company, when founder-led selling maxed out somewhere around two million in revenue, I hired the most impressive salesman I had ever met. Big logistics background, enterprise logos on the CV, a handshake like a hydraulic press and a watch that cost more than our server bill. Call him Ruud. Ruud was genuinely good at the job he had. The problem is that the job he had was operating a machine: a marketing department feeding him warm leads, a solutions engineer for demos, a legal team for contracts, a brand that opened doors before he knocked. At our place there was no machine. There was me, a shared spreadsheet, and a slide deck with my phone number on the last page. Ruud sat in the machine-shaped hole for seven months, closed almost nothing, and left before I could decide whose failure it was. It was mine. I had hired a train driver and been surprised he could not lay track.

The second attempt worked because we did it backwards. Before hiring anyone, I spent six weeks writing down what I actually did in deals: the eleven discovery questions I always asked, the three objections that killed us and the answers that saved us, who the real buyer was inside a delivery company (never the person who first emailed us), our walk-away price and the reference customers for each fleet size. It was twenty rather scruffy pages. Then we hired two salespeople at once, deliberately, a hunter and a farmer temperamentally, both from smaller companies where they had sold without a machine. Two, because with one you learn nothing: if she fails you cannot tell whether you hired wrong or built wrong. With two, if both fail the playbook is broken, if one fails you look at the hire, and if both succeed you have an engine. Both succeeded, at about eighty percent of my close rate, which the twenty scruffy pages turned out to deserve. I stayed the closer of last resort for the biggest deals for another two years, and the day the team asked me to please stop attending their demos was one of the proudest and most insulting days of my career.

The sequence that story encodes has five moves, and the order is the point.

First, write the playbook while you are still the best salesperson. Not a corporate binder. The scruffy twenty pages: who we sell to, who we refuse, the discovery questions, the demo that works, the objections and their answers, the pricing floor and who may cross it, the references. If you cannot write it, you do not yet have a sale that repeats, you have a founder who improvises well, and hiring people to imitate an improvisation is how you fund other companies' training programmes.

Second, hire for your stage, not your aspirations. The person who thrived inside a big machine will suffocate without one. You want people who have sold something unfamous from a company nobody had heard of, who treat the scruffy playbook as a gift rather than an insult. The same logic applies to sales leadership later: you want the person who has taken a team from two reps to ten, not the vice-president who once presided over five hundred, and the latter will interview better every single time.

Third, hire in pairs. One data point is an anecdote with a salary. Two is the beginning of an experiment.

Fourth, feed the engine before it starves. Founder-led selling ran on your network, your reputation, and warm intros, a fuel supply that does not transfer to employees. The week your first reps start, they need a source of leads that is not your address book: whatever fits your motion, outbound lists, partnerships, the marketing engine chapter eight's brand work makes possible. Reps without lead flow do not sit idle, which would merely be expensive. They chase bad-fit deals and discount hard to win them, which is expensive twice, now and at renewal.

Fifth, change your own job on purpose. You become coach, playbook editor, and closer of last resort, in that order of hours. The transfer takes quarters, not weeks, and there is a table for tracking whether it is actually happening, because founders lie to themselves about this one with special fluency.

How it failsYou run out of TuesdaysThe playbook is wrong at scale, and now it is wrong in stereo

One warning shot about discounting, because it is the leak that sinks scaled sales quietly. When you sold, you defended the price with conviction and context. A rep under quota, in the last week of a quarter, defends nothing; she discounts, because the incentive you designed tells her closed is closed. Multiply a casual twelve percent discount culture across an engine and you have not scaled sales, you have scaled a margin problem and attached commissions to it. The fix is structural, not moral: floors in the playbook, exceptions that need a named human's yes, and quota credited at full-price equivalent so a discount costs the rep what it costs you.
