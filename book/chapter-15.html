Chapter 15

Selling the Business

The wire transfer takes four seconds, and everything the books forgot to mention starts right after it lands.

The money arrived on a Tuesday morning at 11:52. I know the exact minute because I have a screenshot of the banking app, taken with unsteady hands at a kitchen table that had, an hour earlier, started belonging to a different man.

The notary had called at 10:40 to say the words she must say a hundred times a year and I will hear once in my life: completion has occurred. Fourteen years of the routing company, forty-one employees, some thousands of delivery vans following our suggestions around Europe every morning, and it all resolved into one sentence from a woman I had met twice, delivered in the tone you use to confirm a dentist appointment. Then she wished me a pleasant day.

I refreshed the banking app somewhere between thirty and forty times. When the number finally appeared, it sat directly on top of the transaction history of an ordinary life: groceries, the gym membership I kept forgetting to cancel, thirty euros to my nephew for his birthday. Then this. A number with more digits than the account had ever held, wearing the same font as the groceries, as if it were a normal thing that normal accounts do.

I had imagined that moment many times, I will admit that. In the imagined version there was champagne, or at least other people. In the real version I was alone in the kitchen, because the closing had happened remotely and my wife was at work, since neither of us had understood that this Tuesday would be the Tuesday. I looked at the number until it stopped meaning anything. Then I made a cheese sandwich, standing up, because fourteen years end and nobody hands you a script.

That is not the part I need to tell you about. This is: the next morning, Wednesday, I woke at 6:30 without an alarm, the way I had for years, and reached for my phone to check the overnight support queue. The app was gone. IT had done its job at midnight, clean and correct, exactly as agreed in a schedule I had signed myself. My email said my mailbox no longer existed. The dashboard that had been the first thing my eyes touched every morning for a decade, gone. I sat on the edge of the bed, a wealthy and healthy man of around forty, free in every way a person can be free, and I felt the specific vertigo of a cartoon character who has run off the edge of the canyon and just looked down.

For fourteen years I had woken up as the person the routing company was happening to. That Wednesday I woke up as a man with dead apps on his phone and no reason to get up before nine. It took me a lot longer than I expected to build a new reason, and nobody had warned me, not the lawyers, not the bankers, not one of the eleven advisors who had earned fees on my deal. Their job ends at the wire transfer. The Wednesday after appears on nobody's invoice.

In the introduction I promised that when we reached this cliff, I would bring photographs. This chapter is the photographs. We will walk the whole road: why founders actually sell, what your company is worth and what hearing that number does to you, the kinds of buyers and how to find them, the year-long tunnel of diligence and deal terms, earn-outs and their traps, and then the part that comes after, the cliff itself. I will tell you now what I wish someone had told me: the deal is a project, and the project is the easy part. The hard part is that you are also selling a version of yourself, and that transaction has no notary.

One more thing before we start. Read this chapter even if you never intend to sell. First, because a company built as if it could be sold, with clean books, documented processes, and a founder who is not load-bearing, is a better company to own and to work in. And second, because intentions are lovely and inboxes are patient. One day an email arrives, and you will want to have read this chapter before you answer it, not after.

Why Founders Actually Sell

Ask a founder at the closing dinner why they sold and you will hear the press release: the time was right, the buyer shares our values, this is the best next step for the team and the product. All of which may even be true. But the press release is the third or fourth draft. The first draft, the one written at 2 a.m. somewhere in the preceding years, usually says one of a small number of much plainer things.

I am tired. This is the honest number one, and nobody puts it on a slide. Not tired like a bad month. Tired like year eleven, when the problems started repeating and you noticed you were solving the same three issues you solved in year seven, only with more people in the room. Founder fatigue compounds, and it is a legitimate reason to sell. The trouble is that it is also the worst possible negotiating position, and buyers can smell it the way dogs smell fear. We will come back to that.

Everything I own is in one basket, and the basket has wheels. By the later years of the routing company, my house, my pension, my children's future, and my ego were all riding on one mid-sized software company in one niche of one industry. A single platform shift, one aggressive competitor with venture money, one lost anchor customer, and the family balance sheet would have gone with it. Selling, or selling part, is how a founder finally gets to do what every financial advisor tells everyone else to do on day one: diversify. There is no shame in it. Concentration built the wealth, and concentration is a terrible way to keep it.

The company needs a different owner to become what it should be. Sometimes true, sometimes a flattering translation of reason number one. But it is real: there are moments when the next phase needs a distribution network you do not have, capital you do not want to raise, or an appetite for a fight you have already fought once. If the company needs money to grow while you keep the wheel, be careful: that is raising, not selling, a different decision with a different chapter, chapter six.

The window is open. Industries consolidate in waves. For a few years the buyers are hungry, the multiples are generous, and your phone warms up. Then the wave passes, and the same company fetches a duller price for half a decade. Part of selling well is admitting that the market's calendar and your emotional calendar are unrelated documents. The routing company sold during a consolidation wave in logistics software. If the same buyers had come knocking three years later, I know exactly what the offers would have looked like, because I watched what happened to the founders who waited.

Life happened. Health, divorce, a parent who needs care, a partner who got a job abroad, a shareholder who needs out. These reasons are nobody's business and need no defending. The only advice I have is practical: deals take a year, so if you can feel one of these coming, start earlier than feels necessary.

And then there are the bad reasons, which deserve one honest paragraph. Do not sell to escape a problem that a chapter of this book could fix. Founders regularly try to sell their way out of a broken management team, a cash crunch, or their own burnout, and buyers pay badly for problems, when they pay at all. If the company is struggling, a sale at a distressed price is sometimes right, but check first whether what you are fleeing is fixable, because the fix is almost always worth more than the discount. And do not sell purely because a number turned your head. Numbers do that. It is their job. The real question is what you are buying with the number, and the answer had better be a life you actually want, described in more detail than "not this."

Story

The first serious approach came two years before I sold, an email from the corporate development man of a large logistics group, polite and vague in the way those emails are. I did what I had done with every large decision for twenty years: I drove to Henk's kitchen table.

He was well into his eighties by then, slower on the stairs, and he had taken to wearing a wool cardigan in a warm kitchen, reading glasses parked in his hair as if daring them to be useful. He held my phone at arm's length, read the email twice, and put it down as if it were somebody else's newspaper.

"Do you want to sell?"

"I don't know. The company is doing well, the team is strong, the market is..."

"I did not ask about the company," he said. "The company is fine. Companies are always fine until Tuesday. I asked about you. Do you want to sell?"

I sat there for a while, and then I said the true thing, which surprised me on its way out. "I don't know who I would be without it."

Henk nodded, like a customs officer accepting a declaration. "Now we have found the real problem, and it has nothing to do with price. Wouter. You built this company the way a carpenter builds a table." He tapped the table in question. "You are not the company. Sell it or keep it, I do not care. But do not keep it because you are afraid of meeting the man who is left over. That is fear calling itself loyalty."

Then, because he could never resist the practical footnote: "And if you do sell, sell it while you still love it. Buyers pay for a thing the owner loves. They can smell tired. It smells like discount."

Henk's line, you are not the company, will come back at the end of this chapter, because I did not understand it at his kitchen table. I understood it about eight months after the wire transfer, at the bottom of the cliff. But his practical footnote you can use today: the best time to sell is when you do not need to and do not entirely want to. Everything about a sale gets easier from strength, the price, the terms, the diligence, your own sleep. The paradox of exits is that they reward the founder who could happily keep going, and punish the one who cannot.

Try this

Take a pen and write two letters, one page each, both dated the day after a hypothetical closing. Letter one: to yourself, explaining why you sold, and describing an ordinary Tuesday in your new life, morning to evening, in boring detail. Letter two: explaining why you refused the offer and what the next five years of keeping the company look like. Do not skip the Tuesday detail, it is the whole exercise. One of these letters will be noticeably easier to write. That letter is telling you something the spreadsheet cannot.

The Number: Valuation from the Inside

Here is what every valuation method has in common: it is a way of arguing about the future using arithmetic about the past. Multiples of profit, multiples of revenue, discounted cash flows, comparable transactions. They are all, in the end, negotiation furniture. Useful furniture, you should understand it, but furniture. Your company is worth what a real buyer will actually wire on an actual day. Everything else is astrology with spreadsheets.

That said, the astrology has rules, and you should know them before anyone quotes you a number.

How buyers actually count

For most founder-led companies in Europe, the anchor is a multiple of normalized profit, usually EBITDA, which is roughly your operating profit before interest, tax, depreciation, and amortization, in other words profit before the accountants get creative. A stable services firm or a distributor might trade at three to five times EBITDA. A well-run niche software company with real recurring revenue might see six to ten, sometimes more in a hot consolidation wave. Businesses with high recurring revenue and low founder dependence sit at the top of their range. Project-based businesses where each January starts at zero sit at the bottom, and sometimes below it.

The word doing the heavy lifting in that paragraph is normalized. Buyers will restate your profit before they multiply it. Out go the one-off windfalls. Out goes the below-market salary you have been paying yourself, replaced by the market cost of a real managing director, which lowers profit and therefore price. Back in come the genuinely private costs you have been running through the company, the car that is nicer than the job requires, the conference in a suspiciously pleasant location. This restatement is called quality of earnings, and it produces the first of many moments in a sale when a stranger tells you what your life's work really earns, per their spreadsheet, and you have to sit still and hear it.

Then come the discounts, and this is where founders get surprised. Customer concentration: if your top three customers are forty percent of revenue, expect the multiple to drop, because the buyer is pricing the morning one of them leaves. Founder dependence: if the biggest deals close because of you, if the product roadmap lives in your head, if key customers have your personal mobile number, the buyer is not buying a company, they are buying a job with you in it, and they will price accordingly. Making yourself replaceable is chapter twelve's subject, and here is where that work pays out in cash. Every hour you spent turning yourself from the hero of the company into its architect converts, at exit, into multiple.

The gap between the headline and your bank account

Whatever number you agree, you will not receive it. This surprises everyone once. The headline is usually an enterprise value, the price of the business as a machine. From it, subtract the company's debt, add surplus cash, adjust for whether working capital is at the agreed normal level. Then subtract the part held back in escrow against future claims, typically for one to three years. Then subtract the earn-out, the slice of the price that exists only as a promise about future performance, which we will treat with appropriate suspicion later in this chapter. Then advisor fees, then tax, which varies wildly by country and structure and is the reason you talk to a tax advisor before the first offer, not after. A founder who sells for a headline of ten million euros and receives something like six on day one has met the normal anatomy of a deal, and you want to understand that anatomy before you start spending the headline in your head.

What the number does to your head

Now the part the corporate finance books skip. The first time someone puts a number on your company, something strange happens: it stops feeling like a price and starts feeling like a grade. A grade on your fourteen years, your marriage's patience, your missed birthdays, the nights you did not sleep in 2016. If the number is lower than you hoped, and it usually is, it lands like an insult from someone you wanted to impress. I have watched calm, rational founders blow up good deals over the last five percent, because the number had become their report card and they wanted the higher grade.

Two defenses. First, get the number early and privately, before any process, by asking an advisor for a cold, unflattering valuation range. Hear it, hate it, metabolize it on your own time. Second, and I say this gently: beware other founders' headlines. Exit numbers travel like fishing stories, and by the time a peer's sale reaches you at a conference dinner, the earn-out that never paid has merged into the headline, the escrow has evaporated, and the taxes have vanished. You are comparing your honest net to their decorated gross. Headline exits are Instagram for founders. You see the number. You do not see the terms.

The Menu of Exits

"Selling the business" sounds like one door. It is a corridor of doors, and they lead to very different rooms. The buyer type determines almost everything downstream: the price logic, the terms, what happens to your people, and what your own life looks like for the two years after closing. So before you think about price, think about which door you are standing in front of.

A strategic buyer, usually a competitor or a larger player in your industry, is buying your customers, your product, your geography, or the removal of you as an annoyance. Strategics tend to pay the best prices because they can count synergies you cannot, but they are also the buyer most likely to dissolve your company into theirs, brand, culture, coffee machine and all. The routing company sold to a strategic, and I will tell you honestly: the price was the best available and the integration still hurt to watch, even though they did it kindly and competently.

A financial buyer, private equity or a family holding, is buying your cash flows and your potential as a platform. They pay with discipline rather than romance, usually a defensible multiple of EBITDA, and they very often want you or your management team to stay and to keep meaningful skin in the game by rolling part of your proceeds into the new structure. This can be a genuine second act with someone else's capital, or it can be five more years of your old job with a new board, and it is worth knowing which one is on offer.

A management buyout sells the company to the people already running it. Prices are fair rather than spectacular, and since managers rarely have millions lying around, you often finance part of your own exit through a vendor loan, which means your money arrives slowly and stays exposed to the company's health. In exchange you get continuity, a protected culture, and the deep satisfaction of handing the keys to people who earned them. For many founders of stable, profitable, unglamorous companies, this is the best door in the corridor, and the least discussed.

Then there are the smaller doors. An individual buyer or search fund, one operator buying one company to run it, which lives or dies on personal fit and financing. Family succession, where the price is often symbolic and the real negotiation is emotional, conducted over years of Sunday dinners. The acqui-hire, mostly a software phenomenon, where the buyer wants your team and the product is a rounding error. And the door nobody photographs: the orderly wind-down, where you conclude the company is worth more as satisfied customers, well-referenced employees, and sold assets than as a going concern. Taken deliberately, early and solvent, it can be the most honest exit on the menu, and the founders who choose it do far better than the ones who arrive at it by exhaustion.

Finding a Buyer Without Hanging a Sign in the Window

You cannot list a company the way you list a house, because the listing itself does damage. If customers hear you are for sale, they delay renewals. If employees hear it, the best ones polish their CVs. If competitors hear it, they tell your customers for you, with embellishments. So the entire buyer-finding process runs on a strange fuel: discretion that is somehow also outreach. Every deal I have watched, including mine, ran under a code name for this reason. The routing company spent eleven months as Project Osprey, and I still feel a small jolt when I see the bird.

The inbound illusion

If your company is any good, you already get emails. Someone from a private equity firm is impressed by your journey and would love a call. A corporate development person at a bigger company happens to be looking at your space. Here is what nobody tells you: most of this is weather, in the chapter nine sense, noise that looks like signal. Junior analysts send hundreds of these to keep pipelines warm, and an eager reply to one of them is how founders end up in the single worst structure that exists, the one-buyer negotiation. With a single buyer at the table, the "market" becomes an appraisal conducted by the person who benefits from a low number. One buyer is a hostage situation with better catering.

Inbound can still be the spark. My own sale started from an inbound email, the one I drove to Henk about. The trick is to let an approach trigger a process rather than a negotiation. The correct response to a serious approach is warm, unhurried, and plural: you take the call, you say the company is not for sale but you are always happy to know your neighbors, and then, if the interest is real and your letters from the exercise above pointed at selling, you go build a market around that one buyer.

Hiring the person who does this for a living

Building that market is what M&A advisors do, and for any company worth more than a couple of million euros, you should probably hire one. Not the global investment banks, they will not return your calls at this size and you would not enjoy their invoices if they did. You want a boutique that closes five to fifteen deals a year in companies your size, ideally in or near your industry. They earn their fee three ways: they know which buyers are hungry right now, they create the plural that gives you leverage, and they absorb the conflict, playing the villain in negotiations so that you can keep being the person the buyer wants to work with afterwards.

The fee structure is a monthly retainer plus a success fee of a few percent of the deal, higher percentages at smaller sizes, often with a ratchet that pays them more above an agreed threshold. That ratchet is worth having, because it points their greed the same direction as yours. When choosing, ignore the league tables and ask three questions: show me the last ten deals you closed at my size, let me call three of those founders, and who exactly, which human being, will work on my deal. The partner charms you at the pitch, then a 29-year-old runs your process. Meet the 29-year-old.

The process itself, run properly, looks like this. The advisor helps you build a longlist of plausible buyers, typically twenty to sixty names, strategics and financials both. Here is a useful exercise even if you never sell: ask yourself who mentions your company in their board meetings, whose expansion plans you sit in the middle of, who loses deals to you and hates it. Those are your buyers. the interested sign NDAs and receive the information memorandum, a document that describes your company the way a good estate agent describes a house, accurate but sunlit. First-round indicative offers arrive, you pick a handful for management meetings, the strange theater where you pitch your life's work across a conference table to people deciding whether to buy it. Second-round offers follow, and then you choose, and here is the part that matters: you choose on price, terms, and believability as a package, never on price alone. A high number from a flaky buyer is worth less than a fair number from a serious one, for reasons the next two sections will make painfully clear.

The Shape of a Deal: A Year of Your Life

Founders consistently expect a sale to take three months. It takes nine to eighteen. Mine took eleven, from the first serious meeting to the notary's phone call, and that was considered brisk. You need to see the whole shape in advance, because the length is not an accident, it is a weapon. Buyers know that a tired seller is a cheaper seller, and some of them slow-walk the middle innings on purpose, waiting for deal fatigue to soften you. The founders who get hurt are the ones who mentally sold in month two and then spent seven months negotiating like people who just want it over.

Three things about this shape. First, the early phases overlap, preparation bleeds into marketing bleeds into offers, and that is normal. Second, the LOI in the middle, the letter of intent, is the deal's hinge: before it you have competition and leverage, after it you have granted one buyer exclusivity and your leverage drops by roughly half the moment the ink dries. Everything important should be negotiated into the LOI while you still have alternatives, price, structure, earn-out mechanics, what happens to the team, because negotiating it later means negotiating alone. Third, look at the grey bar at the bottom. The single most expensive mistake in this entire chapter is letting the business sag while you sell it. Your price was built on your trajectory. If the trajectory dips in month seven, the price follows it down, with enthusiasm.

Diligence: Strangers Read Your Diary

After the LOI comes due diligence, and I want to describe it accurately, because founders are always warned it will be "thorough" and never warned what thorough feels like. For two to four months, teams of accountants, lawyers, tax specialists, and technical experts, none of whom you chose and all of whom bill by the hour, will read everything your company has ever written down. Every contract, every employment agreement, every board minute, every tax filing, every customer renewal, the code, the leases, the insurance policies, the eleven-year-old shareholder agreement with the typo in your own name. They upload questions into a data room portal, hundreds of them, numbered like exhibits, and every question has a deadline and every answer breeds children.

It is not personal, and it feels intensely personal. Somewhere around week six, a 26-year-old you have never met will ask you to explain, in writing, a decision you made under fire nine years ago, and the explanation will sound thin even to you, because written down and read cold, decisions made in weather always sound thin. This is the phase where founders get worn down, and remember, the wearing down is partly the point. Deal fatigue is a negotiating tactic that bills by the hour.

Two rules got me through it. Rule one: never lie, and never let an optimistic answer slide into a false one, because the contract you eventually sign contains warranties, your personal promises that the answers were true, and a discovered lie converts your sale proceeds into a lawsuit fund. If there is a skeleton, and every company has a drawer, the unsigned contract with the biggest customer, the VAT question from 2019, the contractor who built half the product with no IP assignment, you disclose it, early and framed, on your terms. Disclosed skeletons cost a little price. Discovered skeletons cost deals, and worse, they cost trust in everything else you said. Rule two: split yourself in two, and give the deal your nights but never your mornings. Mornings belong to the company, because of the grey bar in the diagram. I answered data room questions from nine to midnight for four months and ran the routing company from eight to six, and it nearly broke me, and it was still the right allocation.

The re-trade

Late in diligence, some buyers come back with a lowered price, citing findings that are real but minor. This is called a re-trade, and with some buyers it was the plan all along: get you exclusive, get you tired, get you invested, then clip ten percent when walking away feels unbearable. Your defenses are all built earlier: competitive tension before the LOI, skeletons disclosed before they can be findings, a business that keeps performing, and a number, written down in advance with your advisor, below which you walk. Decide your walk-away price on a calm day in month one. You will not be able to compute it honestly in month nine.

Story

Priya nearly sold her B2B software company, three years before she actually did. An American strategic buyer arrived with a number that made her laugh out loud alone in her car, an LOI followed in six weeks, and she entered exclusivity in the spring, doing what most first-time sellers do: she started living in the deal. She flew to management meetings, staffed the data room personally, and mentally moved into the life on the other side. Sales discovery calls got delegated. The autumn marketing plan quietly died on a whiteboard. Why polish a company that would belong to someone else by Christmas?

In week fifteen of diligence, the buyer's sponsor, the executive whose pet project the acquisition was, left for another company. The deal did not die dramatically. It just got slower and colder, meetings pushed by a week, then two, and then a call in which a man she had never spoken to before cited "evolving strategic priorities" and, noting her softening quarterly pipeline, offered to proceed at seventy percent of the LOI price. The pipeline he cited had softened because she had spent two quarters feeding his data room instead of her funnel. The deal had caused the weakness it was now charging her for.

She walked, which took more nerve than signing would have. It took her company eighteen months to climb back to its old trajectory, and it took her about as long to stop doing the arithmetic of the mental life she had furnished and lost. When she finally did sell, years later, she ran the entire process by two rules taped inside her laptop lid: the company runs as if the deal does not exist, and the money is fictional until it is in the account. "The first time, I sold the company in my head in week three," she told me. "The buyer just never showed up to the version I had already moved into."

Priya's rules deserve to be yours. A signed LOI is not a deal. A handshake is not a deal. A congratulatory dinner where the buyer's CEO toasts your future together is, and I am sorry about this, not a deal. Deals die in exclusivity constantly, at rates that would shock you, for reasons that have nothing to do with you: the buyer's own numbers dip, a champion changes jobs, a bigger target appears, financing wobbles. Until money moves, you are running a company that happens to be in talks, and everyone on your team who knows should be able to recite that sentence from memory.

The good news is that diligence, unlike most ordeals, rewards preparation almost linearly, and you can start years early. Here is the checklist I wish I had worked through at the routing company in year ten instead of during the deal itself. Companies that do this in peacetime call it vendor readiness. I call it the drawer audit.

The Language of the Deal

Somewhere around the LOI, you will notice that everyone in the room is speaking a dialect you do not, and that every term in it quietly moves money from one side of the table to the other. This is not an accident. Deal language evolved among people who do ten of these a year, negotiating against people who do one per lifetime. The single cheapest thing you can do for your exit is to learn the vocabulary before you need it, so that when someone proposes "a standard locked box with a modest escrow and a performance-based deferred component," you hear what was actually said, which is: we fix the price early, keep part of your money hostage, and make another part of it contingent on things you will no longer control.

Working capital adjustmentPrice adjusts if working capital at closing deviates from an agreed normal levelThe most-fought-over boring clause in every deal. How "normal" gets calculated is worth real money. Let your accountant fight this one, it is what they are for.
Locked box vs completion accountsPrice fixed at a past balance sheet date, versus recalculated after closingLocked box gives certainty and an early effective date. Completion accounts give the buyer a second bite at the price after you have lost all leverage. Sellers generally want the box.
Earn-outPart of the price paid later, contingent on future performanceSee the next section, which exists because this row cannot hold everything I need to tell you.
Escrow / holdbackPart of the price parked with a third party for one to three years against warranty claimsNormal in moderation. Negotiate the size, the duration, and that it earns interest for you. Assume you see it late, and treat it as a bonus, not as budget.
Reps and warrantiesYour contractual statements that the facts disclosed are true, with liability if they are notThese are personal promises with your proceeds as collateral. Disclose everything, cap the liability, and never warrant something you have not personally verified.
W&I insuranceInsurance covering warranty claims, increasingly common in mid-sized dealsCosts a little, can free your escrow and let you sleep. Ask your advisor whether your deal is big enough for it, and let the buyer share the premium.
Vendor loanYou lend the buyer part of the price, repaid over yearsCommon in MBOs and small deals. You are now your buyer's bank, junior to their real bank. Price the risk honestly, and get security if you can.
Non-compete / non-solicitYour promise not to compete or poach staff and customers for a periodReasonable in scope is fine, they are buying the business, not renting it. But read it against your actual plans: three years, your industry, all of Europe, might quietly outlaw your next act. Chapter sixteen has opinions about your next act.
Good leaver / bad leaverClauses deciding what happens to your remaining stake or earn-out if you leave, resign, or are dismissed during the earn-out periodRead twice, then again. In sloppy drafts, the buyer can fire you and thereby cheapen the price. Make dismissal without cause a good-leaver event, always.
Field note

Assemble your own side of the table before the LOI, not after: an M&A lawyer who does deals for a living, not your regular corporate lawyer, however loyal, and a tax advisor who sees the structure before it hardens, because after signing, tax optimization becomes tax archaeology. Together they will cost real money, one to two percent of the deal is normal, and they are the best-paid hours of the entire process. The buyer does this every year. You do it once. Rent the experience gap shut.

Earn-Outs: The Deferred Argument

An earn-out sounds reasonable the first time you hear it, and that is what makes it dangerous. The buyer believes your forecast less than you do, so instead of arguing about the future, you agree to split the difference with it: a chunk of the price is paid over the next one to three years, if the company hits agreed targets. You get your full valuation if you were right, the buyer gets protection if you were wrong. It sounds like fairness. It compresses the entire gap between your optimism and their skepticism into a clause, and everyone signs and goes to dinner.

Here is the flaw, and it is structural, not moral: the earn-out makes your money depend on the performance of a company you no longer control, operated by the people who owe you the money. I do not mean buyers routinely cheat, most do not. I mean that the day after closing, your company stops being an organism and becomes an organ. The buyer, entirely legitimately, starts integrating: your prices get aligned to theirs, your star developer gets borrowed for the group platform, your sales team gets a new CRM in the middle of the quarter, head office allocates a share of group overhead onto your P&L, and every one of those sensible integration decisions moves your earn-out, and none of them require your consent. You will sit in meetings watching people optimize the group at the expense of your targets, and they will not be villains, they will be employees doing their jobs inside a system that no longer has your price in its objectives.

Mine was two years, and I collected about sixty percent of it. The forty percent I lost, I lost in a beige meeting room in another city, in a discussion about cost allocation methodology, and the man across the table was not hostile, he was just applying group policy to a subsidiary. I had negotiated the earn-out on revenue growth assumptions and let the targets be written on profit. Profit is the number integration touches most. That mistake has a price tag I still know to the euro.

So, the rules, earned the expensive way. First, treat the earn-out as weather, not as price. The price is what transfers at closing. If you would not do the deal for the closing amount alone, do not do the deal. Anything the earn-out later delivers is a pleasant storm of money, and if it delivers nothing, your life still works. Second, if you must have one, base it on revenue, not profit, because revenue is harder for integration to distort, and define every term with an accountant's paranoia: whose revenue, recognized how, including which products, in which entity. Third, keep it short. One year is an agreement, two is a tolerance, three is a hostage arrangement with quarterly reviews. Fourth, negotiate protective covenants: the buyer must run the unit in the ordinary course, must not divert customers or staff, must keep you in the seat with authority over the levers your targets depend on, and if they fire you without cause, the earn-out pays in full. And fifth, know what you are really being paid for during those years, because an earn-out is also a retention device, a way of making sure the founder shows up and hands over the relationships. That part is fair. Just price your servitude honestly: you will be an employee, in your own former building, watching someone repaint it. Some founders sail through that. I did not, entirely, and the next sections are about why.

Telling People

There is a cruel structural fact at the center of every sale: the people the deal affects most are the last to know. You will spend the better part of a year carrying a secret past the desks of people you hired, people who trust you, people who notice, because good people always notice, that you are tired, distracted, and oddly often "at the dentist." You will feel like a spy in your own company. This is normal, it is even correct, and it still corrodes something in you a little every week.

The sequencing that works is concentric circles, widened as late as possible. Your cofounder and shareholders are in from the start, obviously, and chapter five's advice about writing down exit intentions while you still like each other is what makes this circle painless or terrible. Your finance lead comes in early, because the data room cannot be built without them. The rest of the leadership team typically enters around the LOI, when diligence makes secrecy impractical, and each person you tell should hear two sentences from you directly: here is what is happening and why, and here is what I am negotiating on your behalf. Retention bonuses for key people, funded by you from the proceeds, are common, decent, and cheap insurance. Everyone else learns at signing or closing, not because you do not trust them, but because a rumor of a sale, unaccompanied by a signed deal, is pure poison: customers pause, competitors circulate the story with improvements, and your best engineer interviews elsewhere purely as a precaution.

Story

I told the routing company on a Thursday morning, all forty-one of them in the canteen, some on screens from the Lyon office. I had rehearsed for days. I had a speech about journeys and chapters, about how the group's resources would take what we built further than we could alone, and I delivered it adequately, voice cracking only once, at the sentence I had practiced most.

When I finished there was a silence, and then Ilse from support, nineteen years old when I hired her, eleven years ago by then, put up her hand and asked the only question that mattered: "Do we still have jobs?" Yes, I said, all of them, I had it in writing, it had cost me price and I would do it again. She nodded. Then Tomás from the routing team raised his hand and asked whether the good coffee machine would survive the integration, and the room laughed, and I understood something I have never forgotten: the sale was the biggest event of my professional life, and for them it was a Thursday with an announcement in it. Their lives were the mortgage, the commute, the colleagues, the coffee. My epic was their line item. That is not cynicism, that is health, theirs, and the sooner a selling founder grasps it, the kinder their communication gets.

Afterwards, an older developer named Ruud, who had been with me since year two, shook my hand in the corridor and said, "Congratulations. And condolences." He had sold a company once, long before. At the time I thought he was being witty. He was being precise.

Customers get told after closing, jointly with the buyer, in an order sorted by importance and flight risk, and the message that works is continuity with specifics: same team, same contacts, here is what improves for you. What you say matters less than what happens in the following ninety days, so negotiate the post-closing plan for customers as attentively as you negotiated the escrow. Your reputation rides on how the buyer treats them, for years after your name is off the door, and in your home market, where you will build whatever you build next, that reputation is the only asset from this deal that cannot be wired anywhere.

The Cliff

And now the part of the chapter I actually wrote this chapter for. Everything up to here, other books cover, some of them well. What follows, I have found mainly in late-night conversations with other sold founders, all of us slightly embarrassed to be discussing our excellent fortune in the tones people use for a bereavement.

Here is what nobody tells you: closing day is a cliff, and the cliff is not metaphorical the way business writing is usually metaphorical. There is a before, in which you are a person with a place in the world that requires no explanation, and an after, which begins the moment the wire lands and the phone goes quiet. And the drop between them is severe in exact proportion to how much of yourself you had stored in the company, which, for most founders, after a decade or more, is nearly everything without their noticing.

Think about what the company was silently doing for you, all those years, beyond the salary. It answered the question "what do you do?" at every party. It structured every day, every week, every year, without you ever once needing to decide what a Tuesday is for. It gave you a tribe with an inside language, a scoreboard that told you whether you were winning, a queue of problems perfectly shaped for your skills, and a reason to get up that renewed itself overnight like bread. Then one Tuesday at 11:52, in exchange for a number, all of it switches off at once. Salary you can replace, the number took care of that. Nobody wires you a reason to get up.

Let me narrate the curve, since I have walked it. The spike at closing lasts somewhere between a day and a month: euphoria, relief, dinners, a holiday that finally deserves the name. Then the pit opens, usually around month two, and it is made of specific things. Grief with no funeral: you have lost something enormous, but nobody sends flowers to a man who just got rich, so the grief goes unwitnessed, which is the loneliest kind. Status vertigo: at parties, "what do you do?" now produces a stammer, and you catch yourself saying "I used to run a company" in the past tense of a person describing someone who died. The strange silence of a phone that rang for fourteen years. The discovery that some friendships were proximity, colleagues you loved who, without the shared building, turn out to have been colleagues. And underneath all of it, if you are on an earn-out, the special limbo of badging into your own former building as an employee, watching the new owners repaint your decisions, needing permission where you used to give it.

The money, and I know how this sounds, is its own strangeness. You will feel guilt at your luck, roughly aimed at the employees who built the value and got salaries. You will be surrounded, within weeks, by well-dressed men from private banks who have your number by means you never quite establish, all offering to help with "the complexity of your new situation." You will notice yourself becoming cagey about the amount with friends, and you will notice a few friends becoming odd about it anyway. None of this is a tragedy, and I refuse to write the paragraph where a rich man asks for sympathy. It is simply disorientation, and disorientation plus idleness plus an unwitnessed grief is the exact recipe for the founders you have heard about who went a bit wrong after their exit, the impulsive yacht, the impulsive divorce, the impulsive new company built out of pure withdrawal. The pit is not dangerous because it is deep. It is dangerous because it is expensive to flail in.

The wire transfer takes four seconds. Becoming a person who is not the company takes about a year, and nobody sends flowers, because you are not supposed to be grieving.

What actually helps, from my own year in the pit and from comparing notes since. Keep a structure, immediately: I eventually rebuilt Tuesdays out of exercise, a woodworking course I was reliably bad at, school pickups, and two volunteer advisory roles, and the content mattered far less than the shape. Say yes slowly: I made a rule of twelve months with no major commitments, no new company, no big investments, no board seats collected like stamps, and every good thing in my current life fits through the door that rule kept open, while nearly every exited founder I know who skipped the waiting year bought at least one expensive mistake inside it. Get your grief witnessed: a therapist, or at minimum other sold founders, who are the only people who will not laugh when you describe crying in your car outside your own former office, which I did, in month four, having driven there without any errand, like a dog returning to a house the family moved out of. And protect your marriage or its equivalent through the transition, because the household had also organized itself around the company, and your sudden full-time presence in it is a renovation nobody requested.

And in month eight, at the bottom of the curve, I went back to Henk's kitchen table, because that is what the table was for. He was very old by then, and the stairs took a while, and the coffee was still black and the cigar smell had outlived, as far as I could tell, any actual cigars, possibly by decades.

"So," he said. "You got your money. And now you are nobody, and it is worse than you expected."

I said that was approximately the situation, yes.

"Good," he said, which was not the sympathy I had driven forty minutes for. "It is supposed to be. You put fourteen years of yourself into one thing, and the thing is gone, and it worked. Both are true, and you are the first person you have ever met to whom both happened at once. Give it a year and stop insulting the year by wishing it shorter." He turned his coffee cup a quarter turn, his version of a paragraph break. "I told you before you sold: you are not the company. You did not believe me, because back then the sentence was about the company. Now it is about you. You built one good thing, Wouter. What are the odds you contained exactly one good thing? Men who can build are not emptied by one building."

He was right, annoyingly, as usual, and it took the remaining months of the curve for the sentence to finish arriving. The far side of the cliff, I should tell you, does not return you to the old altitude, and this is the part I misunderstood in advance. You come out lower and wider, if that makes sense: no single thing holds all of you anymore, and once the vertigo passes, that turns out to be the healthiest configuration I have ever lived in. Whether you then take all this hard-won width and immediately roll it into another company, which a suspicious number of us do, is the subject of chapter sixteen, and I will meet you there.

The Short Version

  • Sell while you still love the company. Buyers pay for loved. Tired smells like discount, and the whole process rewards the founder who could happily keep going.
  • Interrogate your reason first: the sale should buy a life you can describe in Tuesday-level detail, not just an escape from a fixable problem.
  • Your company is worth what a real buyer wires on a real day. Normalize your earnings, reduce customer concentration, and make yourself replaceable, because founder dependence is the biggest discount of all.
  • One buyer is not a buyer, it is an appraisal by an interested party. Create competition, and rent an advisor who does this for a living to run it.
  • Negotiate everything important into the LOI while you still have alternatives. Your leverage halves the day exclusivity starts.
  • Prepare for diligence years early, disclose every skeleton before it becomes a finding, and write down your walk-away price on a calm day in month one.
  • Run the company as if the deal does not exist, because deals die in exclusivity constantly, and the price rests on the numbers you deliver during the process.
  • Treat the earn-out as weather, not price. If the closing amount alone does not justify the deal, do not sign it.
  • The money is fictional until it is in the account. Priya's rule. Tape it somewhere.
  • The cliff after closing is real. Plan the year after the sale as deliberately as the sale itself, say yes slowly for twelve months, and remember: you are not the company. You are the person who built it, and builders are not emptied by one building.
