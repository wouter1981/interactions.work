Chapter 16

Doing It Again

The second company is not a repeat performance. It is a new play, staged by someone who has finally stopped being afraid of the theatre.

Two springs ago, Sam bought me a coffee on a terrace in Utrecht and slid a single sheet of paper across the table, face down, like a man settling an old debt.

The last time Sam slid something across a table at me, we were twenty-two, and it was a pitch deck for a recipe app. Forty slides. A market size with three commas in it. A slide titled "Why Now" that did not survive contact with a single actual cook. You know how that story ends, because chapter five keeps the autopsy on file: eighteen months, no users, no money, and a friendship that needed two years and one very awkward wedding to fully recover.

This time it was one page. On it: a paragraph describing a problem, three numbers, a short list under the heading "Things I believe but have not checked," and at the bottom, in bold, a date and a sentence beginning "If by this date I cannot show..." A kill criterion. Sam, who once defended a recipe app the way a lawyer defends a guilty client, had written the terms of his own execution at the bottom of his own idea.

"Tell me why this fails," he said.

Not "what do you think," which is a request for applause wearing a fake moustache. Why does this fail. So I spent an hour genuinely trying to kill it. The idea was software for restaurant kitchens, tracking stock and waste, the least romantic corner of the food world he loved. I attacked the pricing, the sales motion, the assumption that chefs would ever touch a screen with wet hands. He wrote down every blow, argued back against about half, and thanked me for the other half. At one point he said, with the quiet satisfaction of a man describing a reliable car, "It is a boring company. That is a feature."

Walking to the station afterwards, it struck me that I had just watched the entire difference between a first company and a second one, performed live, by the same man, at the same kind of table. At twenty-two, Sam and I defended our idea to the death, and it died anyway, partly of our defending. At thirty-eight, Sam prosecuted his idea like a hostile witness, and it lived. That difference, what it is made of, what it costs, and how to get it without paying full price twice, is this whole chapter.

This is also the last chapter, which means it has two extra jobs. It has to talk honestly about the loop at the end of the road, the one the map in the introduction drew with a small arrow and the words "and around again." And it has to say goodbye properly, which books are usually as bad at as founders are. There is a letter near the end, and after the letter, an epilogue. But first, the loop.

You Will Probably Do It Again

I want to start with the conclusion, because it changes how you should read everything else: if you have built one company, you will probably try to build another. Not certainly. But probably, and sooner than you plan to.

I have no clean statistic for this, and I would not trust one if I did, but I can offer you the evidence of every founder dinner I have attended since I sold the routing company. Around those tables sit people who swore, hand on heart, sometimes in writing, that they were done. That they would garden. That they would finally learn to sail, or be present for their children in some vague but emphatic way, or do "a few small angel things." Give them eighteen months. Then watch them lean across the table, lower their voice as if confessing to a crime, and say, "So, I have been looking at something."

Why does this happen? Partly identity. If you spent ten or twenty years being the person who builds the thing, then not building the thing is not restful, it is amputated. Chapter fifteen talked about the cliff on the far side of the sale, the strange grief of waking up with money and no mission. That grief has a favourite medicine, and the medicine is a new company. Partly it is that the skill has no other arena. You have spent years getting good at an extremely specific sport, the sport of making promises to customers and keeping them without running out of money, and there is no veterans' league. And partly, honestly, it is that building things with people you chose, for customers who need it, is one of the best games available to an adult human, and you know it, because you have played it.

The itch has a specific texture. You will be on holiday, reading some harmless article about, say, the European market for heat pumps, and you will notice that you have opened a spreadsheet. You will give a friend "just a bit of advice" about her struggling company, and at midnight you will catch yourself rewriting her pricing page in your head, with a level of care and joy you have not felt in months. The itch does not announce itself as ambition. It announces itself as helpfulness, and curiosity, and one small spreadsheet that grows tabs.

There is even a schedule to it, reliable enough that friends of exited founders privately bet on it. Year one after the sale, the founder is publicly serene and privately jetlagged in a way no flight explains. Somewhere in year two, the sentences start changing shape. "I would never go back to that life" becomes "I would only do it again if the right thing came along," which becomes "I have been sketching something, purely as an exercise," which becomes a chamber of commerce registration. I sat across from a man at a dinner in Antwerp who delivered the full progression in one evening, between the starter and the cheese, apparently without noticing. Nobody laughed, because everyone at that table had either done the same or was two courses away from starting.

Here is what I need you to hold onto, though, and it is the load-bearing idea of this chapter: the second company is not the first company again. It is a different play. You are older, richer, better calibrated, more watched, and less hungry. Your starting conditions are so different that treating lap two as a rerun of lap one is not just a small mistake, it is a category error, and it is the category error that kills most sequels. Some of what you carry from the first company is treasure. Some of it is expired medicine in an impressive bottle. The work of this chapter is sorting one from the other before you swallow anything.

And there is one more complication, which readers of the introduction will see coming. By the time you consider doing it again, your first company has stopped being an experience and become a story. You have told it at dinners, in interviews if you had them, to yourself in the shower. And stories, as we established on page one, lie about the weather. Your memory has quietly tidied the desk, thrown out the terror and the flukes, and left a narrative in which your decisions caused your outcomes. Before you build on top of that story, you will need to un-tidy it. There is an exercise for that a few sections from now, and it is the least comfortable one in this book.

So this chapter walks the loop in order. What genuinely transfers to a second company. What dangerously does not. The ego trap that is waiting for you, personally, with your name on it. Rest, which turns out to be a phase with its own work rather than a reward with a hammock. The substitutes, angel investing and advising and their cousins, and how to tell whether they will feed you or just take the edge off. Then Sam's second act in full, a decision framework, a letter, and the road home.

What Transfers

Let me give you the good news first, because it is genuinely good and you have paid a great deal for it.

The first thing that transfers is judgment about people. This one is close to priceless. The first time around, you learned to hire by hiring wrong, which is the tuition model described in chapter ten, and the fees were brutal. You hired the brilliant jerk, the charming empty suit, the loyal friend who could not do the job. Each one took months to recognise and longer to resolve. The second time, you can smell trouble in the first twenty minutes of an interview. Not because you are psychic, but because you have a library now. The candidate who claims credit in suspiciously round numbers, the client who negotiates hard before the work has even started, the potential cofounder who talks about his last partner the way bitter divorcés talk. You have met them all before. First-time founders have to learn each of these as a surprise. You get them as reruns.

The second is cash instinct. If you did the work of chapter six the hard way, and almost everyone does it the hard way, you now feel runway in your body. You will never again confuse profit with cash, because you have lived inside the gap between the two, in that special month when the P&L smiled and the bank account wheezed. Henk's line, profit is opinion, cash is fact, is engraved somewhere behind your sternum. In the second company this shows up as an almost boring calm about money. You know what a thirteen-week cashflow is for. You know an invoice is not money. You know exactly how long a customer who "just needs sign-off from finance" can take, to the day. This calm is worth more than most of the capital you could raise.

The third is the phase map itself, the thing this whole book has been drawing. You now know that a sales slump can be an identity problem, that a scaling disaster is usually a skipped earlier chapter presenting its bill, that the fire and the fuel are often in different rooms. The first time, every phase ambushed you. The second time, you can hear phases coming down the corridor. You will still have to live them, the map is not the terrain, but you will waste far less time wondering what is happening to you and whether you are the only one. You are never the only one. You now know that in your bones instead of just from a book.

The fourth is calibrated fear, and I want to dwell on this one because nobody talks about it. First-time founders are afraid of everything at the same volume. A one-star review, a resignation, a tax letter, a competitor's press release, all of it arrives at 2 a.m. with the same siren. By the end of company one, your fear has been through calibration. You know which sirens mean fire and which mean a sensitive smoke detector. A big customer going quiet, real siren. A competitor raising a round, smoke detector. This calibration is why experienced founders seem unnervingly calm in crises. They are not braver. They just have a better sorted alarm system, and it lets them spend their adrenaline where it buys something.

The fifth is craft, plural. You can sell, because founder-led selling forced you to learn discovery and pricing conversations, and that craft moves to any product. You can run a meeting that ends on time with decisions in it. You can fire someone kindly, which is a terrible sentence and a real skill. You can read a contract and know which three clauses matter. None of this needs relearning. It is yours now, like riding a bike, if the bike occasionally invoiced you.

Let me make craft concrete with the least pleasant example, because it is the clearest. The first time I had to let someone go at the routing company, I prepared for a week, slept badly for two nights, and still made every classic mistake: I buried the message in the fourth paragraph of a speech, I negotiated against myself before he had said a word, and I managed to make a kind decision feel cruel through sheer clumsiness. The last time I had to do it, years later, the conversation took eleven minutes, he heard the decision in the first sentence, the terms were generous and clear, and we still exchange messages in December. Same painful act. The difference was a hundred repetitions of adjacent conversations in between. That difference gets on the boat with you to the second company, free of charge, and there are twenty other crafts like it in your luggage.

The sixth is the network, and its shadow twin, reputation. People return your calls now. Talent that would not have gambled on you at twenty-five will listen at forty-five. Banks behave differently. Suppliers extend terms. If you sold well, some customers of the old company will follow you out of pure curiosity. This is real and useful, and it comes with a catch big enough that it appears again in the next section wearing a darker coat.

And the seventh, if you did the mirror work of chapter twelve, is self-knowledge. You know your personal failure modes now. You know whether you are the founder who hides in product when sales get scary, or hires to avoid conflict, or holds on to decisions like a dog with a sock. The second company does not cure these, I am sorry to report. But you see them coming, and seeing them coming is most of the fight.

What Dangerously Does Not Transfer

Now the customs inspection.

The first thing that does not transfer is market knowledge, and this one catches almost everyone, because it feels like the crown jewel. You spent a decade becoming a genuine expert in your old market. You knew the buyers by first name, the price points to the euro, the seasonal rhythms, which trade fair mattered and which was a wine-tasting with lanyards. Here is the uncomfortable truth: that knowledge started expiring the day you stopped using it, and it expires faster than you think. Three years out of a market is a geological age. Your contacts have changed jobs. The channel that built your first company is now saturated, regulated, or dead. The pricing logic has moved. Worse, your knowledge does not feel expired. It feels crisp and authoritative, because expertise does not come with a best-before date printed on it. You have to stamp one on yourself.

The second thing that does not transfer is the playbook, and I mean this precisely. Your first company's playbook, the sales motion, the hiring sequence, the pricing model, the marketing channel, was not a set of universal truths. It was a set of answers, fitted to one specific body: that product, that market, that decade, that team. The routing company grew up on trade fairs and patient, unglamorous enterprise sales, eighteen-month cycles, pilots, procurement departments with fax machines. It worked because delivery fleets bought software that way, then. Take that same playbook into a product-led market where buyers want to swipe a credit card before lunch, and you will die politely, in well-run meetings, wondering why your excellent process produces nothing. A tailored suit is proof that the tailor can cut cloth. It is not proof it will fit the next body. Keep the tailoring skill. Burn the suit.

I ran this experiment on myself, involuntarily. Early in my current company, I caught myself budgeting for a stand at an industry fair, because that is what you do, that is how companies find customers, I had a decade of receipts proving it. Then I did the thing I am begging you to do, which is check the fitting before wearing the suit: I asked six people who matched my new customer profile when they had last attended a trade fair for anything. Two laughed. One said 2019, in a tone that suggested bereavement. The channel that built my previous company was, for these buyers, a nostalgia act. The correct channel turned out to be something I would have dismissed as unserious a decade earlier, and finding it required the beginner's work of chapter nine, done again, from zero, by a man who considered himself excused from it. Nobody is excused from it. That is the whole point of the suit metaphor, and I paid a conference deposit to learn it, so please enjoy it at no cost.

The third thing that does not transfer is timing, which is a polite word for luck. Your first company existed in specific weather: an interest rate, a technology wave, a competitor who fumbled, a regulation that landed in your favour, a big customer whose champion happened to like you. You cannot re-book the weather. And here memory, that helpful colleague from the introduction, does its most expensive tidying. In the story you now tell about company one, the lucky breaks have been quietly re-filed as strategy. The customer who found you by accident becomes "our go-to-market." The hire who walked in the door becomes "our talent magnet." I am not accusing you of vanity. I am accusing you of being human, and memory of doing its job, which is to make you feel coherent, not to keep you honest.

Experience transfers. Luck does not. The second company is how you find out which one you were running on.

The fourth thing that does not transfer, or transfers much less than you hope, is the team. There is a fantasy every exited founder entertains, usually in the bath: getting the old band back together. A few of them may come, and choose those few carefully. But most will not, and should not, and even the ones who come are not the same people. They were great in a company that had found its rhythm; a garage is a different instrument. And there is a subtler problem. If you transplant the old company's inner circle wholesale, you transplant its culture, its jokes, its assumptions, into a company that has not yet earned any of them. Chapter one argued that identity has to be designed on purpose, and that is true the second time too; a sequel that inherits its identity secondhand starts life wearing someone else's clothes.

The fifth thing that does not transfer is your own engine. I will be gentle here, but not dishonest. At twenty-four you could sprint on cheap pasta and adrenaline, sleep under a desk, and call it culture. Twenty years later you have a back, possibly a family, certainly a more accurate sense of what a decade of your life is worth. This is not weakness, and it is not fatal, but it must be planned for. The second company has to be designed for pace rather than sprint: fewer heroics, better delegation from month one, a founder who goes home. Chapter twelve made the case that your energy is a company asset; in the sequel it is a scarcer one, and you are the only person who can budget it.

Which brings us to the least comfortable exercise in this book. Before you trust your experience, audit your luck.

Try this

The luck audit. Write down the ten most important things that went right in your first company. Real, specific things: the anchor customer, the hire that changed everything, the price increase that stuck, the competitor that stumbled. Next to each one, write a single letter: S if it was mostly skill, W if it was mostly weather, B if it was honestly both. Rules: at least three of the ten must be W or the audit is void, and you must then do it again with someone who was there and has no reason to flatter you. Your cofounder. Your first employee. Your accountant, who remembers the weather with terrifying precision. The point is not to diminish what you did. The point is to know, before you bet years of your life on it, which parts of the last miracle were yours to repeat.

The Ego Trap of the Sequel

Everything in the previous section can be handled with humility and a notebook. This section is about the thing that prevents the humility, and it deserves its own heading because it is the single most common way second companies die. They do not usually die of markets. They die of motive.

Here is the trap in one sentence: the second company gets built to prove something about the first one.

If the first company succeeded, the whisper says: prove it was not luck. Show them the first one was no fluke, that you are a builder of companies, plural, a serial entrepreneur, a phrase that should always be pronounced with one raised eyebrow. If the first company failed, the whisper is crueller and stronger: prove you are not what the failure implied. Either way, the new company is conscripted at birth into an argument about the old one, and companies make terrible lawyers. A business built to serve customers makes decisions about customers. A business built to prove a point makes decisions about the audience, and the audience, unlike customers, pays in applause, which your bank does not accept.

The trap has symptoms, and they are gallingly easy to spot in others. First: skipping discovery. "I know this market" is the most expensive sentence in second-time entrepreneurship, usually pronounced about a market as it existed several years ago. The humble validation work of chapter four, the interviews, the ugly first offers, the discovery questions of chapter nine, all of it feels beneath you now, like a professor being asked to retake the entrance exam. Do the exam. The market is allowed to have changed while you were away, and it did.

Second: day-one infrastructure. The first-time founder underbuilds; the second-timer overbuilds, magnificently. A brand agency before a customer. A holding structure fit for a group of companies before there is a company. An office with a meeting room named after a philosopher. Three job offers out before the product has survived contact with a single stranger's money. It all feels like professionalism, like the hard-won lesson that you should do things properly this time. But infrastructure is a bet on a specific future, and you do not know the future yet. You only know the previous one.

Third: the announcement. First companies launch quietly because nobody cares. Second companies launch loudly because people do, and the loudness is a debt. Announce big and every later pivot becomes a public confession; chapter seven called pivots a healthy part of a living plan, but they are much harder to make with an audience scoring you against your own press release. The correction is old and simple: sell before you announce, and let the announcement, when it comes, be a report rather than a promise.

A word about the phrase "serial entrepreneur," since the world will start applying it to you around now. Notice what the phrase quietly does: it relocates your identity from the company to the sequence. A founder serves a business; a serial entrepreneur performs a career, and performances need audiences, and audiences need escalation. I have watched this label talk sensible people into raising money they did not need, for companies that did not want to be big, because the sequence demanded a louder next act. You do not have to accept the label. When someone introduces me with it, I say I am between mistakes, which is accurate, gets a laugh, and cancels the expected keynote. Find your own version of that sentence. It is cheaper than the alternative, which is becoming the audience's employee.

Fourth: refusing small revenue. The first invoice of a second company has a strange psychological weight. Some part of you feels that a founder of your standing should not be excited about 400 euros. That part of you is the enemy. Small revenue is not beneath you. Small revenue is the sound of a real customer with a real problem taking you seriously, and it is exactly as informative at forty-five as it was at nineteen.

Story

I will not tell you what my current company does, but I will tell you how it started, because it started badly in precisely the way this section describes, and I wrote the section from the inside.

Within six weeks of deciding to build again, and before I had spoken to one potential customer, I had: instructed a notary to set up a holding structure suitable for a fifty-person group, two notaries actually, because the first one asked questions I found annoying, filed trademarks in three classes, opened conversations with two brand agencies, and drafted a hiring plan with eight roles in it, including, I am ashamed to type this, a Head of Operations. For a company that did not exist. Whose product did not exist. Whose customer I had not met.

What saved me was coffee with the routing company's old CFO, a woman who audits sentences the way she audits ledgers. I talked for ten minutes about the structure and the brand direction and the hiring plan. She let me finish, stirred her coffee, and asked, "Nice. What week are you in?" I asked what she meant. "Of the thirteen-week cashflow. Which week. What came in." There was, of course, no cashflow, because there was no cash coming in, because there were no customers, because the founder had been decorating the theatre instead of writing the play. I went home, cancelled the agencies, parked the trademarks, and spent the next month doing the thing I should have started with: selling something small and ugly to one real person. The first invoice was 400 euros. Roughly the butcher's 350 from when I was nineteen, adjusted for inflation. It taught me more than the holding structure ever has.

Warning

The most dangerous investor in your second company is you. Exit money makes you a lenient backer: you approve your own ideas at board level, you extend your own runway without a milestone, and the company never feels gravity. A business that cannot run out of money learns nothing from spending it. Fund your sequel like a stranger would: a written amount, a written runway, kill criteria with dates on them, and one person with permission to read them back to you in a flat voice. Chapter six's runway math does not stop applying because the investor loves you.

There is one more form of the ego trap, quieter than the others: choosing the market itself to impress someone. The founder who did unglamorous logistics software and now, mysteriously, must do something in space, or AI, or whatever this decade's dinner-party currency is. If you find yourself drawn to a market and cannot explain the pull without mentioning how it will sound, that is not a market, that is a mirror. The boring test is Sam's, from the terrace: the best second companies are frequently the ones their founders describe, with satisfaction, as boring. Boring means the founder is done performing. Customers can feel it, the way they could smell improvisation in the introduction's story about our year-four sales mess. Calm is a brand, and it cannot be faked, only earned or inherited from a previous life.

Rest Is a Phase, Not a Reward

Between the last company and the next one there is a stretch of road that has no buildings on it, and almost every founder I know has tried to sprint across it as if it were embarrassing. It is not embarrassing. It is a phase, the seventeenth one this book never promised you, and like every phase it has its own work, its own weather, and its own ways of going wrong.

Chapter fifteen owns the cliff itself, the strange grief that follows the wire transfer, so I will not re-teach it here. What I want to describe is what the months after the cliff are actually for, because "rest" suggests a hammock, and what happens is closer to renovation.

The first stretch, call it months one to three, is decompression, and you will be worse at it than you expect. Your body, it turns out, has been keeping books all along, and once the adrenaline stops it presents the invoices: the sleep debt, the molars you have been grinding, the shoulder that has been living somewhere near your ear since the diligence process. You will check your phone with the reflex of a man checking a wound. It will not ring, or worse, it will ring with things that are no longer yours to fix, and you will have to practice, actually practice, the sentence "that is not my problem anymore" until it stops tasting strange. Do the admin of ending things. Sleep like it is a project. Say yes to lunches with no agenda. That is the whole job of this stretch, and it is a real job.

Then, somewhere around month four, boredom arrives, and it arrives in disguise. It does not say "I am bored." It says "I have an idea." Suddenly everything looks like a company: the bad software at your dentist, the empty shop on the corner, the inefficiency at the marina. Your spreadsheet grows tabs. And here is the distinction that I would tattoo on the inside of every exited founder's eyelids if they would hold still: boredom pushes, curiosity pulls. Boredom is a shove away from an emptiness you have not finished sitting with. Curiosity is a pull toward one specific problem that will not leave you alone. Companies started from the shove are rebound relationships. Companies started from the pull have a chance. The only way to tell them apart is time, which is exactly the thing the shove insists you do not have.

So the rules of the rest phase are mostly rules about reversibility. For six months, make no commitments you cannot walk back over a weekend. No leases. No cofounder promises, which are engagements, and chapter five has shown you what breaking one costs. No term sheets, in either direction. No buying a vineyard, a restaurant, or, and I say this with love for a specific friend, a fishing trawler. Cheap, reversible experiments are not just allowed but encouraged: a landing page, twenty conversations, a weekend prototype, a tab in the spreadsheet. When people push you for decisions, and they will, because the world finds a resting founder unbearable, use the sentence a wise, tired man once gave me: "I will tell you in March." It is astonishing how much nonsense dies quietly between now and March.

You will want a case study, so here is a composite one that several friends will each believe is about the other. A founder I know sold his company in the autumn and by February, month four, note the month, had signed a five-year lease on a building, because the idea that arrived with the boredom needed a home, and the building was a bargain, and doing things properly means committing. By summer the idea had quietly changed shape twice, which healthy ideas do, except each new shape had to fit the building instead of the customer. He describes that lease now as the most expensive way he has ever learned the difference between a decision and an anaesthetic. The company he eventually built, two years later, is doing fine. It operates, he enjoys telling people, from a rented room, month to month, above a physiotherapist.

The rest phase also has deeper work, and you can guess it from chapter twelve: the rebuilding of an identity with more than one leg. Spend the year becoming someone whose whole weight does not stand on the next company. Coach a team. Learn something with your hands, badly, in public. Repair the marriage that survived the company, and say the thank-yous out loud. This is not self-care garnish. It is structural engineering for the next lap, because a founder with one identity leg builds companies that must never wobble, and all companies wobble.

Field note

The timeline I have watched, across many founders: months one to three, sleep and admin. Months three to six, boredom wearing idea costumes. Months six to twelve, real curiosity emerging, usually about something quieter than expected. After twelve, decisions worth trusting. Founders run it faster and slower, but the order almost never changes, and the expensive mistake is always the same one: treating a month-four shove as a month-ten pull.

Angel Investing and Other Ways to Half-Scratch the Itch

Somewhere in the rest phase, an alternative will present itself, wearing excellent shoes: what if you stayed in the game without playing it? Become an angel investor. Take some board seats. Advise. Mentor. It sounds like the mature synthesis, all of the craft, none of the 2 a.m. It deserves an honest look, so here is one.

Angel investing, first. The pitch to yourself is that you will be the investor you wished you had, and your scar tissue will compound across a dozen companies instead of one. The reality, and I say this as someone with several cheques out in the world, is that you are a passenger. A well-informed, occasionally consulted passenger, but a passenger. You will watch founders drive your favourite road, and some of them will take a bend in a way you would not, and your entire toolkit for the situation is one phone call and the tone of voice you use on it. If your identity is builder, this is a peculiar torture. The money math is also blunter than the dinner-party version: most small cheques die, the winners take years to matter, and the whole activity only works with money you can genuinely wave goodbye to, the same honesty chapter six demanded about runway, pointed at your own portfolio.

Since we are being practical: in the European corners I know, a first angel cheque is usually somewhere between ten and fifty thousand euros, you should expect to write ten or fifteen of them before the portfolio tells you anything about your judgment, and you should expect the honest accounting to take most of a decade. Decide the total you are prepared to lose before you meet a single founder, because founders are, professionally, people who are good at moving that number upward in person. And write down, in advance, your rule about follow-on rounds, because the hardest cheque is not the first one into a company, it is the third one into a struggling one, requested by a founder you like, backed by a story you would have told yourself, word for word, fifteen years ago.

There is a real test for whether investing will feed you, and it is this: do you enjoy the companies you cannot control? Not tolerate. Enjoy. Some founders discover, to their surprise, that they love the pattern across ten companies more than the steering wheel of one, that helping a stranger see her pricing problem gives them the old spark without the old cortisol. Those people convert, genuinely, and become the rare angels worth their cap table space. Others discover that every board meeting is a small bereavement, an hour of watching someone else hold the pen. Those people should build, and the sooner they admit it the cheaper their portfolio will be.

Advising and board work sit one step closer to the action and are cheap to try, which is their great virtue. Done honestly, with preparation and real skin in the conversation, they are useful work and a good way to find out what you actually miss. Done as identity maintenance, a lanyard that says "still relevant," they curdle into theatre within two quarters, and founders can smell it. Buying an existing small business is the underrated middle path, especially in Europe, where a generation of owners is retiring with solid companies and no successors. You skip the chapters this book put first and inherit the ones it put in the middle: real customers, real cashflow, real hiring and structure problems from day one. It is building, with the loneliness moved to different rooms.

If what you miss is making promises to customers and keeping them with people you chose, nothing but building scratches that, and you might as well know it early, before your portfolio becomes a monument to avoidance.

Sam's Second Act

Now let me tell you the rest of the story from the terrace, because Sam has spent this whole book being the cautionary exhibit, and he has earned the ending.

After the recipe app died, Sam did the thing that looks, on the résumé of a future founder, like giving up: he took a job. Product roles at a food-delivery scale-up, then a supplier platform, ten years, all of it inside the world he had always actually loved, which was never apps, it was food. And in those ten years, mostly in the back of professional kitchens at unsociable hours, he watched. He watched chefs count courgettes on paper at midnight. He watched walk-in fridges eat five percent of a restaurant's margin in quiet, unrecorded waste. He watched a head chef, a woman he admired, do stock counts on a Sunday because the software her owners bought was built by people who had clearly never touched a wet kitchen. The problem picked him, slowly, over a decade. At twenty-two, our idea had arrived in one espresso. This one arrived like a tide.

What he did next reads like a checklist of this book, which is unfair to Sam, who did it years before I wrote any of this down. He kept his job for six months while he interviewed forty kitchens, the discovery work of chapter four done properly at last. The first version of the product was a spreadsheet, an actual spreadsheet, that he charged for, because he wanted the information in the transaction, not the applause. His first customer was a brasserie in Leiden that paid him ninety euros a month and complained constantly, which he correctly treated as gold. There was no website for the first year. No press release ever, that I know of. When he brought in a cofounder at month eight, an engineer from the supplier platform, they spent a weekend doing the prenup conversation from chapter five, roles, equity, exits, the works, on paper, like two men who had each been in a crash and compare scars easily. And on one page in his kitchen drawer, updated monthly, lived the plan, with its assumptions and its kill dates, the living document chapter seven begs for.

Story

Two years in, Sam's sync service failed at seven on a Saturday morning, which in his world is the worst possible hour, because breakfast service waits for no database. The head chef of his largest customer called him at 7:04 and swore at him for four minutes without repeating herself, an achievement, Sam noted, that required two languages. He apologised, woke his cofounder, had it fixed by 7:40, and sent the chef a bottle of something serious with a handwritten card.

Then he called me, and I braced for a shaken man. He was delighted. Audibly, indecently delighted. "Wouter," he said, "she swore at me for four minutes. Do you understand? People only swear like that about things they depend on." He let that sit for a second, and then he said the thing I have repeated to every founder who will hold still since: "Nobody ever swore at the recipe app. They just left."

Today the kitchen company is fourteen people and profitable, a phrase Sam pronounces the way other men say the names of their children. It is boring on purpose. It will never be written about, if he can help it. He intends to be running it at sixty, and having watched him across both acts, I believe him. The friendship, for the record, is now structured: once a year, over one long dinner, we prosecute each other's plans, hostile witness rules, notes taken. It is the best board meeting either of us attends.

I once asked Sam what boring actually buys him, because "boring" was clearly a load-bearing word and I wanted to see the engineering. He counted it off on his fingers, and I wrote it down in the car afterwards. Boring means no competitor raises forty million to subsidise his customers into leaving him. Boring means he can charge a fair price without a land-grab discount, so the company was profitable in its second year and every strategic decision since has been made by well-slept people who cannot be forced into anything. Boring means the trade press ignores him, so his roadmap is set by chefs instead of by narrative. And boring means that when he makes a mistake, and he makes them, the mistake happens in private, at a size the company can digest. "The recipe app was exciting," he said, pulling on his coat. "Excitement is what it costs to not know what you are doing."

Here is Sam's two acts side by side, and I want you to notice something before you read it: almost every row is the same founder making the opposite choice. That is what transfers best of all, if you let it. Not the playbook. The lesson.

That is the flavour of courage available to you on lap two, and it is the better vintage.

Deciding with Your Eyes Open

So. The rest has been rested, the luck has been audited, the ego has been looked at squarely in its handsome face. How do you actually decide whether to go again? Here are the tests I trust, in the order I would run them.

First, the pull test, which you already have from the rest section: is this a specific problem that will not leave you alone, or a general restlessness looking for a costume? Interrogate the idea the way Sam interrogates his: can you name the customer, the ache, the moment in their week when they would pay to make it stop? Chapter two taught a question worth re-asking here in its original form: what will people pay you to make go away? If your answer is a market, a trend, or a technology, that is a costume. If it is a person with a problem, on a day of the week, keep going.

Second, the pseudonym test, which is the cheapest ego detector I know. Imagine you had to build this company under a name nobody could connect to you. No announcement, no LinkedIn post, no "excited to share." Every customer won cold, every hire made on the merits of the actual job. Does the idea still glow? If the appeal dims when the audience is removed, you have your diagnosis, and it is section four of this chapter, and the treatment is more rest, not more company.

Third, the kitchen-table conversation, and I mean the one at home. The people who lived through your first company have votes this time, formal ones. Not a briefing after the domain is bought and the notary is instructed. A real conversation, before, with real numbers: the years, the money at risk, the version of you that shows up during a bad quarter, because they have met that version and you owe them the courtesy of admitting he exists. I have watched second companies succeed commercially and fail at this table. It is not a trade worth making, and the founders who made it will tell you so, quietly, at the dinners where everyone else is talking about growth.

Fourth, the size permission, which is less a test than a release form. The second company is allowed to be smaller than the first. Read that again, because the whole weight of the phrase "serial entrepreneur" pushes against it. There is no law of sequels that demands escalation. A ten-person company that pays well, serves its customers, funds your life and lets you attend the school play is not a step down from a hundred-person exit. It might be the point. Some of the happiest second acts I know are deliberately, luxuriously small, run by founders who finally have nothing to prove and have discovered that nothing-to-prove is a superpower with compounding returns.

If the tests all pass, do one last piece of paperwork before you tell anyone: write the sequel's terms of engagement, one page, dated, signed by you. How many years you are giving it before you evaluate honestly. How much money, the number from the warning earlier, in writing. What you are explicitly not going to do this time, your three vetoes, taken straight from the luck audit and the refuse-to-repeat line on your folded paper. And what "enough" looks like, in revenue, in headcount, in what a Tuesday feels like, so that success cannot be quietly redefined upward by the audience later. This is chapter seven's living plan applied to your own life, and it takes an evening. The founders I know who wrote it treat it like a keel. The ones who did not tend to discover, three years in, that they are running the exact company they swore they would never build again, and cannot name the meeting where they decided to.

And fifth, the permission that this chapter has been quietly holding until now: you are allowed not to. Some people are one-great-company people, and they are not failed serial entrepreneurs any more than a person with one great marriage is a failed romantic. If the honest answer, after the rest and the audits and the tests, is that you would rather teach, or invest, or sail, or sit on two boards and grow impossible tomatoes, then that is not the road ending. The road also loops for you. It just loops through different country, and it is still the long way, and it still counts.

Try this

Go and get the piece of paper. The one from the introduction, folded inside the back cover, where you wrote the phase your business was loudest in and the phase you were avoiding. If you skipped the exercise back then, this book forgives raiders, write both answers now as you remember that earlier self would have written them. Unfold it. Compare it with what actually happened. Be amused; past you was guessing, and so is present you, just with better instruments. Then turn the paper over and write three lines: "If I ever do it again, the one thing I refuse to repeat is..." with one honest sentence. "The phase I would genuinely enjoy entering again is..." and "The phase that tires me just to imagine is..." If the tired line was easier to write than the enjoy line, you have your answer about timing, and it is: not yet. Fold it up. Put it back inside the cover, for whichever you opens this book next.

A Closing Letter

Dear reader,

Sixteen phases ago I promised you the weather, and I have done my best to report it: what each stretch of this road feels like from inside, while it is raining, before anyone knows the ending. Before you go, three things, briefly, the way you would say them at the door with your coat already on.

First: the phases loop, and you will be a beginner again, repeatedly, forever. The founder of a second company is a beginner at being a second-time founder. The seller of a business is a beginner at being sold. There is no version of this road where you arrive at competence and stay there, and once you stop expecting one, the beginnings become the good part. You now know what almost nobody tells beginners: everyone is doing it sideways, in the rain, making it up. You will be making it up with better instruments, that is all, and that is a great deal.

Second: trust your eyes over this book. I said it in the introduction and it is truer now that you have read the whole map. Where these chapters contradict what you can see in your own business, on your own terrain, with your own customers, the terrain wins. A field guide that outranks the field is a scripture, and I have no interest in writing one of those. Argue with me at any dinner table in Europe. I will bring wine, and Henk's ghostly disapproval of us both.

Third, and this is the one I would fold into your pocket if I could: the point was never the exit. I have been to the exit. It is a Tuesday with a notary and better catering than usual, and then it is chapter fifteen, and then it is this chapter. The point was the road: the people who became themselves working next to you, the customers whose problem genuinely went away, the person you had to become to keep all those promises. If you build again, build for that. It is the only return that survives the wire transfer.

Thank you for walking the long way with me. Take care of your people. Tell the truth about the weather when it is your turn to write it down.

Your friend on the road,
Wouter

Epilogue: And Around Again

Story

When the manuscript of this book was finished, I printed it, put it in a bag, and drove to Henk's, to the same kitchen table where a nineteen-year-old once tried to impress him with a famous founder memoir. Henk is old now, slower on the stairs, coffee still black, still faintly cigar-scented, still, in all the years I have known him, never once seen smoking. I put the stack of paper on the table and said nothing, because with Henk you learn to submit evidence and wait.

He read the back page first, which is very Henk. Then he opened it somewhere in the middle, chapter six I think, and read for about ninety seconds, exactly the sentence he once gave the memoir. Then he closed it and pushed it back across the table.

"Better weather," he said.

We drank the coffee. Then, because he cannot help himself, he asked, "So what are you building now?" I said I was mostly resting, mostly writing, keeping things small on purpose, and he looked at me the way he looked at that memoir all those years ago, like a man being told the truth about decisions and lies about the weather. "Resting," he said. He turned his cup a quarter turn, his version of a long speech. "The road does not care what you call your walking. It goes around anyway."

He is right, of course. He is always right, it remains extremely annoying. Look at the map from the introduction once more, but from higher up this time, the way you can only see a road after you have walked it.

The introduction drew this road as a winding line with a small arrow at the end, doubling back. Sixteen chapters later you can see what the arrow was admitting all along: the line was a loop. The idea leads to the company, the company to the years, the years to the sale, the sale to the rest, the rest to the itch, and the itch, in most of the lives I have watched closely, leads back around to an idea, standing at the top of the map like it never left.

But here is the thing the map cannot show, and it is the last thing I have for you. The road is the same both times. You are not. The bends that terrified you are still bends, but you know them now, you know which ones tighten and which ones only look sharp. The weather is still the weather, but you have stopped expecting it to be fair. And somewhere out on the second lap, or the first, you will pass someone younger, walking the other way around some bend you know by heart, defending their forty slides to anyone who will listen. Buy them a coffee. Tell them the truth about the weather. It is the only toll this road charges.

So: around again, then. The long way. As if there were any other.

The Short Version

  • You will probably do it again. Plan for that on purpose instead of being ambushed by it in month eighteen of your retirement.
  • Judgment, cash instinct, craft, calibrated fear and self-knowledge transfer. Market knowledge, the old playbook, the old team and the old luck do not.
  • Audit your luck before you trust your experience. At least three of your first company's ten best breaks were weather. Find them.
  • The deadliest sequel is the one built to prove something about the original. Companies make terrible lawyers.
  • Do not fund yourself so comfortably that the company never feels gravity. Written runway, kill dates, one person allowed to read them back to you.
  • Rest is a phase with its own work. Months one to three are decompression, month four's brilliant idea is usually boredom in a costume. Boredom pushes, curiosity pulls. Wait for the pull.
  • Angel investing is a passenger seat. Take it only if you honestly enjoy companies you cannot steer.
  • The second company is allowed to be smaller than the first. Nothing-to-prove is a superpower.
  • Not going again is also a valid ending. One great company is a whole career.
  • Same road, new walker. That is the entire trade, and it is a good one.
