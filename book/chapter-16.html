Chapter 16

Doing It Again

The second company is not a repeat performance. It is a new play, staged by someone who has finally stopped being afraid of the theatre.

Two springs ago, Sam bought me a coffee on a terrace in Utrecht and slid a single sheet of paper across the table, face down, like a man settling an old debt.

The last time Sam slid something across a table at me, we were twenty-two, and it was a pitch deck for a recipe app. Forty slides. A market size with three commas in it. A slide titled "Why Now" that did not survive contact with a single actual cook. You know how that story ends, because chapter five keeps the autopsy on file: eighteen months, no users, no money, and a friendship that needed two years and one very awkward wedding to fully recover.

This time it was one page. On it: a paragraph describing a problem, three numbers, a short list under the heading "Things I believe but have not checked," and at the bottom, in bold, a date and a sentence beginning "If by this date I cannot show..." A kill criterion. Sam, who once defended a recipe app the way a lawyer defends a guilty client, had written the terms of his own execution at the bottom of his own idea.

"Tell me why this fails," he said.

Not "what do you think," which is a request for applause wearing a fake moustache. Why does this fail. So I spent an hour genuinely trying to kill it. The idea was software for restaurant kitchens, tracking stock and waste, the least romantic corner of the food world he loved. I attacked the pricing, the sales motion, the assumption that chefs would ever touch a screen with wet hands. He wrote down every blow, argued back against about half, and thanked me for the other half. At one point he said, with the quiet satisfaction of a man describing a reliable car, "It is a boring company. That is a feature."

Walking to the station afterwards, it struck me that I had just watched the entire difference between a first company and a second one, performed live, by the same man, at the same kind of table. At twenty-two, Sam and I defended our idea to the death, and it died anyway, partly of our defending. At thirty-eight, Sam prosecuted his idea like a hostile witness, and it lived. That difference, what it is made of, what it costs, and how to get it without paying full price twice, is this whole chapter.

This is also the last chapter, which means it has two extra jobs. It has to talk honestly about the loop at the end of the road, the one the map in the introduction drew with a small arrow and the words "and around again." And it has to say goodbye properly, which books are usually as bad at as founders are. There is a letter near the end, and after the letter, an epilogue. But first, the loop.

You Will Probably Do It Again

I want to start with the conclusion, because it changes how you should read everything else: if you have built one company, you will probably try to build another. Not certainly. But probably, and sooner than you plan to.

I have no clean statistic for this, and I would not trust one if I did, but I can offer you the evidence of every founder dinner I have attended since I sold the routing company. Around those tables sit people who swore, hand on heart, sometimes in writing, that they were done. That they would garden. That they would finally learn to sail, or be present for their children in some vague but emphatic way, or do "a few small angel things." Give them eighteen months. Then watch them lean across the table, lower their voice as if confessing to a crime, and say, "So, I have been looking at something."

Why does this happen? Partly identity. If you spent ten or twenty years being the person who builds the thing, then not building the thing is not restful, it is amputated. Chapter fifteen talked about the cliff on the far side of the sale, the strange grief of waking up with money and no mission. That grief has a favourite medicine, and the medicine is a new company. Partly it is that the skill has no other arena. You have spent years getting good at an extremely specific sport, the sport of making promises to customers and keeping them without running out of money, and there is no veterans' league. And partly, honestly, it is that building things with people you chose, for customers who need it, is one of the best games available to an adult human, and you know it, because you have played it.

The itch has a specific texture. You will be on holiday, reading some harmless article about, say, the European market for heat pumps, and you will notice that you have opened a spreadsheet. You will give a friend "just a bit of advice" about her struggling company, and at midnight you will catch yourself rewriting her pricing page in your head, with a level of care and joy you have not felt in months. The itch does not announce itself as ambition. It announces itself as helpfulness, and curiosity, and one small spreadsheet that grows tabs.

Here is what I need you to hold onto, though, and it is the load-bearing idea of this chapter: the second company is not the first company again. It is a different play. You are older, richer, better calibrated, more watched, and less hungry. Your starting conditions are so different that treating lap two as a rerun of lap one is not just a small mistake, it is a category error, and it is the category error that kills most sequels. Some of what you carry from the first company is treasure. Some of it is expired medicine in an impressive bottle. The work of this chapter is sorting one from the other before you swallow anything.

And there is one more complication, which readers of the introduction will see coming. By the time you consider doing it again, your first company has stopped being an experience and become a story. You have told it at dinners, in interviews if you had them, to yourself in the shower. And stories, as we established on page one, lie about the weather. Your memory has quietly tidied the desk, thrown out the terror and the flukes, and left a narrative in which your decisions caused your outcomes. Before you build on top of that story, you will need to un-tidy it. There is an exercise for that a few sections from now, and it is the least comfortable one in this book.

So this chapter walks the loop in order. What genuinely transfers to a second company. What dangerously does not. The ego trap that is waiting for you, personally, with your name on it. Rest, which turns out to be a phase with its own work rather than a reward with an hammock. The substitutes, angel investing and advising and their cousins, and how to tell whether they will feed you or just take the edge off. Then Sam's second act in full, a decision framework, a letter, and the road home.

What Transfers

Let me give you the good news first, because it is genuinely good and you have paid a great deal for it.

The first thing that transfers is judgment about people. This one is close to priceless. The first time around, you learned to hire by hiring wrong, which is the tuition model described in chapter ten, and the fees were brutal. You hired the brilliant jerk, the charming empty suit, the loyal friend who could not do the job. Each one took months to recognise and longer to resolve. The second time, you can smell trouble in the first twenty minutes of an interview. Not because you are psychic, but because you have a library now. The candidate who claims credit in suspiciously round numbers, the client who negotiates hard before the work has even started, the potential cofounder who talks about his last partner the way bitter divorcés talk. You have met them all before. First-time founders have to learn each of these as a surprise. You get them as reruns.

The second is cash instinct. If you did the work of chapter six the hard way, and almost everyone does it the hard way, you now feel runway in your body. You will never again confuse profit with cash, because you have lived inside the gap between the two, in that special month when the P&L smiled and the bank account wheezed. Henk's line, profit is opinion, cash is fact, is engraved somewhere behind your sternum. In the second company this shows up as an almost boring calm about money. You know what a thirteen-week cashflow is for. You know an invoice is not money. You know exactly how long a customer who "just needs sign-off from finance" can take, to the day. This calm is worth more than most of the capital you could raise.

The third is the phase map itself, the thing this whole book has been drawing. You now know that a sales slump can be an identity problem, that a scaling disaster is usually a skipped earlier chapter presenting its bill, that the fire and the fuel are often in different rooms. The first time, every phase ambushed you. The second time, you can hear phases coming down the corridor. You will still have to live them, the map is not the terrain, but you will waste far less time wondering what is happening to you and whether you are the only one. You are never the only one. You now know that in your bones instead of just from a book.

The fourth is calibrated fear, and I want to dwell on this one because nobody talks about it. First-time founders are afraid of everything at the same volume. A one-star review, a resignation, a tax letter, a competitor's press release, all of it arrives at 2 a.m. with the same siren. By the end of company one, your fear has been through calibration. You know which sirens mean fire and which mean a sensitive smoke detector. A big customer going quiet, real siren. A competitor raising a round, smoke detector. This calibration is why experienced founders seem unnervingly calm in crises. They are not braver. They just have a better sorted alarm system, and it lets them spend their adrenaline where it buys something.

The fifth is craft, plural. You can sell, because founder-led selling forced you to learn discovery and pricing conversations, and that craft moves to any product. You can run a meeting that ends on time with decisions in it. You can fire someone kindly, which is a terrible sentence and a real skill. You can read a contract and know which three clauses matter. None of this needs relearning. It is yours now, like riding a bike, if the bike occasionally invoiced you.

The sixth is the network, and its shadow twin, reputation. People return your calls now. Talent that would not have gambled on you at twenty-five will listen at forty-five. Banks behave differently. Suppliers extend terms. If you sold well, some customers of the old company will follow you out of pure curiosity. This is real and useful, and it comes with a catch big enough that it appears again in the next section wearing a darker coat.

And the seventh, if you did the mirror work of chapter twelve, is self-knowledge. You know your personal failure modes now. You know whether you are the founder who hides in product when sales get scary, or hires to avoid conflict, or holds on to decisions like a dog with a sock. The second company does not cure these, I am sorry to report. But you see them coming, and seeing them coming is most of the fight.

But the same confidence that carries the treasure also smuggles in the contraband, and the contraband is the subject of the next section, which is the most important one in this chapter.

What Dangerously Does Not Transfer

Now the customs inspection.

The first thing that does not transfer is market knowledge, and this one catches almost everyone, because it feels like the crown jewel. You spent a decade becoming a genuine expert in your old market. You knew the buyers by first name, the price points to the euro, the seasonal rhythms, which trade fair mattered and which was a wine-tasting with lanyards. Here is the uncomfortable truth: that knowledge started expiring the day you stopped using it, and it expires faster than you think. Three years out of a market is a geological age. Your contacts have changed jobs. The channel that built your first company is now saturated, regulated, or dead. The pricing logic has moved. Worse, your knowledge does not feel expired. It feels crisp and authoritative, because expertise does not come with a best-before date printed on it. You have to stamp one on yourself.

The second thing that does not transfer is the playbook, and I mean this precisely. Your first company's playbook, the sales motion, the hiring sequence, the pricing model, the marketing channel, was not a set of universal truths. It was a set of answers, fitted to one specific body: that product, that market, that decade, that team. The routing company grew up on trade fairs and patient, unglamorous enterprise sales, eighteen-month cycles, pilots, procurement departments with fax machines. It worked because delivery fleets bought software that way, then. Take that same playbook into a product-led market where buyers want to swipe a credit card before lunch, and you will die politely, in well-run meetings, wondering why your excellent process produces nothing. A tailored suit is proof that the tailor can cut cloth. It is not proof it will fit the next body. Keep the tailoring skill. Burn the suit.

The third thing that does not transfer is timing, which is a polite word for luck. Your first company existed in specific weather: an interest rate, a technology wave, a competitor who fumbled, a regulation that landed in your favour, a big customer whose champion happened to like you. You cannot re-book the weather. And here memory, that helpful colleague from the introduction, does its most expensive tidying. In the story you now tell about company one, the lucky breaks have been quietly re-filed as strategy. The customer who found you by accident becomes "our go-to-market." The hire who walked in the door becomes "our talent magnet." I am not accusing you of vanity. I am accusing you of being human, and memory of doing its job, which is to make you feel coherent, not to keep you honest.

Experience transfers. Luck does not. The second company is how you find out which one you were running on.

The fourth thing that does not transfer, or transfers much less than you hope, is the team. There is a fantasy every exited founder entertains, usually in the bath: getting the old band back together. A few of them may come, and choose those few carefully. But most will not, and should not, and even the ones who come are not the same people. They were great in a company that had found its rhythm; a garage is a different instrument. And there is a subtler problem. If you transplant the old company's inner circle wholesale, you transplant its culture, its jokes, its assumptions, into a company that has not yet earned any of them. Chapter one argued that identity has to be designed on purpose, and that is true the second time too; a sequel that inherits its identity secondhand starts life wearing someone else's clothes.

The fifth thing that does not transfer is your own engine. I will be gentle here, but not dishonest. At twenty-four you could sprint on cheap pasta and adrenaline, sleep under a desk, and call it culture. Twenty years later you have a back, possibly a family, certainly a more accurate sense of what a decade of your life is worth. This is not weakness, and it is not fatal, but it must be planned for. The second company has to be designed for pace rather than sprint: fewer heroics, better delegation from month one, a founder who goes home. Chapter twelve made the case that your energy is a company asset; in the sequel it is a scarcer one, and you are the only person who can budget it.

Which brings us to the least comfortable exercise in this book. Before you trust your experience, audit your luck.

Try this

The luck audit. Write down the ten most important things that went right in your first company. Real, specific things: the anchor customer, the hire that changed everything, the price increase that stuck, the competitor that stumbled. Next to each one, write a single letter: S if it was mostly skill, W if it was mostly weather, B if it was honestly both. Rules: at least three of the ten must be W or the audit is void, and you must then do it again with someone who was there and has no reason to flatter you. Your cofounder. Your first employee. Your accountant, who remembers the weather with terrifying precision. The point is not to diminish what you did. The point is to know, before you bet years of your life on it, which parts of the last miracle were yours to repeat.

The Ego Trap of the Sequel

Everything in the previous section can be handled with humility and a notebook. This section is about the thing that prevents the humility, and it deserves its own heading because it is the single most common way second companies die. They do not usually die of markets. They die of motive.

Here is the trap in one sentence: the second company gets built to prove something about the first one.

If the first company succeeded, the whisper says: prove it was not luck. Show them the first one was no fluke, that you are a builder of companies, plural, a serial entrepreneur, a phrase that should always be pronounced with one raised eyebrow. If the first company failed, the whisper is crueller and stronger: prove you are not what the failure implied. Either way, the new company is conscripted at birth into an argument about the old one, and companies make terrible lawyers. A business built to serve customers makes decisions about customers. A business built to prove a point makes decisions about the audience, and the audience, unlike customers, pays in applause, which your bank does not accept.

The trap has symptoms, and they are gallingly easy to spot in others. First: skipping discovery. "I know this market" is the most expensive sentence in second-time entrepreneurship, usually pronounced about a market as it existed several years ago. The humble validation work of chapter four, the interviews, the ugly first offers, the discovery questions of chapter nine, all of it feels beneath you now, like a professor being asked to retake the entrance exam. Do the exam. The market is allowed to have changed while you were away, and it did.

Second: day-one infrastructure. The first-time founder underbuilds; the second-timer overbuilds, magnificently. A brand agency before a customer. A holding structure fit for a group of companies before there is a company. An office with a meeting room named after a philosopher. Three job offers out before the product has survived contact with a single stranger's money. It all feels like professionalism, like the hard-won lesson that you should do things properly this time. But infrastructure is a bet on a specific future, and you do not know the future yet. You only know the previous one.

Third: the announcement. First companies launch quietly because nobody cares. Second companies launch loudly because people do, and the loudness is a debt. Announce big and every later pivot becomes a public confession; chapter seven called pivots a healthy part of a living plan, but they are much harder to make with an audience scoring you against your own press release. The correction is old and simple: sell before you announce, and let the announcement, when it comes, be a report rather than a promise.

Fourth: refusing small revenue. The first invoice of a second company has a strange psychological weight. Some part of you feels that a founder of your standing should not be excited about 400 euros. That part of you is the enemy. Small revenue is not beneath you. Small revenue is the sound of a real customer with a real problem taking you seriously, and it is exactly as informative at forty-five as it was at nineteen.

Story

I will not tell you what my current company does, but I will tell you how it started, because it started badly in precisely the way this section describes, and I wrote the section from the inside.

Within six weeks of deciding to build again, and before I had spoken to one potential customer, I had: instructed a notary to set up a holding structure suitable for a fifty-person group, two notaries actually, because the first one asked questions I found annoying, filed trademarks in three classes, opened conversations with two brand agencies, and drafted a hiring plan with eight roles in it, including, I am ashamed to type this, a Head of Operations. For a company that did not exist. Whose product did not exist. Whose customer I had not met.

What saved me was coffee with the routing company's old CFO, a woman who audits sentences the way she audits ledgers. I talked for ten minutes about the structure and the brand direction and the hiring plan. She let me finish, stirred her coffee, and asked, "Nice. What week are you in?" I asked what she meant. "Of the thirteen-week cashflow. Which week. What came in." There was, of course, no cashflow, because there was no cash coming in, because there were no customers, because the founder had been decorating the theatre instead of writing the play. I went home, cancelled the agencies, parked the trademarks, and spent the next month doing the thing I should have started with: selling something small and ugly to one real person. The first invoice was 400 euros. Roughly the butcher's 350 from when I was nineteen, adjusted for inflation. It taught me more than the holding structure ever has.

Warning

The most dangerous investor in your second company is you. Exit money makes you a lenient backer: you approve your own ideas at board level, you extend your own runway without a milestone, and the company never feels gravity. A business that cannot run out of money learns nothing from spending it. Fund your sequel like a stranger would: a written amount, a written runway, kill criteria with dates on them, and one person with permission to read them back to you in a flat voice. Chapter six's runway math does not stop applying because the investor loves you.

There is one more form of the ego trap, quieter than the others: choosing the market itself to impress someone. The founder who did unglamorous logistics software and now, mysteriously, must do something in space, or AI, or whatever this decade's dinner-party currency is. If you find yourself drawn to a market and cannot explain the pull without mentioning how it will sound, that is not a market, that is a mirror. The boring test is Sam's, from the terrace: the best second companies are frequently the ones their founders describe, with satisfaction, as boring. Boring means the founder is done performing. Customers can feel it, the way they could smell improvisation in the introduction's story about our year-four sales mess. Calm is a brand, and it cannot be faked, only earned or inherited from a previous life.
